## 1. Bronchodilators

Bronchodilators are the cornerstone of symptom management in obstructive airway diseases. They work by relaxing airway smooth muscle, thereby reducing airway resistance, improving expiratory airflow, and decreasing dynamic hyperinflation. They are categorized by their receptor target and duration of action. [1]

### 1.1 Beta-2 Adrenergic Receptor Agonists

These agents bind to the β₂-adrenergic receptor, a Gs protein-coupled receptor on airway smooth muscle, mast cells, and epithelium. Activation stimulates adenylyl cyclase to convert ATP to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). Elevated cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates myosin light-chain kinase (inhibiting it) and promotes calcium sequestration into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, causing smooth muscle relaxation and bronchodilation. They also inhibit mast cell mediator release and improve mucociliary clearance. [1]

- **Salbutamol (Albuterol):** Short-acting beta-agonist (SABA). Prototypical rescue bronchodilator.
  - *Contraindications:* Hypersensitivity. Relative caution in severe hypertension, hyperthyroidism, and tachyarrhythmias.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled: 100–200 µg via metered-dose inhaler (MDI) every 4–6 hours as needed. Nebulized: 2.5–5 mg every 4–6 hours.
  - *Important Side Effects:* Tachycardia, palpitations, fine tremor (skeletal muscle β₂ receptors), hypokalemia (intracellular shift of potassium via Na/K-ATPase stimulation), headache, and muscle cramps.
  - *Duration of Action:* Onset within 5 minutes, peak at 30 minutes, duration 4–6 hours.
- **Levosalbutamol (Levalbuterol):** The R-enantiomer of salbutamol. Theoretically fewer side effects (S-enantiomer is inactive and may be pro-inflammatory), but clinical superiority over racemic salbutamol is marginal.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled: 45–90 µg via MDI every 4–6 hours. Nebulized: 0.63–1.25 mg every 6–8 hours.
- **Terbutaline:** SABA with a similar profile, also available in subcutaneous form for severe exacerbations.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled: 500 µg via dry powder inhaler (DPI) up to 4 times daily. Subcutaneous: 0.25 mg, may repeat once after 15–30 minutes.
- **Formoterol Fumarate:** Long-acting beta-agonist (LABA). It is unique among LABAs for its rapid onset (within 1–3 minutes, comparable to salbutamol), making it suitable for both maintenance and reliever therapy (SMART/MART regimen). It is a full agonist at the β₂ receptor.
  - *Contraindications:* As for SABA. Black box warning (historical, now revised) regarding monotherapy in asthma due to increased risk of asthma-related death when used without an ICS.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 6–12 µg twice daily for maintenance. In SMART: 6 µg as needed for symptom relief with concurrent ICS.
  - *Duration of Action:* 12 hours.
- **Salmeterol Xinafoate:** LABA. A partial agonist with a unique long lipophilic side chain that anchors it to the receptor exosite, giving it prolonged duration but slow onset (30–60 minutes). It must never be used for rescue therapy.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled: 50 µg via DPI or MDI twice daily.
  - *Duration of Action:* 12 hours.
- **Indacaterol:** Ultra-LABA. A full agonist with a rapid onset (5 minutes) and 24-hour duration, allowing once-daily dosing.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 150 µg once daily (for COPD). 75 µg once daily if combined with glycopyrronium (LAMA).
  - *Side Effects:* Prominent cough in some patients immediately after inhalation.
- **Olodaterol:** Ultra-LABA. 24-hour duration of action, used only in combination with tiotropium (LAMA/LABA combination) for COPD.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (Soft Mist Inhaler): 2.5 µg once daily (combined with 2.5 µg tiotropium).
- **Vilanterol Trifenatate:** Ultra-LABA. 24-hour duration, used only in fixed-dose combinations with ICS (fluticasone furoate) or LAMA (umeclidinium), or triple therapy.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 25 µg once daily. [1,2]

### 1.2 Muscarinic Acetylcholine Receptor Antagonists

These agents block the action of acetylcholine (ACh) at the M3 muscarinic receptor on airway smooth muscle and submucosal glands. ACh, released from parasympathetic vagal nerve endings, is the dominant reversible bronchoconstrictor pathway in humans. Blocking M3 receptors causes bronchodilation and reduces mucus hypersecretion. They are the most effective bronchodilators for COPD. [1]

- **Ipratropium Bromide:** Short-acting muscarinic antagonist (SAMA).
  - *Contraindications:* Hypersensitivity to atropine alkaloids, soya lecithin, or peanut oil (in some MDI formulations).
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (MDI): 20–40 µg (2–4 puffs) 3–4 times daily. Nebulized: 500 µg every 6–8 hours.
  - *Important Side Effects:* Dry mouth, bitter taste, urinary retention in elderly men with prostatic hyperplasia, precipitation of acute angle-closure glaucoma if nebulized mist is directed at the eye.
  - *Duration of Action:* Onset 15–30 minutes, duration 4–6 hours.
- **Tiotropium Bromide:** Long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA). It exhibits kinetic receptor selectivity, dissociating rapidly from M2 autoreceptors but very slowly from M3 receptors, producing sustained bronchodilation.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI or Soft Mist Inhaler): 5 µg once daily (respimat: 2.5 µg two puffs once daily).
  - *Duration of Action:* 24 hours.
- **Umeclidinium Bromide:** LAMA. Used in combination with the ultra-LABA vilanterol for COPD.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 62.5 µg once daily.
- **Glycopyrronium Bromide:** LAMA. Rapid onset for a LAMA (5 minutes).
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 50 µg once daily.
- **Aclidinium Bromide:** LAMA. Twice-daily dosing, shorter systemic half-life due to rapid plasma esterase hydrolysis, potentially fewer systemic anticholinergic side effects.
  - *Dosage:* Inhaled (DPI): 400 µg twice daily. [1,2]

### 1.3 Methylxanthines

- **Theophylline:** A non-selective phosphodiesterase (PDE) inhibitor, preventing the breakdown of cAMP and cGMP, thereby augmenting bronchodilation. At low therapeutic concentrations, the primary mechanism is likely adenosine receptor antagonism (A1 and A2 subtypes), blocking adenosine-induced bronchoconstriction and histamine release. It also improves diaphragmatic contractility and has weak anti-inflammatory effects (increasing histone deacetylase activity).
  - *Contraindications:* Uncontrolled arrhythmias, epilepsy (lowers seizure threshold), porphyria.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: Initial 200–300 mg twice daily, titrated to a target serum concentration of 10–15 mg/L (now 5–15 mg/L). Requires therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). The dose must be reduced in liver failure, heart failure, and with interacting drugs (macrolides, fluoroquinolones, cimetidine, fluvoxamine).
  - *Important Side Effects:* Narrow therapeutic index. Toxicity: nausea, vomiting, severe headache, supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (can occur without warning, high mortality). Hypokalemia and hyperglycemia are acute markers of toxicity.
  - *Duration:* Chronic, long-term oral therapy.
- **Aminophylline:** Theophylline complexed with ethylenediamine to enhance water solubility for intravenous administration.
  - *Dosage:* Intravenous: Loading dose of 5 mg/kg over 20 minutes (if no theophylline use in the past 24 hours), followed by a continuous infusion of 0.5–0.7 mg/kg/hour. TDM is mandatory. [1,2]

## 2. Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS) and Combination Inhalers

ICS are the most effective anti-inflammatory therapy for persistent asthma. They bind to the intracellular glucocorticoid receptor (GR), which translocates to the nucleus and transrepresses pro-inflammatory genes (NF-κB, AP-1), reducing the synthesis of cytokines, chemokines, inflammatory enzymes, and adhesion molecules. This reduces eosinophilic and lymphocytic airway inflammation, decreases bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and reduces mucus secretion. [1]

### 2.1 Inhaled Corticosteroid Monotherapy

- **Beclomethasone Dipropionate:** Prodrug activated to beclomethasone-17-monopropionate.
  - *Dosage:* MDI/DPI: 100–400 µg twice daily (dose range 200–2000 µg/day).
- **Budesonide:** High first-pass hepatic metabolism, minimizing systemic bioavailability.
  - *Dosage:* DPI/Nebulized suspension: 200–800 µg twice daily.
  - *Key Feature:* The only ICS with strong FDA pregnancy category B data, making it the ICS of choice in pregnancy.
- **Fluticasone Propionate:** Highly potent, very low oral bioavailability.
  - *Dosage:* MDI/DPI: 100–500 µg twice daily.
- **Fluticasone Furoate:** Ultra-long duration of action, once-daily dosing, and high receptor affinity.
  - *Dosage:* DPI (Ellipta): 100–200 µg once daily.
- **Ciclesonide:** Prodrug activated by esterases in the lung. Low oral deposition and high protein binding reduce oropharyngeal and systemic side effects.
  - *Dosage:* MDI: 80–320 µg twice daily.
- **Mometasone Furoate:** High affinity for the glucocorticoid receptor, minimal systemic absorption.
  - *Dosage:* DPI/MDI: 200–400 µg once or twice daily.
- *Common Side Effects of ICS:* Oropharyngeal candidiasis (thrush), dysphonia (hoarseness from vocal cord myopathy), and cough. These are mitigated by using a spacer and rinsing the mouth with water after use. Systemic effects (adrenal suppression, reduced bone mineral density, growth suppression in children) are uncommon at standard doses but possible at high doses. [1,2]

### 2.2 ICS/LABA Fixed-Dose Combinations

Combining an ICS and a LABA in a single inhaler is the foundation of asthma and COPD maintenance therapy. The LABA provides bronchodilation and enhances ICS signaling (synergy). Crucially, in asthma, LABA must never be given without an ICS. [1]

- Fluticasone propionate / Salmeterol (Advair, Seretide)
- Budesonide / Formoterol (Symbicort) – can be used for SMART therapy.
- Fluticasone furoate / Vilanterol (Breo Ellipta) – once-daily dosing.
- Mometasone furoate / Formoterol (Dulera, Zenhale)
- Beclomethasone / Formoterol (Foster, Fostair) – can be used for MART therapy.

### 2.3 ICS/LAMA/LABA Triple Fixed-Dose Combinations

For severe COPD with frequent exacerbations despite dual bronchodilator or ICS/LABA therapy. [1]

- Fluticasone furoate / Umeclidinium / Vilanterol (Trelegy Ellipta). Once-daily.
- Budesonide / Glycopyrronium / Formoterol (Breztri Aerosphere). Twice-daily (MDI).
- Beclomethasone / Glycopyrronium / Formoterol (Trimbow). Twice-daily (MDI).

## 3. Systemic Corticosteroids

Used for acute, severe exacerbations of asthma and COPD, severe eosinophilic pneumonia, and interstitial lung diseases. They have rapid and profound anti-inflammatory effects but a devastating side effect profile with chronic use. [2]

### 3.1 Oral Corticosteroids

- **Prednisolone:** The standard oral corticosteroid. Bioactive form, bypassing hepatic conversion.
  - *Contraindications:* Systemic fungal infections, uncontrolled severe sepsis. Relative: Diabetes, osteoporosis, psychosis.
  - *Dosage for Acute Exacerbation:* 40–50 mg daily for 5–7 days. For chronic use in severe asthma or ILD, the lowest possible dose is used (e.g., 5–10 mg daily). Tapering is required if used for >2–3 weeks to prevent an adrenal crisis.
  - *Side Effects (Chronic Use):* Cushingoid features (weight gain, moon face, buffalo hump), osteoporosis, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, peptic ulcer disease, cataracts, glaucoma, skin thinning, bruising, myopathy, psychosis, immunosuppression.

### 3.2 Intravenous Corticosteroids

- **Hydrocortisone:** For acute severe asthma or anaphylaxis. Has significant mineralocorticoid activity.
  - *Dosage:* 200 mg IV stat, then 100 mg IV every 6 hours.
- **Methylprednisolone:** More potent glucocorticoid with less mineralocorticoid effect than hydrocortisone. Preferred IV steroid for high-dose pulse therapy (e.g., acute interstitial pneumonitis, lupus pneumonitis).
  - *Dosage:* 125 mg IV every 6 hours, or 500–1000 mg IV daily for 3 days (pulse therapy). [1,2]

## 4. Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists and Synthesis Inhibitors

These oral agents target the leukotriene pathway, which is involved in both the allergic inflammatory cascade and sustained bronchoconstriction. [1]

- **Montelukast:** A selective, competitive antagonist of the CysLT1 receptor for cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), which are released from mast cells and eosinophils. This inhibits the potent bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, mucus secretion, and eosinophil recruitment.
  - *Contraindications:* Hypersensitivity.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: 10 mg once daily in the evening for adults.
  - *Important Side Effects:* Headache, gastrointestinal upset. Black box warning for serious neuropsychiatric events (agitation, aggression, hallucinations, depression, suicidal ideation). Families must be counseled on this.
  - *Key Indication:* Asthma, especially in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. Not a substitute for ICS.
- **Zafirlukast:** Similar mechanism, but is an inhibitor of CYP450 enzymes, leading to more drug interactions (warfarin, theophylline). Requires dosing on an empty stomach. Rarely used now due to hepatotoxicity reports.
- **Zileuton:** A 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor that blocks the synthesis of all leukotrienes (LTB4, the cysteinyl LTs). It is a second-line agent due to the need for twice-daily dosing, drug interactions (CYP1A2), and a significant risk of hepatotoxicity, requiring monthly liver function test monitoring for the first year of therapy. [1]

## 5. Monoclonal Antibodies (Biologics) for Severe Asthma

These targeted therapies have revolutionized the management of severe, uncontrolled, type 2-high asthma. They are prescribed by specialists based on asthma phenotype (allergic, eosinophilic, or both) and biomarkers (blood eosinophils, IgE, FeNO). [1,3]

### 5.1 Anti-IgE Antibody

- **Omalizumab:** A recombinant humanized monoclonal IgG1 antibody that binds to the Fc portion of free IgE, preventing it from binding to high-affinity FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. This inhibits mast cell degranulation and the allergic cascade.
  - *Indication:* Moderate-to-severe persistent allergic asthma (with a documented skin prick test or specific IgE to a perennial aeroallergen), chronic idiopathic urticaria.
  - *Dosage:* Subcutaneous injection every 2–4 weeks. Dosing is based on a table using the patient's total serum IgE level (pre-treatment) and body weight (kg). The IgE range for dosing is 30–700 IU/mL for adults. The pre-treatment IgE must be measured because omalizumab forms complexes with IgE, rendering the total IgE level uninterpretable for dosing once treatment has begun.
  - *Side Effects:* Injection site reaction, anaphylaxis (risk ~0.1–0.2%, requires in-clinic observation after the first three injections). Slightly increased risk of cardiovascular events observed in some analyses.

### 5.2 Anti-IL-5 and Anti-IL-5Rα Antibodies

IL-5 is the critical cytokine for eosinophil maturation, differentiation, survival, and activation. [1]

- **Mepolizumab:** Humanized monoclonal anti-IL-5 antibody. Binds and neutralizes IL-5.
  - *Indication:* Severe eosinophilic asthma (blood eosinophils ≥300 cells/µL, historically ≥150 cells/µL).
  - *Dosage:* Subcutaneous injection, 100 mg every 4 weeks.
  - *Side Effects:* Headache, injection site reaction.
- **Reslizumab:** Humanized monoclonal anti-IL-5 antibody. Unlike mepolizumab, it is administered intravenously, with a weight-based dose (3 mg/kg). Used in severe eosinophilic asthma.
- **Benralizumab:** A humanized afucosylated monoclonal antibody directed against the IL-5 receptor alpha subunit (IL-5Rα). By binding the receptor, it blocks IL-5 signaling. Crucially, its afucosylated Fc domain enhances its affinity for the FcγRIIIa receptor on natural killer (NK) cells, inducing robust antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), leading to near-complete and rapid apoptosis (depletion) of eosinophils and basophils.
  - *Dosage:* Subcutaneous injection, 30 mg every 4 weeks for the first 3 doses, then every 8 weeks.
  - *Distinguishing Feature:* The direct cell-depletion mechanism and the 8-week maintenance dosing interval.

### 5.3 Anti-IL-4Rα Antibody

- **Dupilumab:** A fully human monoclonal antibody that binds to the IL-4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα) subunit, which is shared by both IL-4 and IL-13 receptor complexes. This dual blockade inhibits the signaling of both IL-4 and IL-13, the central drivers of type 2 inflammation.
  - *Indication:* Moderate-to-severe eosinophilic or oral corticosteroid-dependent asthma, moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis.
  - *Dosage:* Subcutaneous injection, an initial loading dose of 400–600 mg, followed by 200–300 mg every 2 weeks.
  - *Side Effects:* Injection site reaction, transient eosinophilia (due to inhibition of eosinophil migration into tissues), conjunctivitis.

### 5.4 Anti-TSLP Antibody

- **Tezepelumab:** A first-in-class human monoclonal antibody that targets thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), an epithelial-derived cytokine (alarmin) positioned at the very top of the inflammatory cascade. By blocking TSLP, it inhibits a broad range of downstream inflammatory pathways (both type 2 and non-type 2).
  - *Indication:* Severe asthma without phenotypic or biomarker restrictions. It has shown efficacy even in patients with low blood eosinophils and low FeNO.
  - *Dosage:* Subcutaneous injection, 210 mg every 4 weeks. [1,3]

## 6. Mucolytics, Antioxidants, and Airway Hydration

These agents alter the viscoelastic properties of mucus to facilitate expectoration and clearance. [1]

- **N-Acetylcysteine (NAC):** The free sulfhydryl group in NAC reduces the disulfide bonds in the glycoprotein polymers of mucus, depolymerizing and liquefying thick, purulent sputum. It also functions as an antioxidant by serving as a precursor to glutathione (GSH), the body's master intracellular antioxidant, and directly scavenges reactive oxygen species. In COPD, chronic oral NAC (1200 mg/day) has been shown to modestly reduce the frequency of exacerbations in patients not on ICS. It is the antidote for acetaminophen (paracetamol) overdose.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: 600 mg once or twice daily. Effervescent tablets. Nebulized: 20% solution, 3–5 mL, every 6–8 hours.
- **Dornase Alfa (Pulmozyme):** A recombinant human deoxyribonuclease I (rhDNase) enzyme. It selectively cleaves the long chains of extracellular DNA that accumulate in the airway secretions of cystic fibrosis (CF) patients. DNA is the primary contributor to the extreme viscosity of CF sputum, originating from the breakdown of massive numbers of recruited neutrophils. By depolymerizing DNA, dornase alfa dramatically reduces sputum viscosity and adhesivity. It is indicated only for cystic fibrosis.
  - *Dosage:* 2.5 mg via jet nebulizer once daily.
- **Hypertonic Saline (7% NaCl):** Inhaled hypertonic saline creates an osmotic gradient that draws water into the airway lumen (hydrating mucus) and disrupts the ionic bonds within the mucus gel. It is a cornerstone of mucus clearance in cystic fibrosis and non-CF bronchiectasis.
  - *Dosage:* 4 mL via nebulizer twice daily. Can cause initial bronchospasm, so pre-treatment with a bronchodilator is mandatory.
- **Mannitol Inhalation Powder (Bronchitol):** An osmotic agent similar in mechanism to hypertonic saline. It is a dry powder inhaler used to improve mucus clearance in cystic fibrosis. A formal bronchial challenge test with mannitol is performed first to rule out the risk of severe bronchospasm. [1]

## 7. Pulmonary Vasodilators for Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (PAH)

PAH is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, an imbalance between vasoconstricting/proliferative mediators (endothelin) and vasodilating/antiproliferative mediators (prostacyclin, nitric oxide), leading to increased pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular failure, and death. These drugs target three major pathways. [4]

### 7.1 Prostacyclin Pathway Agents

Prostacyclin (PGI₂) is a potent vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation and smooth muscle cell proliferation. Its synthesis is downregulated in PAH.

- **Epoprostenol (Flolan, Veletri):** Synthetic prostacyclin. The gold standard for high-risk PAH patients.
  - *Administration:* Continuous intravenous infusion via a central venous catheter with a portable infusion pump. The half-life is 3–5 minutes, so any interruption of the infusion can cause lethal rebound pulmonary hypertension. The drug must be reconstituted daily and kept cold. Dose is titrated gradually based on symptoms and side effects (jaw pain, flushing, diarrhea).
- **Treprostinil (Remodulin, Tyvaso, Orenitram):** A prostacyclin analogue stable at room temperature with a longer half-life (4 hours). It is available in intravenous, subcutaneous (very painful injection site reaction), inhaled, and oral formulations, offering a major therapeutic advantage.
- **Iloprost (Ventavis):** Inhaled prostacyclin analogue. Delivered 6–9 times daily via a dedicated nebulizer system. Short duration minimizes systemic hypotension but requires extreme patient adherence.
- **Selexipag (Uptravi):** A selective, non-prostanoid prostacyclin receptor (IP receptor) agonist. It is an oral agent metabolized into a potent, long-acting active metabolite. It is used in WHO Group 1 PAH.
  - *Dosage:* Orally, titrated from 200 µg twice daily to a maximum of 1600 µg twice daily. Side effects are typical of prostacyclin class (headache, nausea, jaw pain). [4]

### 7.2 Endothelin Receptor Antagonists (ERAs)

Endothelin-1 is a powerful vasoconstrictor and smooth muscle mitogen that binds to ETA and ETB receptors on pulmonary vascular smooth muscle. ERAs block this pathway.

- **Bosentan (Tracleer):** Dual ERA (blocks both ETA and ETB). It is a substrate and inducer of CYP3A4, causing significant drug interactions (renders hormonal contraceptives ineffective, interacts with sildenafil). A major, dose-limiting toxicity is reversible hepatic transaminase elevation, requiring mandatory monthly liver function tests (LFTs).
- **Ambrisentan (Letairis):** Selective ETA receptor antagonist. It has a lower risk of hepatotoxicity than bosentan, and no monthly LFT monitoring is required by the FDA. It causes dose-dependent peripheral edema.
- **Macitentan (Opsumit):** A dual ERA designed for sustained receptor binding and tissue penetration. It is a CYP3A4 substrate but a weaker inducer than bosentan. It has a lower hepatotoxicity profile and is given once daily. All ERAs are highly teratogenic and are only available through restricted distribution programs with mandatory pregnancy testing (ERA REMS). [4]

### 7.3 Phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) Inhibitors

These agents augment the nitric oxide (NO) pathway. NO activates soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), producing cGMP, a potent vasodilator. PDE-5 is highly expressed in the pulmonary vasculature and degrades cGMP. Inhibition increases and prolongs cGMP signaling, causing pulmonary vasodilation.

### Sildenafil (Revatio):

  - *Dosage for PAH:* Oral: 20 mg three times daily. (This is the PAH dose, distinct from the on-demand erectile dysfunction dose of Viagra).
  - *Side Effects:* Headache, flushing, nasal congestion, epistaxis. Contraindicated with nitrates (life-threatening hypotension).

### Tadalafil (Adcirca):

  - *Dosage for PAH:* Oral: 40 mg once daily. The long half-life allows for once-daily dosing. [4]

### 7.4 Soluble Guanylate Cyclase (sGC) Stimulators

This novel class stimulates sGC directly, independently of NO, and also sensitizes sGC to low endogenous NO levels.

- **Riociguat (Adempas):** Indicated for PAH and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).
  - *Mechanism:* It stabilizes the NO-sGC binding, increasing cGMP production. Because it works through the same downstream pathway, combination with PDE-5 inhibitors is absolutely contraindicated due to the risk of catastrophic systemic hypotension.
  - *Dosage:* Titrated from 1 mg three times daily, based on blood pressure, up to a maximum of 2.5 mg three times daily. Contraindicated in pregnancy, and must not be combined with PDE-5 inhibitors or nitrates. [4]

## 8. Antifibrotic and Remodeling Agents for Interstitial Lung Disease

These drugs slow the inexorable progression of fibrosing interstitial lung diseases, particularly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). [1]

- **Nintedanib (Ofev):** A small-molecule intracellular inhibitor of multiple tyrosine kinases, including the receptors for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFR), fibroblast growth factor (FGFR), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGFR). By blocking these pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, it slows fibroblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation into myofibroblasts, thereby reducing the deposition of extracellular matrix. It slows the decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) by approximately 50% in IPF.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: 150 mg twice daily with food.
  - *Side Effects:* Diarrhea is extremely common (>60%), requiring active management with loperamide. Nausea, vomiting, and hepatotoxicity (LFT elevation). It is a VEGFR inhibitor, so there is a theoretical risk of bleeding and thrombosis. It is a P-glycoprotein substrate.
- **Pirfenidone (Esbriet):** A pyridone compound whose exact mechanism is unknown but is believed to exert anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects by inhibiting the synthesis of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), a master pro-fibrotic cytokine, and inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. Like nintedanib, it slows FVC decline.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: Titrated over 15 days to a maintenance dose of 801 mg three times daily with food (total 2403 mg/day).
  - *Side Effects:* Profound photosensitivity (sunburn with minimal sun exposure; strict sun protection is mandatory), nausea, anorexia, fatigue, and hepatotoxicity. [1]

## 9. Antimicrobial Agents for Respiratory Infections

The lung is directly exposed to the environment, making respiratory tract infections the most common affliction of the system. Pharmacotherapy is targeted against specific pathogens.

### 9.1 Antibiotics for Common Community-Acquired and Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia

Selection is based on pathogen likelihood, local antibiograms, and patient severity. [5]

- **Aminopenicillins (Amoxicillin):** First-line for mild CAP. Bactericidal by binding penicillin-binding proteins, inhibiting cell wall synthesis.
- **Aminopenicillin + Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor (Amoxicillin-Clavulanate):** Covers β-lactamase-producing H. influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis.
- **Macrolides (Azithromycin, Clarithromycin):** Bacteriostatic, inhibit 50S ribosomal subunit protein synthesis. Cover atypical bacteria (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Legionella). Azithromycin has a long tissue half-life (3-day course covers 10 days). Major side effects: QTc prolongation, potential for sudden cardiac death.
- **Respiratory Fluoroquinolones (Levofloxacin, Moxifloxacin):** Bactericidal, inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. Excellent coverage against respiratory Gram-positives (S. pneumoniae), Gram-negatives, and all atypicals. Contraindicated in pregnancy and children. Black box warnings for tendinopathy/tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, aortic dissection, and CNS effects. Used as monotherapy for severe CAP.
- **Cephalosporins (Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime):** Third-generation intravenous cephalosporins. Hospital standard for severe CAP, combined with a macrolide. Excellent Gram-negative and reasonable Gram-positive activity.
- **Anti-MRSA Agents (Vancomycin, Linezolid):** Added to a CAP regimen if risk factors for MRSA exist (recent hospitalization, prior IV antibiotics).
- **Anti-Pseudomonal Agents (Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Cefepime, Meropenem, Ceftazidime-Avibactam):** Used for hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) or ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) where Pseudomonas aeruginosa risk is high. Double-coverage with an aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone (Tobramycin, Ciprofloxacin) may be employed in severe, resistant cases. [5]

### 9.2 Antituberculosis Drugs

First-line, standard short-course therapy for drug-susceptible TB is a 2-month intensive phase with 4 drugs (RHZE), followed by a 4-month continuation phase with 2 drugs (RH). Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) is the standard. [6]

- **Isoniazid (INH, H):** Prodrug activated by KatG enzyme in M. tuberculosis. Inhibits mycolic acid synthesis, a critical component of the mycobacterial cell wall. It is bactericidal against actively dividing organisms.
  - *Side Effects:* Hepatotoxicity (age and alcohol-related), peripheral neuropathy (INH depletes pyridoxine/vitamin B6; supplementation with 25–50 mg/day is mandatory to prevent this), drug-induced lupus.
- **Rifampicin (RIF, R):** Bactericidal. Binds to the β-subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, inhibiting RNA transcription. It is a potent inducer of the cytochrome P450 enzyme system (CYP3A4, 2C9, 2C19, and others), causing numerous, severe drug interactions (renders hormonal contraceptives, warfarin, and many antiretrovirals ineffective).
  - *Side Effects:* Hepatotoxicity, orange-red discoloration of bodily fluids (urine, tears, sweat—this is universal and harmless), flu-like syndrome with intermittent dosing.
- **Pyrazinamide (PZA, Z):** Requires an acidic pH for activity, making it effective against intracellular bacilli in the caseous granuloma. The mechanism involves inhibition of fatty acid synthase I. It is the cornerstone for shortening TB therapy from 9 to 6 months because it uniquely sterilizes semidormant organisms. Major side effect is hyperuricemia, which can precipitate gout, and hepatotoxicity.
- **Ethambutol (EMB, E):** Bacteriostatic. Inhibits arabinosyl transferase, blocking the polymerization of arabinogalactan, a key component of the mycobacterial cell wall. Its single most feared, dose-dependent side effect is retrobulbar (optic) neuritis, causing a loss of visual acuity and the inability to perceive the color red (red-green color blindness). Patients must be instructed to report any visual change immediately and have monthly visual acuity and color vision testing. It is used in the intensive phase while awaiting drug susceptibility testing to ensure no resistance to INH or RIF. [6]

### 9.3 Antifungals

Invasive pulmonary fungal infections (aspergillosis, mucormycosis) require aggressive therapy. [5]

- **Voriconazole:** First-line for invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Trough drug level monitoring is essential for efficacy and to avoid neurotoxicity. It causes visual disturbances, photosensitivity, and periostitis.
- **Isavuconazole:** Newer broad-spectrum azole with a better safety profile and fewer CYP450 drug interactions than voriconazole.
- **Liposomal Amphotericin B:** The drug of choice for mucormycosis. Nephrotoxicity and electrolyte wasting (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia) mandate aggressive monitoring.
- **Echinocandins (Caspofungin, Micafungin):** Inhibit β-glucan synthase in the fungal cell wall. They are used for salvage therapy in aspergillosis and are first-line for invasive candidiasis. They lack activity against Cryptococcus and Mucor. [5]

### 9.4 Antivirals for Respiratory Viruses

- **Oseltamivir:** Neuraminidase inhibitor for influenza A and B. It must be administered within 48 hours of symptom onset to be clinically effective. It reduces symptom duration by about one day and decreases the risk of lower respiratory tract complications in at-risk populations.
  - *Dosage:* Oral: 75 mg twice daily for 5 days (treatment).
- **Remdesivir:** A nucleotide prodrug that inhibits the SARS-CoV-2 RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, causing chain termination. It has efficacy in hospitalized patients requiring supplemental oxygen but not yet on mechanical ventilation.
- **Nirmatrelvir/Ritonavir (Paxlovid):** For non-hospitalized high-risk patients with COVID-19. Nirmatrelvir is a SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) inhibitor. Ritonavir is a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor included solely as a "booster" to increase nirmatrelvir concentrations. This leads to a vast number of drug interactions that must be rigorously screened.
- **Ribavirin:** Inhaled formulation for severe Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) bronchiolitis in high-risk infants and immunocompromised patients. Oral form used with interferon for hepatitis C. Teratogenic; strict precautions for pregnant healthcare workers. [5]

## 10. Agents for Alpha-1 Antitrypsin (AAT) Deficiency

AAT deficiency is a genetic disorder leading to low levels of this protective antiprotease, resulting in early-onset panacinar emphysema. Augmentation therapy is human plasma-derived AAT. [1]

### Human Alpha-1 Proteinase Inhibitor (Prolastin-C, Zemaira, Aralast NP):

  - *Mechanism of Action:* Weekly intravenous infusions of purified, pooled human plasma AAT restore serum and lung epithelial lining fluid levels to above the protective threshold of 11 µM (or 57 mg/dL, typically aiming for 60–80 mg/dL). This inhibits neutrophil elastase, preventing the ongoing proteolytic destruction of the lung matrix.
  - *Indication:* Only for individuals with confirmed severe AAT deficiency (genotype PiZZ, PiZNull, or other severe variants) with established airflow obstruction (FEV₁ 30–65% predicted). Evidence of efficacy is strongest in slowing the decline of lung density on CT scan, with a strong trend toward mortality benefit.
  - *Dosage:* 60 mg/kg body weight, administered intravenously once weekly.
  - *Administration:* Must be administered in a healthcare setting or by home infusion service. Potential side effects include infusion reactions (fever, chills, dyspnea) and the theoretical risk of transmission of infectious agents, though the product undergoes rigorous viral inactivation. [1]

## 11. Immunomodulators and Immunosuppressants for Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD)

For non-IPF, inflammatory-driven ILDs (e.g., connective tissue disease-associated ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, sarcoidosis), immunosuppression is the mainstay. [1]

- **Mycophenolate Mofetil (CellCept, Myfortic):** Prodrug of mycophenolic acid, a non-competitive inhibitor of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH). This blocks the de novo synthesis of guanosine nucleotides, a pathway on which T and B lymphocytes are critically dependent (other cells can use a salvage pathway). It acts as a selective inhibitor of lymphocyte proliferation.
  - *Use:* First-line steroid-sparing agent for connective tissue disease-associated ILD, especially systemic sclerosis-ILD, and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis.
  - *Side Effects:* Diarrhea (extremely common, limiting), nausea, bone marrow suppression (leukopenia, anemia), teratogenicity (mandatory negative pregnancy test and contraceptive use).
- **Azathioprine:** Purine analogue (6-mercaptopurine prodrug) that inhibits purine synthesis, resulting in inhibition of DNA and RNA synthesis and thus lymphocyte proliferation. It must never be combined with allopurinol (can be fatal). It requires pre-testing for the enzyme thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) to guide dosing and avoid severe myelotoxicity. It is a historical agent, largely replaced by mycophenolate due to lower toxicity. **Crucially, it is contraindicated in IPF; the PANTHER-IPF trial demonstrated a significant increase in mortality and hospitalization with a combination of azathioprine, prednisone, and N-acetylcysteine.**
- **Cyclophosphamide:** An alkylating agent that cross-links DNA, causing cell death. It is a powerful, broad-spectrum immunosuppressant reserved for severe, rapidly progressive ILD, such as acute exacerbation of systemic sclerosis-ILD with a UIP pattern or severe lupus pneumonitis. A major toxicity is hemorrhagic cystitis due to the toxic metabolite acrolein; aggressive hydration and co-administration of mesna are required. It carries a high long-term risk of malignancy and infertility.
- **Rituximab:** Chimeric monoclonal anti-CD20 antibody causing B-cell depletion via ADCC, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and apoptosis. Used for ILD related to rheumatoid arthritis, ANCA-associated vasculitis, and refractory sarcoidosis or myositis-associated ILD. Risk of infusion reactions and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML, fatal brain infection).
- **Tocilizumab:** Humanized monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor. It is a key agent for giant cell arteritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and severe systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis. It is finding a role in rapidly progressive systemic sclerosis-ILD with a high inflammatory burden. It normalizes CRP rapidly, which can mask infection. [1]

## 12.1 Pleural Sclerosants

Used to achieve pleurodesis (obliteration of the pleural space) in recurrent malignant pleural effusion or recurrent pneumothorax. They cause intense inflammation of the visceral and parietal pleura, leading to fibrosis and adherence. [1]

- **Sterile Graded Talc:** The most effective sclerosant. It is administered either as an aerosolized powder during medical thoracoscopy (talc poudrage) or as a slurry via an intercostal chest drain. Talc activates pleural mesothelial cells to release IL-8 and other chemokines, inducing a massive neutrophil-driven inflammatory response followed by fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition.
  - *Major Risk:* Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), related to particle size and systemic distribution, though with modern graded, large-particle talc, this risk is significantly reduced.
- **Doxycycline:** A tetracycline antibiotic used as a chemical sclerosant when talc is not available. Less effective but easier to administer.

### 12.2 Fibrinolytics for Pleural Infection

For complicated parapneumonic effusion and empyema, where thick fibrinous loculations prevent chest tube drainage of infected material, fibrinolytic therapy can be injected into the pleural space to break down the septations, averting the need for surgery (VATS). [1]

- **Tissue Plasminogen Activator (tPA, Alteplase):** A serine protease that converts plasminogen to plasmin, the key enzyme in fibrinolysis and clot breakdown.
- **Dornase Alfa (Pulmozyme):** As described above, reduces the viscosity of pus by depolymerizing free DNA.
- *Standard Regimen (MIST2 Trial Protocol):* A combination of tPA 10 mg and DNase 5 mg injected intrapleurally twice daily for 3 days is synergistic and significantly more effective than either agent alone, reducing surgical referral and hospital stay.

## 13. Smoking Cessation Pharmacotherapy

The single most impactful intervention in respiratory medicine. [1]

- **Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT):** Patch (slow onset, steady state), gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray. Provides nicotine without the carcinogens of tobacco smoke, alleviating withdrawal symptoms.
- **Varenicline (Chantix, Champix):** A partial agonist at the α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor in the brain's mesolimbic dopamine system. It relieves craving (agonist effect) and reduces the reward of smoking (antagonist effect, as it blocks the binding of nicotine from a cigarette).
  - *Dosage:* Titrated oral dose, 0.5 mg daily to 1 mg twice daily for 12 weeks.
  - *Warning:* The black box warning for neuropsychiatric events was removed after the large EAGLES trial. Nausea is the most common side effect.
- **Bupropion (Zyban):** An atypical antidepressant that inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine. It reduces craving and withdrawal symptoms, independent of its antidepressant effect.

## 14. Vaccines for Respiratory Disease Prevention

Vaccination is the bedrock of respiratory public health. [7]

- **Influenza Vaccine:** Inactivated (IM) or live-attenuated (intranasal). Annual seasonal vaccination is recommended for all individuals >6 months. It reduces the risk of severe lower respiratory tract infection, hospitalization, and mortality, particularly in the elderly and immunocompromised.
- **Pneumococcal Vaccines:** Two types: (1) Polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23), covers 23 serotypes, but it induces a T-independent B-cell response, generating poor, waning immunity with no memory. (2) Conjugate vaccines (PCV13, PCV15, PCV20), link the polysaccharide to a protein carrier (CRM197), inducing a potent T-dependent response with immunologic memory. Current ACIP guidelines for adults recommend a single dose of PCV20, or PCV15 followed by PPSV23, for all adults aged 65 years or older and younger adults with chronic diseases.
- **RSV Vaccines (Arexvy, Abrysvo):** Newly approved recombinant subunit vaccines for adults aged 60 years and older to prevent severe RSV lower respiratory tract disease. They contain the RSV prefusion F protein, the target of neutralizing antibodies.
- **SARS-CoV-2 Vaccines:** mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2, mRNA-1273) and adjuvanted protein subunit vaccine. The mRNA vaccines deliver lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA encoding the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which is translated by host ribosomes, presented on MHC-I, and drives a robust antibody and T-cell response.
- **BCG Vaccine:** Live attenuated strain of *Mycobacterium bovis*. Used in endemic countries to prevent severe childhood disseminated TB (miliary TB, TB meningitis). Its efficacy in preventing pulmonary TB in adults is highly variable and low. [6]

## 15. Other Agents

- **Oxygen:** A drug, not a benign environmental gas. It must be prescribed with a target saturation range (e.g., 88–92% for COPD patients at risk of type 2 respiratory failure due to loss of hypoxic drive and increased V/Q mismatch; 94–98% for others). In chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure (PaO₂ ≤55 mmHg or SaO₂ ≤88% on room air), Long-Term Oxygen Therapy (LTOT) for >15 hours per day is the only pharmacotherapy proven to reduce mortality in COPD. In acute settings, the risk of oxygen toxicity (absorption atelectasis, reactive oxygen species injury) and CO₂ retention from uncontrolled high-flow oxygen must be respected. [1]
- **Heliox:** A mixture of helium (70–80%) and oxygen (20–30%). Its density is significantly lower than that of nitrogen-oxygen mixtures (air). By reducing the density of the inspired gas, it converts turbulent airflow in a narrowed upper airway into more efficient laminar flow, dramatically reducing the work of breathing. It is a temporizing measure for severe upper airway obstruction (stridor, post-extubation stridor, tracheal tumors) and severe refractory asthma, buying time for definitive therapy (steroids, adrenaline, intubation) to work. It is not a definitive treatment. [1]
- **Racemic Epinephrine:** A mixture of the D- and L- isomers of epinephrine. It acts as a non-selective alpha- and beta-adrenergic agonist. When administered via nebulizer, its alpha-1-mediated vasoconstrictor effect on the edematous mucosa of the upper airway and larynx is its primary clinical utility. It reduces airway wall edema and capillary leak in post-extubation stridor, severe viral croup, and angioedema. The clinical effect is transient (1–2 hours), so patients must be observed for rebound edema. The beta-agonist effect causes tachycardia but is not the primary clinical goal in this setting. [2]
