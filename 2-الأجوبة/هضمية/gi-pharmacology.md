## 1. Acid-Suppressive Agents

These agents suppress gastric acid secretion through distinct mechanisms. They are the cornerstone of treatment for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), and the prevention of stress-related mucosal disease, and are integral to H. pylori eradication regimens. [1,2]

### 1.1 Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

PPIs are the most potent inhibitors of gastric acid secretion. They are substituted benzimidazole prodrugs. They are weak bases that are absorbed into the systemic circulation, then selectively concentrated and activated in the acidic environment (pH < 4) of the secretory canaliculi of the gastric parietal cell. Here, they undergo acid-catalyzed conversion to a reactive thiophilic sulfenamide. This sulfenamide forms a covalent, irreversible disulfide bond with the cysteine residues (specifically Cys813) on the alpha subunit of the H+/K+-ATPase (the proton pump), the final common pathway of gastric acid secretion. This permanently inactivates the pump. Restoration of acid secretion requires the parietal cell to synthesize new H+/K+-ATPase molecules, a process that takes 24-48 hours. Because they irreversibly inhibit both basal and stimulated acid secretion, and because not all pumps are active at any given time (and thus not all are accessible to the drug during a single dosing interval), maximal acid suppression requires 3-5 days of continuous dosing. PPIs should be administered 30-60 minutes before the first meal of the day, because the pumps are inserted into the canalicular membrane and activated only in the presence of food-induced histamine/gastrin stimulation; taking a PPI with a meal renders it less effective. PPIs are metabolized by hepatic CYP450, predominantly CYP2C19, with some contribution from CYP3A4. Genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C19 (rapid vs. poor metabolizer phenotypes) and drug interactions with other CYP2C19 substrates (e.g., clopidogrel) are clinically significant. [1,3]

### Names:

- Omeprazole
- Esomeprazole (the S-isomer of omeprazole, with less first-pass metabolism and potentially greater acid suppression)
- Lansoprazole
- Dexlansoprazole (the R-isomer of lansoprazole, with a novel dual delayed-release formulation providing two peaks of drug concentration)
- Pantoprazole (the least CYP2C19-dependent, and thus associated with fewer drug interactions; it is more stable at neutral pH, so it is the only PPI that can be formulated for intravenous use without a bicarbonate buffer)
- Rabeprazole

### Indications:

- GERD (erosive esophagitis, symptomatic non-erosive reflux disease, maintenance)
- Peptic ulcer disease (gastric and duodenal ulcer healing)
- Helicobacter pylori eradication (as part of triple or quadruple therapy; the PPI raises the gastric pH to >6.0, which promotes H. pylori replication and increases the susceptibility of the organism to amoxicillin and clarithromycin, and stabilizes these acid-labile antibiotics)
- Prevention and treatment of NSAID-induced gastropathy and peptic ulcer
- Pathological hypersecretory conditions (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, systemic mastocytosis)
- Prevention of stress-related mucosal bleeding in critically ill patients
- Eosinophilic esophagitis
- Barrett's esophagus (for symptom control, though chemopreventive effect is controversial)

### Contraindications:

- Hypersensitivity to any PPI
- Relative: Known severe hepatic impairment (dose reduction required)

### Important Adverse Effects:

- *Mechanism-based*: Hypergastrinemia. The profound, prolonged achlorhydria abolishes the normal acid-mediated negative feedback on the antral G cells (via somatostatin from D cells). Uninhibited G cells secrete excessive gastrin, causing trophic stimulation of gastric enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells. In humans, this causes ECL cell hyperplasia, which is benign and reversible; the risk of carcinoid tumor (as seen in rats) is negligible in humans with standard doses.
- *Enteric infections*: Gastric acid is a critical innate immune barrier. PPI-induced hypochlorhydria increases the risk of enteric infections: Clostridioides difficile colitis (2-3 fold increased risk), community-acquired pneumonia, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in cirrhotics with ascites, and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO).
- *Nutrient malabsorption*: Acid is required to release vitamin B12 from food proteins and to reduce dietary non-heme iron from Fe3+ to the absorbable Fe2+. Chronic PPI use is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency (with prolonged use >2-3 years), iron deficiency, hypomagnesemia (the mechanism is PPI-induced inhibition of active magnesium transport via TRPM6 channels in the gut; this is a rare but serious, FDA-warned adverse effect that can cause tetany, seizures, and cardiac arrhythmias), and calcium malabsorption (controversially linked to an increased risk of osteoporosis and hip fractures, particularly in high-dose, long-term users).
- *Chronic kidney disease*: PPIs are associated with an increased risk of acute interstitial nephritis (a hypersensitivity reaction that can occur at any time, presenting with insidious, non-specific symptoms of fatigue, nausea, and slowly rising creatinine; often missed) and progression of chronic kidney disease. The FDA has issued a warning.
- *Rebound acid hypersecretion*: Upon abrupt discontinuation of a PPI after prolonged use (>4-8 weeks), the hypergastrinemia-driven increase in ECL cell mass and parietal cell mass causes a temporary, symptomatically significant rebound increase in acid secretion. This causes dyspepsia and heartburn, which can lead to chronic, unnecessary, long-term PPI dependence. PPIs must be tapered, not stopped abruptly, after prolonged courses.

### Dosages (Standard Adult):

- Omeprazole: 20-40 mg once daily
- Esomeprazole: 20-40 mg once daily
- Lansoprazole: 15-30 mg once daily
- Dexlansoprazole: 30-60 mg once daily
- Pantoprazole: 40 mg once daily (oral); 40 mg IV once daily
- Rabeprazole: 20 mg once daily

### Duration of Treatment:

- Erosive esophagitis: 8 weeks; may require maintenance therapy for severe disease.
- Duodenal ulcer: 4 weeks.
- Gastric ulcer: 8 weeks.
- H. pylori eradication: 10-14 days (duration of the antibiotic regimen).
- Idiopathic GERD without esophagitis: a trial of 4-8 weeks, followed by tapering to the lowest effective dose or on-demand use.
- Prevention of NSAID ulcer: indefinitely, as long as the NSAID is continued.

### 1.2 Histamine H2-Receptor Antagonists (H2RAs)

H2RAs are competitive, reversible antagonists of the H2 receptor on the basolateral membrane of the gastric parietal cell. Histamine, released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells in the gastric mucosa, is the primary paracrine agonist for gastric acid secretion, acting synergistically with gastrin and acetylcholine. By blocking the H2 receptor, H2RAs reduce intracellular cAMP generation and inhibit the activation of the H+/K+-ATPase. They are less potent than PPIs because they competitively antagonize only one of the three acid secretory pathways; gastrin and vagal stimulation can still partially stimulate acid secretion. They are effective at suppressing nocturnal basal acid secretion but less effective at suppressing meal-stimulated (postprandial) acid secretion. Tolerance (tachyphylaxis) develops rapidly, within 3-5 days of continuous dosing, significantly limiting their long-term efficacy for chronic acid suppression. This tolerance is thought to be due to the resulting hypergastrinemia, which stimulates histamine release from ECL cells that overcomes the competitive block. For this reason, PPIs have largely replaced H2RAs for chronic acid suppression. [1,3]

### Names:

- Famotidine
- Cimetidine (largely historical; the least potent and associated with the most drug interactions)
- Nizatidine (withdrawn from many markets due to the presence of NDMA, a probable human carcinogen)

### Indications:

- Mild, intermittent GERD (on-demand use)
- Peptic ulcer disease (healing, now uncommon as PPIs are preferred)
- Prevention of stress-related mucosal disease in non-critically ill hospitalized patients
- Over-the-counter relief of heartburn and dyspepsia

### Contraindications:

- Hypersensitivity

### Important Adverse Effects:

- *Cimetidine-specific*: Inhibits multiple CYP450 isoenzymes (CYP1A2, 2C9, 2D6, 3A4), leading to numerous clinically significant drug interactions (e.g., warfarin, theophylline, phenytoin). It also has anti-androgenic effects because it binds to the androgen receptor; it can cause reversible gynecomastia and impotence in men taking high doses for prolonged periods (e.g., for Zollinger-Ellison syndrome). These effects are not seen with famotidine.
- *Famotidine*: Generally very well-tolerated. Does not inhibit CYP450. Rarely causes confusion, delirium, hallucinations, or seizures, especially in elderly patients with renal impairment and high IV doses (dose reduction is required in renal failure).

### Dosages (Standard Adult):

- Famotidine: 20-40 mg orally once or twice daily; 20 mg IV every 12 hours.

### 1.3 Antacids

Weak bases (aluminum hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate) that chemically neutralize existing gastric hydrochloric acid. They do not inhibit acid secretion. Their action is rapid (within minutes) but short-lived (30-60 minutes, unless taken after a meal, which prolongs gastric emptying and extends the duration of action to 2-3 hours). They provide purely symptomatic relief of heartburn. [1]

### Names and Key Features:

- *Calcium carbonate (Tums)*: Rapid neutralization. Can cause rebound acid hypersecretion (the absorbed calcium stimulates gastrin release). Overuse can cause the milk-alkali syndrome (hypercalcemia, metabolic alkalosis, and acute kidney injury).
- *Aluminum hydroxide*: Constipating. Binds dietary phosphate in the gut, preventing its absorption; used therapeutically as a phosphate binder in chronic kidney disease.
- *Magnesium hydroxide (Milk of Magnesia)*: An osmotic laxative. Causes diarrhea. Contraindicated in renal failure (risk of hypermagnesemia, causing neuromuscular depression, respiratory failure, and cardiac arrest).
- *Aluminum hydroxide/Magnesium hydroxide combinations (e.g., Maalox, Mylanta)*: The constipating effect of aluminum and the laxative effect of magnesium are balanced to minimize bowel habit disturbance.
- *Alginates (Gaviscon)*: Alginic acid combined with an antacid. In the stomach, the alginate reacts with acid to form a low-density, viscous, foamy raft of alginic acid gel that floats on top of the gastric contents. This physical barrier mechanically prevents reflux. It provides more durable symptom relief than simple antacids.

### 1.4 Mucosal Protectants

**Sucralfate:**

A complex of aluminum hydroxide and sulfated sucrose. In the acidic environment of the stomach (pH < 4), it undergoes polymerization and cross-linking to form a viscous, sticky, adherent gel. This gel selectively binds to the positively charged proteins in the crater of an ulcer (the exposed, denuded, necrotic ulcer base), forming a protective physical barrier that shields the ulcer from acid, pepsin, and bile salts for up to 6 hours. It also stimulates local mucosal prostaglandin (PGE2) synthesis, promoting mucosal blood flow, bicarbonate secretion, and mucosal repair. It is an effective ulcer-healing agent, equivalent to H2RAs for duodenal ulcer healing. Because it requires an acidic pH to polymerize, it must not be co-administered with antacids, H2RAs, or PPIs (separate administration by at least 30-60 minutes). It also forms a non-specific barrier on the gastric mucosa that impairs the absorption of many concurrently administered medications (phenytoin, digoxin, warfarin, fluoroquinolones), which must be dosed 2 hours before or after sucralfate. The most common adverse effect is constipation (from the aluminum). It is used primarily for the prevention of stress ulcer prophylaxis in ICU patients and as a second-line agent for PUD. [1]

**Bismuth Subsalicylate:**

A complex molecule with multiple gastrointestinal effects. It has mild antimicrobial activity (bactericidal against H. pylori, though not sufficient as monotherapy; directly toxic to enteropathogenic bacteria like enterotoxigenic E. coli). It has anti-secretory and anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase and reducing mucosal prostaglandin synthesis. It coats the ulcer base. It binds enterotoxins (used for traveler's diarrhea). The salicylate component is absorbed and can interact with anticoagulants. It causes a harmless blackening of the tongue and stool (bismuth sulfide), which must be distinguished from melena. It is an integral component of quadruple therapy for H. pylori. [1]

**Misoprostol:**

A synthetic prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) analogue. It activates prostaglandin EP3 receptors on the parietal cell, inhibiting the activation of adenylate cyclase and reducing histamine-stimulated cAMP production, thereby inhibiting acid secretion. Its primary clinical utility, however, is its prostaglandin-mediated enhancement of the gastric mucosal barrier: it stimulates mucus and bicarbonate secretion from the surface mucous cells, increases mucosal blood flow, and promotes epithelial restitution. It is FDA-approved solely for the prevention of NSAID-induced gastric ulcers in high-risk patients. It is an abortifacient; it causes uterine smooth muscle contraction and is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy (it is an FDA Category X drug and must never be prescribed to a woman of childbearing potential unless a negative pregnancy test and reliable contraception are documented). Diarrhea, from increased intestinal chloride secretion and altered motility, is a frequent, dose-limiting adverse effect. [1]

### 1.5 Potassium-Competitive Acid Blockers (P-CABs)

A new class of acid-suppressive agents that competitively and reversibly inhibit the H+/K+-ATPase by binding to and blocking the K+ binding site on the pump. Unlike PPIs, P-CABs do not require acid activation; they are active in their native state. They achieve a higher concentration at the canalicular membrane more rapidly because they are concentrated in the parietal cell's acid compartment and are acid-stable. Their clinical effects are therefore much faster in onset (maximal acid suppression within hours of the first dose, vs. 3-5 days for PPIs), they are unaffected by food intake (can be taken without regard to meals), and they suppress acid more consistently, irrespective of CYP2C19 genotype. They are more effective than PPIs for severe erosive esophagitis. The first in class is vonoprazan, available in Japan and the US. [2]

### Names:

- Vonoprazan
- Tegoprazan
- Fexuprazan

### Indications:

- Erosive esophagitis (healing and maintenance)

## H. pylori eradication

- Non-erosive GERD

**Adverse Effects:** Similar profile to PPIs, with the same long-term concerns expected (hypergastrinemia, infection risk), though long-term data are still accumulating. [2]

## 2. Prokinetic Agents

Prokinetics are drugs that enhance the propulsive motility of the gastrointestinal tract by increasing the frequency or strength of peristaltic contractions, accelerating gastric emptying, and improving esophageal and small bowel transit. They are indicated for gastroparesis and for motility-predominant functional dyspepsia. [1,3]

**Metoclopramide:**

Mechanism of Action: Metoclopramide has a dual mechanism. (1) It is a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist in the chemoreceptor trigger zone (area postrema) and the GI tract. In the GI tract, blockade of the inhibitory D2 receptors on the enteric cholinergic neurons disinhibits acetylcholine release, enhancing the amplitude of gastric antral contractions, relaxing the pylorus, and coordinating antroduodenal motility, thereby accelerating gastric emptying. (2) It is a 5-HT4 receptor agonist, which also stimulates acetylcholine release from the myenteric plexus. (3) It is a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist at high doses, contributing to its antiemetic effect, which is centrally mediated by blocking D2 receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone.

Indications: Gastroparesis (the only FDA-approved prokinetic for this indication; use is limited to a maximum of 12 weeks due to the risk of tardive dyskinesia), severe GERD refractory to PPIs (as a promotility adjunct, largely historical), nausea and vomiting.

Contraindications: Gastrointestinal obstruction, perforation, or hemorrhage; history of seizure disorder; pheochromocytoma (can cause a hypertensive crisis by releasing catecholamines from the tumor); concurrent use of drugs that also cause extrapyramidal symptoms or tardive dyskinesia.

Important Adverse Effects: Central nervous system effects from dopamine D2 receptor antagonism in the nigrostriatal pathway. Acute dystonic reactions (torticollis, oculogyric crisis, trismus) are frightening, occur within 24-72 hours of initiating therapy, and are more common in young women and children. They are treated with IV diphenhydramine or benztropine. Parkinsonism (bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity) can develop with prolonged use. Tardive dyskinesia, the most feared adverse effect, is a potentially irreversible, disfiguring, involuntary, choreoathetoid movement disorder predominantly affecting the face, mouth, and tongue. The risk increases with the duration of treatment and cumulative dose, and is higher in elderly women. The FDA has issued a black box warning. Hyperprolactinemia from pituitary D2 blockade causes galactorrhea, amenorrhea, and gynecomastia.

Dosage: 10 mg orally or IV 30 minutes before meals and at bedtime (for gastroparesis). The maximum duration in the US is 12 weeks. [1,3]

**Domperidone:**

Mechanism of Action: A peripheral dopamine D2 receptor antagonist. It is functionally similar to metoclopramide in its GI prokinetic and antiemetic actions because it blocks D2 receptors in the GI myenteric plexus and the chemoreceptor trigger zone (which is outside the blood-brain barrier). However, it does not cross the blood-brain barrier and therefore has a much lower incidence of central nervous system (extrapyramidal) adverse effects than metoclopramide.

Indications: Gastroparesis, functional dyspepsia with postprandial distress, nausea and vomiting. It is not FDA-approved in the US but is widely used in Europe, Canada, and other countries, and is available in the US only under an FDA Expanded Access Investigational New Drug (IND) protocol.

Important Adverse Effects: Hyperprolactinemia (galactorrhea, amenorrhea). The primary safety concern is QT interval prolongation, which predisposes to torsades de pointes and sudden cardiac death. This risk is dose-dependent and is increased at doses >30 mg/day, in patients over 60 years of age, and with concurrent use of other QT-prolonging drugs or CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole, clarithromycin). A baseline ECG and monitoring are required. [1]

**Erythromycin:**

Mechanism of Action: A macrolide antibiotic that, at sub-antimicrobial doses, acts as a potent motilin receptor agonist. Motilin is a 22-amino-acid peptide hormone secreted by the M cells in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa during the fasting state. Its physiological role is to initiate Phase III of the migrating motor complex (the housekeeper wave). Erythromycin binds to the motilin receptor on the gastric and duodenal smooth muscle cells and on cholinergic neurons in the myenteric plexus, triggering high-amplitude, forceful, propulsive antral contractions that accelerate the gastric emptying of solids. It is the most potent gastric prokinetic agent available for acute use. However, its clinical utility is severely limited by rapid tachyphylaxis: the motilin receptor downregulates with continuous exposure, and its prokinetic effect wanes significantly after 2-4 weeks. It is thus used primarily for acute exacerbations of gastroparesis in hospitalized patients.

Indications: Acute gastroparesis exacerbation (especially for clearing retained gastric contents prior to endoscopy), critically ill patients with feed intolerance. It is not suitable for chronic gastroparesis management.

Dosage: 250 mg orally or IV 3 times daily, 30 minutes before meals. IV is more commonly used.

Important Adverse Effects: QT prolongation, sudden cardiac death (risk is especially pronounced with oral erythromycin and in combination with CYP3A4 inhibitors; IV azithromycin is safer from a cardiac standpoint but has less robust prokinetic data). Nausea, abdominal cramping, and diarrhea (from the motilin-stimulated propulsive contractions, and from antibiotic disruption of the gut microbiome). Pyloric stenosis has been associated with erythromycin use in infants (for pertussis prophylaxis), which was the basis for the lawsuit that established the concept of class-action litigation against a drug. [1]

**Prucalopride:**

Mechanism of Action: A highly selective, high-affinity serotonin 5-HT4 receptor agonist. Activation of 5-HT4 receptors on the presynaptic terminals of the intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs) in the myenteric plexus stimulates the release of acetylcholine and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which initiates the peristaltic reflex. Unlike the older, non-selective 5-HT4 agonists cisapride (withdrawn due to fatal QT prolongation from hERG channel blockade and CYP3A4 interactions) and tegaserod (withdrawn, then reintroduced with restricted access due to cardiovascular ischemic events from 5-HT1 receptor-mediated vasoconstriction), prucalopride has a very clean receptor profile; it does not block the hERG potassium channel or significant 5-HT1B/D or 5-HT2B receptors, giving it a favorable cardiac safety profile.

Indications: Chronic idiopathic constipation in adults when standard laxatives have failed to provide adequate relief.

Dosage: 2 mg orally once daily (1 mg in patients >65 years).

Important Adverse Effects: Headache, nausea, diarrhea, abdominal pain. These are generally transient and reflect the prokinetic effect. No clinically significant QT prolongation or cardiovascular adverse events have been identified in trials. [1]

## 3. Antiemetic Agents

Antiemetics suppress nausea and vomiting by antagonizing one or more of the key neurotransmitter receptors in the medullary vomiting center, the chemoreceptor trigger zone (area postrema), and the GI tract (via vagal afferents): dopamine D2, serotonin 5-HT3, histamine H1, muscarinic M1, neurokinin-1 (NK1, substance P), and cannabinoid CB1 receptors. [1,3]

### 5-HT3 Receptor Antagonists (Setrons):

- Names: Ondansetron, Granisetron, Dolasetron, Palonosetron.
- Mechanism of Action: Potent, selective antagonists of the serotonin 5-HT3 receptor. They have a dual site of antiemetic action: (1) Centrally, they block 5-HT3 receptors in the area postrema (chemoreceptor trigger zone). (2) Peripherally, they block 5-HT3 receptors on the vagal afferent nerve terminals in the GI tract. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy cause the release of serotonin from the enterochromaffin cells of the small intestinal mucosa, which activates 5-HT3 receptors on the vagal endings, triggering the vomiting reflex. Setrons are the most effective agents for the acute phase of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) and radiation-induced nausea and vomiting (RINV), and are highly effective for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).
- Important Adverse Effects: Headache, constipation (from the blockade of 5-HT3 receptors on the colonic myenteric plexus, impairing the peristaltic reflex), transient, asymptomatic QTc prolongation (most pronounced with dolasetron and higher-dose ondansetron; the FDA has restricted the maximum single dose of ondansetron to 16 mg IV for this reason).
- Key Distinction: Palonosetron has a longer half-life (40 hours vs. 3-6 hours for ondansetron) and a unique allosteric binding to the 5-HT3 receptor, conferring superior efficacy for delayed CINV.

### Dopamine D2 Receptor Antagonists:

- Names: Metoclopramide (see Prokinetics), Prochlorperazine (Compazine), Promethazine (Phenergan), Haloperidol, Droperidol, Chlorpromazine.
- Mechanism of Action: Blockade of dopamine D2 receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone (area postrema), which is located outside the blood-brain barrier on the floor of the fourth ventricle. The chemoreceptor trigger zone samples the systemic circulation and CSF for emetogenic toxins, and D2 receptors are the primary receptors mediating the detection of these emetogenic stimuli. Phenothiazines (prochlorperazine, promethazine, chlorpromazine) also have anticholinergic and antihistaminic (H1) effects that contribute to their antiemetic efficacy. Haloperidol and droperidol are butyrophenone antipsychotics used as potent antiemetics at low doses.
- Important Adverse Effects: Extrapyramidal symptoms (acute dystonic reaction, akathisia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia) from the blockade of D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway. Sedation. QT prolongation (droperidol has an FDA black box warning; it is a second-line agent requiring ECG monitoring). Promethazine is highly vesicant and causes severe tissue necrosis if extravasated from a vein or inadvertently injected into an artery; deep intramuscular injection is preferred.

### NK1 Receptor Antagonists:

- Names: Aprepitant, Fosaprepitant (IV prodrug of aprepitant), Rolapitant, Netupitant (combined with palonosetron as NEPA).
- Mechanism of Action: Selective, high-affinity antagonists of the neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor. Substance P, the endogenous ligand for the NK1 receptor, is a key neurotransmitter in the final common pathway of the vomiting reflex in the nucleus tractus solitarius and the vomiting center. NK1 receptor antagonists are uniquely effective for the delayed phase of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) (24-120 hours after chemotherapy) and provide significant additive benefit when combined with a 5-HT3 antagonist and dexamethasone for highly emetogenic chemotherapy.
- Adverse Effects: Fatigue, hiccups, anorexia. Aprepitant, fosaprepitant, and netupitant are moderate inhibitors and inducers of CYP3A4; they interact with many drugs, including dexamethasone (whose dose must be reduced when co-administered) and oral contraceptives.

### Corticosteroids (Dexamethasone):

- Mechanism of Action: The antiemetic mechanism is incompletely understood, but its profound efficacy in acute and delayed CINV has made it an essential component of virtually all modern antiemetic protocols. Proposed mechanisms include: central suppression of prostaglandin synthesis (inhibition of COX-2 and phospholipase A2) in the area postrema and the nucleus tractus solitarius, reduction of 5-HT release, and stabilization of the blood-brain barrier in the area postrema. The anti-inflammatory and anti-edema effects also contribute.
- Dexamethasone is used in combination with 5-HT3 and NK1 antagonists.

### Histamine H1 Receptor Antagonists:

- Names: Dimenhydrinate (Dramamine), Meclizine, Cyclizine, Diphenhydramine, Promethazine.
- Mechanism of Action: Blockade of histamine H1 receptors and muscarinic M1 receptors in the vestibular nucleus and the cerebellum. They are the primary agents for the prevention and treatment of motion sickness, Ménière's disease, and other vestibular causes of vertigo-associated nausea and vomiting. They are also used in the emergency department for acute, undifferentiated nausea and vomiting.
- Adverse Effects: Sedation (profound with dimenhydrinate and diphenhydramine, which are also used as hypnotics; less with meclizine), anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion in the elderly).

### Cannabinoids:

- Names: Dronabinol (synthetic delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, THC), Nabilone (synthetic cannabinoid).
- Mechanism of Action: Agonists at the cannabinoid CB1 receptor in the brain. The precise antiemetic mechanism is unclear but may involve inhibition of serotonin and dopamine release in the vomiting center and area postrema, and cortical effects that alter the perception of nausea.
- Indications: Refractory chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting unresponsive to standard antiemetics, and HIV/AIDS-associated anorexia and cachexia (the appetite stimulation effect is a key therapeutic advantage in this population).
- Adverse Effects: Euphoria, dysphoria, sedation, dizziness, hallucinations, psychosis, tachycardia, hypotension. The psychoactive effects limit their widespread use.

## 4. Agents for Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

### 4.1 Aminosalicylates (5-ASA)

- Names: Sulfasalazine, Mesalamine (Mesalazine), Olsalazine, Balsalazide.
- Mechanism of Action: The active moiety is 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA). It acts topically on the colonic mucosa. The precise anti-inflammatory mechanism is pleiotropic and not fully defined: it is a potent agonist of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma (PPAR-γ), a nuclear receptor highly expressed in colonic epithelial cells. Activation of PPAR-γ negatively regulates the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes by antagonizing NF-κB, thereby inhibiting the production of TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, and leukotrienes. 5-ASA also inhibits cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways, reducing prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis. 5-ASA is a free radical scavenger, reducing oxidative tissue damage. It also inhibits lymphocyte function and antibody secretion. The key principle is that 5-ASA must be delivered to the colonic mucosa in high concentration. The various formulations represent different delivery systems to achieve this goal:
  - *Sulfasalazine*: 5-ASA linked by an azo bond to sulfapyridine (a sulfonamide antibiotic, which acts solely as a carrier to prevent proximal small bowel absorption). Colonic bacterial azoreductases cleave the azo bond, releasing active 5-ASA and the sulfapyridine, which is absorbed and causes most of the adverse effects (nausea, headache, oligospermia, rash, hemolytic anemia in G6PD deficiency).
  - *Mesalamine*: 5-ASA alone, with various pH-dependent (Eudragit coating that dissolves at pH >7, delivering 5-ASA to the terminal ileum and colon) and time-dependent controlled-release formulations.
  - *Olsalazine*: Two 5-ASA molecules linked by an azo bond. Cleaved in the colon. The most common adverse effect is a dose-dependent, secretory diarrhea from the stimulation of ileal and colonic chloride secretion.
  - *Balsalazide*: 5-ASA linked to an inert, non-absorbed carrier (4-aminobenzoyl-β-alanine). Colonic azoreductase releases the 5-ASA.
- Indications: First-line therapy for the induction of remission and maintenance of remission in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis. The role in Crohn's disease is very limited (ineffective for maintenance, marginal for induction).
- Adverse Effects: Sulfasalazine adverse effects are mainly due to the sulfapyridine moiety. The newer 5-ASA agents are very well-tolerated. Rare but serious: acute interstitial nephritis (idiosyncratic, dose-independent, reversible with early detection; monitoring of serum creatinine is mandatory every 3-6 months for the first year, then annually). A paradoxical worsening of colitis can occur (an allergic-type reaction). [4,5]

### 4.2 Corticosteroids

- Names: Prednisone, Methylprednisolone, Budesonide.
- Mechanism of Action: Corticosteroids bind to the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor, translocate to the nucleus, and potently suppress the transcription of virtually all pro-inflammatory cytokine genes (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, chemokines) by multiple mechanisms, including direct inhibition of the transcription factors NF-κB and AP-1. They inhibit phospholipase A2 (via annexin-1 induction), preventing the release of arachidonic acid and the subsequent synthesis of prostaglandins and leukotrienes. They reduce vascular permeability, inhibit neutrophil and lymphocyte migration into tissues, and promote the apoptosis of activated lymphocytes. They are the most rapidly and universally effective anti-inflammatory agents for the acute induction of remission in moderate-to-severe IBD.
- Indications: Induction of remission in moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease. They are absolutely NOT maintenance agents. Budesonide is a high-potency, low-systemic-bioavailability corticosteroid with extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism (90% via CYP3A4). It is formulated in an oral, pH-dependent, controlled-release preparation (budesonide MMX for UC; Entocort for ileocecal Crohn's) that delivers the drug directly to the distal ileum and colon, where it exerts a potent topical anti-inflammatory effect, with minimal systemic absorption. It is indicated for the induction of remission in mild-to-moderate ileocecal Crohn's disease and mild-to-moderate UC, with significantly fewer systemic adverse effects than prednisone.
- Adverse Effects: The classic, well-known Cushingoid syndrome (moon facies, truncal obesity, buffalo hump, striae, acne, hirsutism) and the protean, devastating systemic effects of long-term use: osteoporosis, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, cataracts, glaucoma, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, myopathy, psychosis, adrenal suppression. The dictum is: "Start high, taper fast." A typical prednisone taper is 40 mg daily for 1-2 weeks, then tapering by 5 mg per week. Every patient on corticosteroids for >3 months requires osteoporosis prophylaxis (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate) and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis. [4,5]

### 4.3 Thiopurines (Immunomodulators)

- Names: Azathioprine (AZA), 6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP).
- Mechanism of Action: Azathioprine is a prodrug that is non-enzymatically converted to 6-MP in the blood. 6-MP is then metabolized by three competing intracellular pathways. The therapeutic pathway is the conversion by hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) to the active thiopurine nucleotides (6-thioguanine nucleotides, 6-TGNs). The 6-TGNs are purine nucleotide analogues that are incorporated into the DNA of rapidly dividing lymphocytes, inhibiting DNA replication and inducing apoptosis. They also inhibit Rac1, a small GTPase involved in T-cell activation and the inflammatory response. The two other metabolic pathways are responsible for toxicity: (1) metabolism by thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) to 6-methylmercaptopurine (6-MMP), which is hepatotoxic at high levels; and (2) metabolism by xanthine oxidase to the inactive 6-thiouric acid. The activity of TPMT is genetically determined. Approximately 1 in 300 individuals of European descent are homozygous for a low-activity TPMT allele and have absent TPMT activity. In these patients, the normal dose of azathioprine shunts the entire 6-MP load down the HGPRT pathway, producing massively excessive, potentially lethal 6-TGN levels that cause severe, life-threatening myelosuppression. TPMT genotyping or enzymatic testing is mandatory before starting a thiopurine. Concurrent use of allopurinol (a xanthine oxidase inhibitor for gout) blocks the xanthine oxidase pathway, also shunting metabolism toward 6-TGNs. When allopurinol is combined with a thiopurine, the dose of the thiopurine must be reduced to 25-33% of the standard dose, and levels must be monitored.
- Indications: Steroid-sparing maintenance of remission in IBD (both UC and Crohn's). They have a slow onset of action (8-12 weeks) and are therefore not used for acute induction.
- Adverse Effects: (1) Myelosuppression (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia), which is dose-dependent and predictable based on TPMT activity and 6-TGN monitoring, but can occur at any time. Complete blood counts must be monitored. (2) Hepatotoxicity (elevated transaminases, cholestatic hepatitis, nodular regenerative hyperplasia). (3) Pancreatitis (acute pancreatitis, occurring in 3-5% of patients, usually within the first 4 weeks; it is an idiosyncratic, dose-independent reaction that mandates permanent discontinuation of the drug; re-challenge is contraindicated as pancreatitis will recur within hours). (4) Nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort. (5) Non-melanoma skin cancer (increased risk, mandate annual dermatological surveillance and strict sun protection). (6) Lymphoma (an increased risk of EBV-driven post-transplant-like lymphoproliferative disorder, particularly in young males under 35 years of age, and hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma, a rare but almost universally fatal malignancy, predominantly in young males with Crohn's disease on combination thiopurine and anti-TNF therapy for >2 years). [4,5]

### 4.4 Methotrexate

- Mechanism of Action: A dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) inhibitor that blocks the synthesis of thymidylate and purines, inhibiting DNA synthesis and lymphocyte proliferation. At the low doses used for IBD (25 mg subcutaneously weekly), its anti-inflammatory effect is primarily mediated by the release of adenosine, a potent endogenous anti-inflammatory purine nucleoside. Methotrexate increases intracellular and extracellular adenosine levels by inhibiting the enzyme aminoimidazole carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) transformylase. Adenosine binds to A2A and A2B receptors on inflammatory cells, suppressing the production of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, and inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and adhesion.
- Indications: Steroid-sparing maintenance of remission in Crohn's disease. It is effective for inducing and maintaining remission in Crohn's disease. Its role in UC is much less established.
- Adverse Effects: (1) Myelosuppression. (2) Hepatotoxicity (chronic use can lead to fibrosis and cirrhosis). (3) Pneumonitis (an acute or subacute, hypersensitivity or dose-related inflammatory lung disease, presenting with dyspnea, non-productive cough, and fever; requires immediate cessation and corticosteroid treatment). (4) Teratogenicity (absolute contraindication in pregnancy; it is an abortifacient and a folate antagonist causing neural tube defects). (5) Folate deficiency-related adverse effects (nausea, stomatitis, fatigue). Folic acid 1 mg daily must be co-prescribed to reduce these, without compromising the anti-inflammatory efficacy (folinic acid is used for rescue in toxicity). [4,5]

### 4.5 Calcineurin Inhibitors

- Cyclosporine A: A calcineurin inhibitor that blocks the dephosphorylation of the nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), preventing its translocation to the nucleus and the transcription of IL-2 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. It is a potent, rapid-acting, salvage therapy for hospitalized patients with acute severe, intravenous corticosteroid-refractory ulcerative colitis, as a "bridge" to thiopurine or biologic maintenance therapy. Administered as a continuous IV infusion with serum drug level monitoring (target trough 300-500 ng/mL). The efficacy window is 5-7 days; if no response, colectomy is indicated. Toxicity is substantial and includes nephrotoxicity (acute renal vasoconstriction and chronic tubulointerstitial fibrosis, the dose-limiting toxicity), hypertension, tremors, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, seizures (especially in hypomagnesemia or with low cholesterol), and opportunistic infections (particularly Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia; mandatory prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is required). [4,5]
- Tacrolimus: An alternative calcineurin inhibitor with a similar mechanism but higher potency. Used for the same indication. Drug monitoring is required.

### 4.6 Biologic Agents (Monoclonal Antibodies)

### Anti-TNF-α Agents:

- Names: Infliximab, Adalimumab, Golimumab, Certolizumab pegol.
- Mechanism of Action: Chimeric (infliximab) or fully human (adalimumab, golimumab) IgG1 monoclonal antibodies, or a PEGylated humanized Fab' fragment (certolizumab pegol), that bind soluble and membrane-bound tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) with high affinity and neutralise it. TNF-α is a master pro-inflammatory cytokine in the pathogenesis of IBD. By neutralising TNF-α, these agents block the TNF-TNFR1/TNFR2 signaling cascade, reducing the production of other pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6), reducing leukocyte recruitment, inducing apoptosis of activated lamina propria T lymphocytes and macrophages (via reverse signaling through membrane-bound TNF and by antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and complement-dependent cytotoxicity for the IgG1 agents), and reducing angiogenesis. The induction of apoptosis of T cells is unique to anti-TNFs among biologics and is thought to be critical for their profound efficacy.
- Indications: Induction and maintenance of remission in moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, both adult and pediatric. Infliximab is the drug of choice for fistulising Crohn's disease.
- Adverse Effects: (1) Acute infusion reactions (infliximab: chills, fever, chest pain, dyspnea, hypotension; anaphylaxis). (2) Delayed hypersensitivity (serum sickness-like reaction: arthralgia, myalgia, rash, fever, 5-14 days after an infusion, associated with anti-drug antibodies). (3) Reactivation of latent tuberculosis (TB). TNF-α is essential for the formation and maintenance of the granuloma that walls off Mycobacterium tuberculosis in latent infection. Neutralising TNF-α disrupts the granuloma, releasing viable bacteria and leading to reactivation. All patients must undergo rigorous screening for latent TB (interferon-gamma release assay or tuberculin skin test, plus a chest X-ray) before initiating any anti-TNF agent. If latent TB is detected, treatment for latent TB must be initiated before the biologic. (4) Reactivation of hepatitis B. All patients must be screened for HBsAg and anti-HBc. In HBsAg+ patients, prophylactic antivirals (e.g., entecavir) must be initiated 2 weeks before the biologic and continued for 6-12 months after it is stopped. (5) Non-melanoma skin cancer and a rare but severe hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (in young male patients on combination thiopurine + anti-TNF therapy). (6) Demyelinating disease (optic neuritis, multiple sclerosis-like syndrome) is a rare but serious adverse effect. (7) Worsening heart failure (contraindicated in NYHA Class III-IV heart failure). (8) Formation of anti-drug antibodies (immunogenicity), which cause loss of response (secondary non-response) and infusion reactions. The risk is reduced by scheduled maintenance dosing (rather than episodic), and by combination with an immunomodulator (thiopurine or methotrexate). [4,5]

### Anti-Integrin Agents (Anti-α4β7):

- Name: Vedolizumab.
- Mechanism of Action: A humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to the α4β7 integrin on the surface of gut-homing T and B lymphocytes. The α4β7 integrin binds to mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule-1 (MAdCAM-1), a receptor selectively expressed on the endothelial cells of the intestinal venules (Peyer's patches and the lamina propria). This interaction is the critical, tissue-specific "postal code" that mediates the selective trafficking of lymphocytes from the bloodstream into the intestinal mucosa. By blocking α4β7-MAdCAM-1 binding, vedolizumab specifically inhibits the homing of lymphocytes to the gut, reducing the chronic inflammatory infiltrate in the intestinal mucosa, without systemic immunosuppression (it does not affect lymphocyte trafficking to the brain, a key difference from natalizumab, an anti-α4 integrin used in multiple sclerosis that caused progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, PML, because it blocked the α4β1-VCAM-1 interaction, which is critical for the immune surveillance of the CNS by T cells. Vedolizumab is gut-selective and has not been associated with PML).
- Indications: Induction and maintenance of remission in moderate-to-severe UC and Crohn's disease, especially in patients who have failed or are intolerant to anti-TNF agents. Its gut-selectivity makes it the safest biologic in terms of systemic infection risk.
- Adverse Effects: Generally well-tolerated. Mild, infusion-related reactions. A theoretical increased risk of enteric infections. No systemic immunosuppressive signal (no increased risk of TB, no PML risk). Onset of action is slower than anti-TNF agents (10-14 weeks). [4,5]

### Anti-IL-12/23 Agents:

- Name: Ustekinumab.
- Mechanism of Action: A fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to the shared p40 subunit of the interleukins IL-12 and IL-23, preventing their interaction with the IL-12Rβ1 receptor on the surface of T and NK cells. IL-12 drives the differentiation of naive T cells into Th1 cells (producing IFN-γ). IL-23 is essential for the stabilization and expansion of the pathogenic Th17 cell lineage (producing IL-17, IL-22, and GM-CSF). By blocking both IL-12 and IL-23, ustekinumab powerfully suppresses the Th1 and Th17 pathways, which are central to the pathogenesis of both Crohn's disease and psoriasis.
- Indications: Induction and maintenance of remission in moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.
- Adverse Effects: An excellent long-term safety profile. No increased risk of serious infection, malignancy, or TB reactivation in the large psoriasis and IBD databases. The induction is a single IV weight-based infusion, followed by subcutaneous maintenance every 8 weeks. [4,5]

### Anti-IL-23 (p19-specific) Agents:

- Names: Risankizumab, Guselkumab, Mirikizumab.
- Mechanism of Action: These are monoclonal antibodies that bind specifically to the p19 subunit unique to IL-23, thereby selectively blocking the IL-23-Th17 axis, without blocking IL-12-Th1 signaling. This selective inhibition is hypothesized to offer a more targeted safety profile while maintaining efficacy. Risankizumab and guselkumab are FDA-approved for Crohn's disease and/or UC. Mirikizumab is FDA-approved for UC.
- Indications: Moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease and UC, with excellent efficacy and safety data from pivotal trials.
- Adverse Effects: Very well tolerated. Injection site reactions, headache, arthralgia. No safety signals for serious infection, TB, or malignancy have been identified. [5]

### 4.7 Janus Kinase (JAK) Inhibitors

- Names: Tofacitinib, Upadacitinib, Filgotinib.
- Mechanism of Action: Small-molecule, orally administered inhibitors of the Janus kinase (JAK) family of intracellular tyrosine kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2). JAKs are physically associated with the cytoplasmic tails of the receptors for numerous cytokines (including IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, interferons, GM-CSF). When a cytokine binds its receptor, JAKs are activated, phosphorylate each other (transphosphorylation), and then phosphorylate the receptor, creating a docking site for Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STAT) proteins. The STAT proteins are then phosphorylated, dimerize, and translocate to the nucleus, where they drive the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. JAK inhibitors block this JAK-STAT pathway. Different JAK inhibitors have different isoform selectivity profiles. Tofacitinib (pan-JAK, primarily JAK1/JAK3) and upadacitinib (selective JAK1 inhibitor) are the major agents.
- Indications: Moderate-to-severe UC (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) and Crohn's disease (upadacitinib). Their rapid onset of action (within days for symptomatic improvement, especially for urgency) is a major clinical advantage over biologics.
- Adverse Effects: (1) Herpes zoster (shingles): a dose-dependent increased risk, particularly with tofacitinib. Vaccination with the recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) is mandatory before starting therapy. (2) Venous thromboembolism (VTE): an FDA black box warning for tofacitinib at higher doses (>10 mg BID) in patients with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors, based on a post-marketing safety study. The mechanism is unclear. (3) Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). (4) Malignancy. (5) Hyperlipidemia (elevated LDL and HDL). (6) Elevation of creatine phosphokinase (CPK). (7) Cytopenias (anemia, lymphopenia, neutropenia). (8) Opportunistic infections (including tuberculosis, as with biologics; TB screening is mandatory). [4,5]

### 4.8 Sphingosine-1-Phosphate (S1P) Receptor Modulators

- Names: Ozanimod, Etrasimod.
- Mechanism of Action: These are small-molecule, orally administered agonists of the sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor subtypes 1 and 5. S1P is a bioactive lysophospholipid that, when bound to the S1P1 receptor on lymphocytes, is a critical signal for lymphocyte egress from the lymph nodes into the systemic circulation. Ozanimod and etrasimod bind and functionally antagonise the S1P1 receptor (by causing sustained receptor internalization and degradation), thereby sequestering the pathogenic, gut-homing lymphocytes in the lymph nodes. The lymphocytes cannot exit the lymph nodes, and the circulating lymphocyte count falls (peripheral lymphopenia). This prevents the lymphocytes from reaching the intestinal mucosa to fuel the chronic inflammation.
- Indications: Moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis. Ozanimod is also approved for relapsing multiple sclerosis.
- Adverse Effects: (1) First-dose bradycardia and AV block (due to the initial agonism at cardiac S1P1 receptors before desensitization occurs). All patients require a baseline ECG, and dose titration is required (ozanimod). (2) Macular edema (requires baseline and periodic ophthalmologic evaluation). (3) Hepatic transaminase elevation. (4) Hypertension. (5) Herpes zoster infection. (6) Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) has not been reported with ozanimod, but is a class effect of S1P modulators (fingolimod). [5]

## 5. Agents for Functional Bowel Disorders

### 5.1 Agents for Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Constipation (IBS-C) and Chronic Idiopathic Constipation

- **Lubiprostone:** A bicyclic fatty acid (prostone) derived from prostaglandin E1. It is a chloride-2 (ClC-2) channel activator on the apical membrane of the intestinal enterocyte. Activation of ClC-2 channels increases the secretion of chloride-rich fluid into the intestinal lumen. This increased luminal fluid passively distends the bowel and stimulates a reflex increase in peristalsis, accelerating colonic transit and softening the stool. It does not stimulate smooth muscle contraction directly. It is FDA-approved for IBS-C and chronic idiopathic constipation. The most common adverse effect is nausea (20-30%) which is often reduced by taking it with food. [1]
- **Linaclotide and Plecanatide:** These are 14-amino-acid and 16-amino-acid synthetic peptides, respectively, that function as agonists of the guanylate cyclase-C (GC-C) receptor on the apical surface of the colonic enterocyte. The endogenous ligand for GC-C is uroguanylin, a paracrine hormone released in response to a meal. Activation of GC-C generates cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) inside the enterocyte. Intracellular cGMP activates protein kinase G II, which phosphorylates and opens the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) chloride channel on the apical membrane, and inhibits the sodium-proton exchanger NHE3. The net result is a marked increase in the secretion of chloride and bicarbonate into the lumen (and a reduction in sodium absorption), which osmotically drives water into the lumen, accelerating colonic transit. The increase in luminal fluid and accelerated transit together soften the stool and increase bowel frequency. Additionally, the rise in extracellular cGMP, released from the enterocyte, is the key analgesic mechanism: extracellular cGMP directly inhibits the colonic nociceptive afferent nerve fibers in the lamina propria, reducing visceral hypersensitivity and abdominal pain. This dual mechanism of action (secretory + analgesic) makes GC-C agonists uniquely suited for IBS-C, where both constipation and abdominal pain require treatment. Both drugs are minimally absorbed and act locally. Plecanatide has a slightly more physiological pH-dependent activation (pH 5-6), while linaclotide is optimally active at the more alkaline pH of the duodenum and jejunum. The principal adverse effect is dose-dependent diarrhea from excessive chloride/water secretion. [1]

### 5.2 Agents for Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Diarrhea (IBS-D)

- **Rifaximin:** A semisynthetic, poorly absorbed (<0.4% oral bioavailability) derivative of rifamycin that inhibits bacterial RNA synthesis by binding to the beta-subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase. It has broad-spectrum activity against Gram-positive, Gram-negative, aerobic, and anaerobic enteric bacteria. In IBS-D, its efficacy is thought to be due to the modulation of the gut microbiome (reducing the bacterial overgrowth, reducing the metabolic activity of the bacteria that produce the noxious metabolites that cause symptoms, and shifting the bacterial composition away from a pro-inflammatory phenotype). It is FDA-approved for IBS-D. A 2-week course provides symptomatic relief that can last for up to 10 weeks. The drug can be re-treated (up to 3 times) if symptoms recur. It is well-tolerated because it is not absorbed. [1]
- **Eluxadoline:** A mu-opioid receptor agonist and delta-opioid receptor antagonist, and a kappa-opioid receptor agonist, all within a single molecule. It has very limited oral absorption and acts locally in the myenteric plexus of the bowel. The mu-opioid agonism reduces gastrointestinal smooth muscle contractility and propulsive motility, slowing colonic transit and reducing diarrhea. The delta-opioid receptor antagonism is thought to counterbalance the mu-opioid-mediated constipation and to enhance analgesia by modulating the heterodimerization of mu and delta receptors. Eluxadoline reduces abdominal pain and improves stool consistency in IBS-D. The most serious adverse effect is pancreatitis, especially in patients without a gallbladder (the drug increases the tone of the sphincter of Oddi, and the absence of a gallbladder removes the reservoir that buffers the pressure). It is contraindicated in patients who have had a cholecystectomy, those who drink more than 3 alcoholic drinks per day, and those with a history of pancreatitis or sphincter of Oddi dysfunction. It is a Schedule IV controlled substance (mu-opioid activity). [1]
- **Alosetron:** A potent and selective serotonin 5-HT3 receptor antagonist (see Setrons, above). It is indicated for severe, diarrhea-predominant IBS in women only, who have failed conventional therapy. It slows colonic transit, reduces intestinal secretion, and reduces visceral sensitivity. It was voluntarily withdrawn from the market, then reintroduced by the FDA under a very restrictive Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) prescribing program because of its association with severe, life-threatening complications: ischemic colitis (from non-specific vasoconstriction or excessive reduction in motility) and severe constipation with fecal impaction. Its use is now limited to an extremely small, highly selected patient population. [1]

### 5.3 Antispasmodics

- **Hyoscine butylbromide:** An anticholinergic agent that blocks muscarinic M3 receptors on the intestinal smooth muscle, reducing the smooth muscle spasm that causes the colicky abdominal pain of IBS. It has minimal CNS penetration and therefore few anticholinergic CNS adverse effects. Used primarily as an on-demand, oral or sublingual agent for acute pain relief in IBS.
- **Dicyclomine:** A tertiary amine with anticholinergic and direct smooth muscle relaxant effects. Used for IBS pain. It crosses the blood-brain barrier and can cause sedation, confusion, and the anticholinergic syndrome in the elderly.
- **Peppermint oil:** The active component, menthol, is an L-type calcium channel blocker on the intestinal smooth muscle, causing smooth muscle relaxation. It is formulated in enteric-coated capsules to prevent gastric release (which would cause heartburn and reflux from relaxation of the LES) and to deliver it to the small intestine. It is an effective antispasmodic for IBS pain and bloating. [1]

## 6. Antidiarrheal Agents

**Loperamide:**

A synthetic, piperidine derivative, opioid receptor agonist. It is a potent agonist of the mu-opioid receptor in the myenteric plexus of the gut. By activating presynaptic mu-opioid receptors on the cholinergic and VIP-ergic neurons, it hyperpolarises the neurons and inhibits the release of acetylcholine and VIP. This reduces the propulsive peristalsis and increases the segmental, non-propulsive contractions. The stool moves more slowly through the colon, increasing the contact time for water absorption, and the stool becomes firmer and less frequent. It also increases the tone of the internal anal sphincter, which reduces fecal urgency and the sensation of impending incontinence. Loperamide is a P-glycoprotein substrate. Normally, P-glycoprotein in the blood-brain barrier actively pumps loperamide out of the CNS, so it has a minimal central opioid effect and no abuse liability at standard doses (unlike diphenoxylate, which contains atropine to deter abuse). However, in massive overdose or when co-ingested with a P-glycoprotein inhibitor (e.g., quinidine, verapamil), loperamide can cross the blood-brain barrier and cause central opioid effects (euphoria, respiratory depression). [1]

**Bismuth Subsalicylate:** (See Mucosal Protectants).

**Racecadotril:**

An enkephalinase inhibitor. Enkephalins are endogenous opioid peptides (delta-opioid receptor agonists) in the GI tract that inhibit intestinal secretion by reducing cAMP. Enkephalinase is the peptidase that degrades the enkephalins. By inhibiting enkephalinase, racecadotril potentiates the anti-secretory effect of the endogenous enkephalins, reducing watery diarrhea without significantly affecting propulsive motility. It does not cross the blood-brain barrier. It is used for acute watery diarrhea, primarily in children and in Europe.

## 7. Laxatives and Purgatives

**Bulk-forming laxatives (Fiber):** Psyllium, Methylcellulose, Calcium polycarbophil. Mechanism: These are natural or synthetic polysaccharides and cellulose derivatives that are resistant to human digestion. They absorb water in the intestinal lumen, swelling to increase the bulk, volume, and water content of the stool. The increased stool mass distends the colon, which stimulates the intrinsic stretch receptors in the colonic wall, triggering a reflex increase in propulsive peristalsis (the gastrocolic reflex). They are the first-line treatment for chronic constipation. They must be taken with sufficient water (at least 8 oz per dose) or they can cause esophageal or colonic obstruction. Onset: 12-72 hours. Adverse effects: Bloating, flatulence (from the bacterial fermentation of the fiber). [1]

### Osmotic laxatives:

- *Polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350 (MiraLAX)*: A long-chain, high-molecular-weight, non-absorbable polymer that creates an osmotic gradient in the intestinal lumen, retaining water and electrolytes in the stool. It is iso-osmotic (it does not cause net movement of fluid across the gut wall; it simply prevents the absorption of the water it is ingested with). It is very well-tolerated, with no significant bloating or flatulence, and is the safest, most effective first-line osmotic laxative for chronic constipation. Onset: 24-48 hours. [1]
- *Lactulose*: A synthetic, non-absorbable disaccharide of galactose and fructose. It is metabolized by the colonic bacteria to short-chain fatty acids (lactic acid and acetic acid), which acidify the colonic lumen. This lowers the pH, which promotes the conversion of the diffusible, neurotoxic ammonia (NH3) to the non-diffusible ammonium ion (NH4+), trapping ammonia in the lumen and promoting its excretion. This is the mechanism of action in hepatic encephalopathy (not a laxative effect). The osmotic effect of the undigested sugars and the short-chain fatty acids draws water into the colon. It causes significant bloating, cramping, and flatulence. [1]
- *Magnesium salts (Magnesium citrate, Magnesium hydroxide)*: Poorly absorbed ions that create an osmotic gradient, drawing water into the intestinal lumen and stimulating cholecystokinin (CCK) release, which also promotes secretion and motility. They are effective, rapid-acting, and potent. Contraindicated in renal failure (risk of hypermagnesemia). [1]

### Stimulant laxatives:

- *Bisacodyl and Senna (anthraquinones)*: These agents directly stimulate the submucosal (Meissner's) and myenteric (Auerbach's) nerve plexuses of the colon, increasing the secretion of fluid and electrolytes and inducing propulsive peristaltic contractions. Senna is a natural anthraquinone glycoside that must be hydrolyzed by the colonic bacteria to its active form, rhein anthrone. Bisacodyl does not require bacterial activation. Their chronic use at high doses for years (historically, in the setting of eating disorders) causes melanosis coli (a brown-black, benign, granular pigmentation of the colonic mucosa from the accumulation of apoptotic epithelial cells phagocytosed by macrophages) and can lead to the cathartic colon syndrome (a dilated, atonic, non-functioning colon with loss of the myenteric plexus neurons). These drugs are effective and safe for short-term use in resistant constipation. [1]

## 8. Agents for Liver Diseases

### 8.1 Agents for Hepatic Encephalopathy

- **Lactulose:** (See Osmotic Laxatives). Mechanism for hepatic encephalopathy: the acidification of the colonic lumen traps ammonia as NH4+, and the catharsis promotes the elimination of nitrogenous waste. It is the first-line agent for both the treatment of acute hepatic encephalopathy and for the secondary prevention of recurrence. [6]
- **Rifaximin:** (See IBS-D agents). In hepatic encephalopathy, rifaximin reduces the population of the urease-producing, ammonia-generating colonic bacteria. It is FDA-approved as an add-on therapy to lactulose for the maintenance of remission in hepatic encephalopathy in patients who have experienced recurrent episodes. [6]

### 8.2 Agents for Ascites and Volume Overload (Diuretics)

- **Spironolactone:** A potassium-sparing diuretic and a competitive antagonist of the mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) receptor in the distal convoluted tubule and the collecting duct of the nephron. In cirrhosis with ascites, the effective arterial blood volume is reduced (from splanchnic vasodilation), activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. The resultant secondary hyperaldosteronism causes avid renal sodium reabsorption, driving the sodium retention that forms the ascites. Spironolactone directly antagonises this, promoting natriuresis while retaining potassium (counteracting the hypokalemia that furosemide would cause). It is the first-line diuretic for cirrhotic ascites, starting at 100 mg/day, titrated to 400 mg/day. Its adverse effects are the anti-androgenic effects (painful gynecomastia, impotence, decreased libido) from its binding to the androgen receptor, and hyperkalemia. Eplerenone is a more selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist without anti-androgenic effects, but is not approved for ascites. [6]
- **Furosemide:** A loop diuretic, added to spironolactone in a 40:100 mg ratio to augment natriuresis. Used when the ascites is not responsive to spironolactone alone (which is the case in the majority). The combination prevents hypokalemia from furosemide and hyperkalemia from spironolactone, and is more effective. [6]

### 8.3 Agents for Portal Hypertension and Variceal Hemorrhage

- **Non-selective Beta-Blockers (NSBBs): Propranolol, Nadolol, Carvedilol.** Mechanism of Action: These agents block both β1 and β2 adrenergic receptors. The splanchnic circulation contains both β1 and β2 receptors. Blockade of the cardiac β1 receptors reduces cardiac output (negative chronotropy and inotropy). The blockade of the splanchnic β2 receptors (which normally mediate vasodilation) leaves the unopposed vasoconstrictive alpha-1 adrenergic tone, leading to splanchnic vasoconstriction. The combination of reduced cardiac output and splanchnic vasoconstriction results in a significant reduction in the portal venous inflow, and thus a reduction in the portal pressure (hepatic venous pressure gradient, HVPG). Carvedilol is a non-selective beta-blocker with additional anti-alpha-1 adrenergic activity, which further enhances the reduction in the intrahepatic vascular resistance and portal pressure. Carvedilol is more potent than propranolol in reducing the HVPG. NSBBs are the cornerstone of the primary and secondary prophylaxis of esophageal variceal hemorrhage. The dose of propranolol is titrated to achieve a 25% reduction in the resting heart rate or a heart rate of 55-60 bpm. Absolute contraindications include severe asthma, severe peripheral arterial disease, and decompensated cirrhosis with refractory ascites and severe hypotension (the window of tolerance is narrowed; NSBBs can precipitate hepatorenal syndrome in this setting). [6]
- **Octreotide:** A synthetic octapeptide analogue of somatostatin. It is a potent splanchnic vasoconstrictor. It inhibits the release of the vasodilator glucagon from the pancreatic alpha cells. It is administered as an IV bolus followed by a continuous IV infusion during an acute variceal hemorrhage to reduce the portal pressure and control the active bleeding, typically for 2-5 days. [6]
- **Terlipressin:** A synthetic vasopressin analogue (triglycyl-lysine-vasopressin) with potent V1 receptor agonist activity. Activation of V1 receptors on the vascular smooth muscle of the splanchnic circulation causes a profound, sustained splanchnic vasoconstriction, drastically reducing the portal inflow and the portal pressure. It is the most effective vasoactive drug for acute variceal hemorrhage and is also the first-line treatment for hepatorenal syndrome (HRS), where its splanchnic vasoconstriction redistributes blood volume to the systemic circulation, improving renal perfusion. The major adverse effect is ischemia (vasoconstriction in the coronary, mesenteric, and peripheral vascular beds), so it is contraindicated in significant ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, and mesenteric ischemia. [6]

### 8.4 Agents for Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)

- **Terlipressin** (see above) is combined with intravenous 25% albumin. The albumin expands the central blood volume and antagonises the systemic vasodilation, while terlipressin constricts the massively dilated splanchnic circulation, thus shifting blood from the splanchnic bed back to the systemic and renal circulations, improving the effective arterial blood volume and renal perfusion. [6]

## 8.5 Agents for Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC)

- **Ursodeoxycholic Acid (UDCA):** A naturally occurring, non-toxic, hydrophilic, tertiary bile acid. It constitutes only 3% of the normal human bile acid pool. When administered at a dose of 13-15 mg/kg/day, it becomes the predominant biliary bile acid. Its mechanisms of action are multifaceted: (1) It displaces the toxic, hydrophobic, endogenous, retained bile acids (chenodeoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid) from the hepatocyte and cholangiocyte membranes, protecting the cells from the detergent, membrane-dissolving, apoptosis-inducing effect. (2) It stimulates the hepatobiliary secretion of the retained toxic bile acids and their elimination. (3) It stabilizes the cholangiocyte and hepatocyte mitochondrial and cell membranes. (4) It has immunomodulatory effects (reduces the aberrant HLA class I expression on the cholangiocytes). UDCA is the first-line therapy for PBC. It normalizes the serum alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin, and prolongs the transplant-free survival, but only in patients who achieve a biochemical response (e.g., ALP < 1.67 x ULN, and normal bilirubin). Approximately 30-40% of patients have an incomplete response. [6]
- **Obeticholic Acid (OCA):** A semi-synthetic, modified bile acid, which is a potent, selective agonist of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), the nuclear receptor for bile acids. FXR is the master regulator of bile acid homeostasis. Activation of FXR in the hepatocyte suppresses bile acid synthesis (by inducing the small heterodimer partner SHP, which inhibits CYP7A1, the rate-limiting enzyme in bile acid synthesis), and simultaneously stimulates the secretion of bile acids and the hepatoprotective effects. OCA is approved as a second-line agent for PBC patients with an incomplete response to UDCA, or who are intolerant of UDCA. Its dose-limiting adverse effect is dose-dependent, severe, intractable pruritus. [6]

### 8.6 Agents for Hepatitis B

- **Nucleos(t)ide Analogues:** Entecavir, Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF). These are oral prodrugs that are taken up by the hepatocyte and are converted by intracellular phosphorylation to their active triphosphate metabolites. The active triphosphates compete with the natural nucleoside substrates at the active site of the HBV DNA polymerase (reverse transcriptase). Once incorporated into the growing viral DNA strand, they act as obligate chain terminators (entecavir and tenofovir) by lacking the 3'-OH group required for the attachment of the next nucleotide. This irreversibly blocks the viral DNA synthesis. They are highly potent, have a high genetic barrier to resistance (particularly entecavir and tenofovir), and are very well tolerated. They can suppress the viral load to undetectable levels, normalize the liver enzymes, and prevent the progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, but they cannot eradicate the virus (the covalently closed circular DNA [cccDNA] persists in the hepatocyte nucleus) and thus require lifelong therapy in most patients. TAF is a newer prodrug of tenofovir that achieves higher intracellular concentrations of the active metabolite with significantly lower systemic plasma levels, thus reducing the renal and bone toxicity (Fanconi syndrome, osteoporosis) associated with long-term TDF. [6]
- **Pegylated Interferon-alpha (PEG-IFN-α):** A finite, 48-week course of immunomodulatory therapy. It stimulates the innate and adaptive immune response against HBV. It is associated with substantial adverse effects (flu-like syndrome, depression, myelosuppression) and has strict eligibility criteria (compensated liver disease, low viral load, high ALT), but can achieve a durable, off-treatment seroconversion (HBeAg loss, and in a minority, HBsAg loss, which is a functional cure), which the NAs cannot. [6]

### 8.7 Agents for Hepatitis C (Direct-Acting Antivirals, DAAs)

DAAs are oral, small-molecule inhibitors that target specific non-structural (NS) proteins of the hepatitis C virus (HCV) that are essential for viral replication. They are combined in single-tablet, fixed-dose, once-daily regimens for 8-12 weeks. The standard regimens are pan-genotypic and achieve a sustained virologic response (SVR = cure) in >95% of patients. The viral genome does not integrate into the host genome, and there is no cellular reservoir. SVR means the virus is eradicated from the body. The classes are:

- **NS5A Inhibitors (Ledipasvir, Velpatasvir, Pibrentasvir, Elbasvir):** NS5A is a viral phosphoprotein essential for viral RNA replication and assembly. The NS5A inhibitor binds to domain I of the NS5A dimer, blocking its function.
- **NS5B Polymerase Inhibitors (Sofosbuvir, Dasabuvir):** NS5B is the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Sofosbuvir is a uridine nucleotide prodrug that, after intracellular activation to its triphosphate, competes with uridine triphosphate for incorporation, and acts as a non-obligate chain terminator.
- **NS3/4A Protease Inhibitors (Glecaprevir, Grazoprevir, Voxilaprevir):** The NS3/4A protease cleaves the viral polyprotein into functional viral proteins. The protease inhibitor binds to the active site of the serine protease, preventing this cleavage. Decompensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh B or C) is an absolute contraindication to protease inhibitors because they are highly protein-bound and hepatically metabolized; their concentrations are dangerously increased in advanced liver disease, causing hepatotoxicity. [6]

### 8.8 Agents for Alcoholic Hepatitis

- **Prednisolone** (or Prednisone) is used for severe acute alcoholic hepatitis (defined by the Maddrey's Discriminant Function >32 or a MELD score >20). It is the only pharmacological agent shown to improve 28-day survival in this select group. It must be withheld if there is active, untreated infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, or acute kidney injury. A 7-day assessment of response (Lille score) is used to determine whether to stop (if futility is predicted) or continue for 28 days. [6]

### 8.9 Agents for Non-Alcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH)

- **Resmetirom:** The first and only FDA-approved drug (approved March 2024) for the treatment of NASH with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis (stage F2-F3). It is a once-daily, oral, liver-targeted, selective thyroid hormone receptor-beta (THR-β) agonist. THR-β is the predominant thyroid hormone receptor in the liver. Activation of THR-β promotes the hepatic beta-oxidation of fatty acids, reduces hepatic de novo lipogenesis, and reduces the toxic lipotoxicity and oxidative stress that drive hepatocyte injury, inflammation, and fibrosis. In the pivotal MAESTRO-NASH trial, resmetirom achieved NASH resolution with no worsening of fibrosis, and fibrosis improvement with no worsening of NASH, compared to placebo, and improved the LDL-cholesterol levels. It is an epochal advance for a disease that previously had no approved therapy. [7]

## 8.10 Agents for Wilson Disease

- **Zinc Acetate:** Oral zinc blocks the absorption of dietary copper from the GI tract by inducing the synthesis of metallothionein in the enterocyte. Metallothionein is a cysteine-rich intracellular protein that preferentially binds copper. The copper-metallothionein complex is trapped in the enterocyte, and the copper is not absorbed into the portal blood. The enterocyte is then sloughed into the stool. Zinc is a maintenance therapy; its onset of action is slow (weeks) because it requires the induction of a new protein. [6]
- **D-Penicillamine:** A chelating agent that binds copper, forming a stable, soluble complex that is excreted in the urine. It is used for the initial decoppering of symptomatic Wilson disease patients. Its use is limited by severe adverse effects: fever, rash, proteinuria (membranous glomerulonephritis), bone marrow suppression, and an autoimmune syndrome (myasthenia gravis, systemic lupus erythematosus, pemphigus). [6]
- **Trientine:** An alternative chelator with a better safety profile than penicillamine. [6]

### 8.11 Agents for Autoimmune Hepatitis

- **Prednisone/Prednisolone** (for induction) and **Azathioprine** (for steroid-sparing maintenance) are the standard-of-care. Budesonide is an alternative for non-cirrhotic patients. [6]

### 8.12 Agents for Pruritus of Cholestasis

- **Cholestyramine:** An insoluble, non-absorbed, quaternary ammonium anion-exchange resin. It binds the negatively charged bile acids in the intestinal lumen, preventing their reabsorption by the ileal sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT) and promoting their fecal excretion. This depletes the systemic bile acid pool. It is the first-line agent for cholestatic pruritus. It must be taken separately (by 4 hours) from all other medications (including UDCA) because it non-specifically binds and prevents the absorption of many drugs. [6]
- **Rifampicin:** A potent, second-line agent. Its antipruritic mechanism is pleiotropic and not fully defined but includes: the induction of hepatic CYP3A4, which accelerates the metabolism and elimination of the endogenous pruritogens, and competitive antagonism of the bile acid-activated TGR5 receptor on the sensory neurons. [6]

## 9. Agents for Pancreatic Diseases

### 9.1 Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT)

- **Pancrelipase:** A porcine-derived extract of pancreatic enzymes, containing lipase, amylase, and protease in a concentrated form. It is formulated as enteric-coated, pH-sensitive microspheres or microtablets that are protected from gastric acid (the coating dissolves at pH >5.5), releasing the active enzymes in the alkaline pH of the duodenum, where they mix with chyme and directly restore the intraluminal digestive capacity. The dose is expressed in units of lipase. The primary clinical goal is the elimination of steatorrhea and the restoration of normal nutritional status. PERT is the treatment for the exocrine pancreatic insufficiency of chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, and after a total pancreatectomy. The lipase is the most labile enzyme in the acid environment, and the dose is titrated to the stool fat content. [1,3]

### 9.2 Somatostatin Analogues (Octreotide)

- **Octreotide:** (See Variceal Hemorrhage). In the pancreas, it is used to suppress the secretions of enterocutaneous and pancreatic fistulas and to treat the severe secretory diarrhea of VIPomas and carcinoid syndrome. [6]

## 10. Agents for Biliary Diseases

### 10.1 Ursodeoxycholic Acid (UDCA)

(See PBC). UDCA is also used to dissolve small, radiolucent, cholesterol gallstones in patients who are not surgical candidates. It slowly reduces the cholesterol saturation of the bile, creating an environment that allows the cholesterol to be released from the stone surface. This process takes 6-24 months and is only effective for small (<1 cm), radiolucent stones in a functioning gallbladder. [1]

## 11. Agents for Helicobacter pylori Eradication

H. pylori therapy always consists of a combination of a potent acid suppressant (PPI) and 2-3 antimicrobials. The PPI is essential to raise the gastric pH to >6.0; this promotes H. pylori's replicative state (making it susceptible to cell wall-active and protein synthesis-inhibiting antibiotics) and stabilizes the acid-labile antibiotics. The recommended first-line regimens, per the 2024 ACG guideline, are: [8]

- **Bismuth Quadruple Therapy (BQT):** A 14-day regimen of PPI (standard dose BID) + Bismuth Subsalicylate (524 mg QID) + Tetracycline (500 mg QID) + Metronidazole (500 mg TID or QID). This is the preferred first-line therapy in areas of high clarithromycin resistance (>15%).
- **Clarithromycin Triple Therapy:** A 14-day regimen of PPI BID + Clarithromycin 500 mg BID + Amoxicillin 1 g BID (or Metronidazole 500 mg BID if penicillin-allergic). This is only recommended in regions where the clarithromycin resistance rate is known to be <15%. This regimen has lost efficacy globally due to rising clarithromycin resistance, driven by point mutations in the 23S rRNA gene of H. pylori, which prevent the drug from binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit.
- **Potassium-Competitive Acid Blocker (P-CAB)-based regimens:** Vonoprazan-containing dual therapy or triple therapy, showing superior eradication rates to PPI-based regimens, regardless of clarithromycin resistance status.

## 12. Agents for Clostridioides difficile Infection

- **Fidaxomicin:** A narrow-spectrum, bactericidal, macrocyclic antibiotic. It inhibits the bacterial RNA polymerase at a site distinct from that of rifamycins. Its critical clinical advantage is its very narrow spectrum: it is highly active against C. difficile, but it spares the commensal Gram-negative and many anaerobic organisms of the healthy colonic microbiome. This reduces the recurrence rate of C. difficile colitis by 50% compared to vancomycin. It is the preferred first-line agent for an initial C. difficile episode, particularly in patients at high risk for recurrence. [9]
- **Oral Vancomycin:** A glycopeptide antibiotic that is not absorbed from the GI tract when given orally (unlike the IV form). It binds to the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the peptidoglycan precursors in the cell wall of C. difficile, blocking the cell wall synthesis. It is highly effective. It is the first-line agent for fulminant C. difficile colitis (megacolon, ileus, shock) where it is given at a higher dose (500 mg PO or per NG tube four times daily) combined with IV metronidazole. [9]
- **Bezlotoxumab:** A fully human monoclonal antibody that binds and neutralizes C. difficile toxin B (TcdB). Toxin B is the primary virulence factor that causes the actin cytoskeleton disruption, cell rounding, apoptosis, and the intense colonic inflammation and diarrhea. Bezlotoxumab provides passive immunity against the toxin. It is administered as a single, one-hour IV infusion during the antibiotic treatment course to reduce the risk of recurrent C. difficile infection in high-risk patients. [9]
- **Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT):** The instillation of processed stool from a rigorously screened, healthy donor into the intestinal tract of the patient to restore the phylogenetic diversity and the colonization resistance of the healthy gut microbiome. It is the most effective therapy for the prevention of recurrent C. difficile infection (cure rates >90%), and is reserved for patients with two or more recurrences after adequate antibiotic therapy. It is also available as FDA-approved, standardized live fecal microbiota products. [9]

## 13. Miscellaneous Agents

### 13.1 Probiotics

Live microorganisms (bacteria or yeasts) that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit. The best-supported evidence in GI disease is for the use of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, Saccharomyces boulardii, and specific multi-strain formulations for the prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and for S. boulardii as an adjunct in the treatment of recurrent C. difficile. The evidence for probiotics in IBS, IBD, and functional dyspepsia is mixed and highly strain-specific. [1]

### 13.2 Antiflatulents

- **Simethicone:** A mixture of polydimethylsiloxane and hydrated silica gel. It is a surfactant that reduces the surface tension of the mucus-covered gas bubbles in the gut, allowing the smaller bubbles to coalesce into larger bubbles that can be expelled more easily by belching or flatus. It is widely used, though the evidence for its efficacy is modest. [1]
