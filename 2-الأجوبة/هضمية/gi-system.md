## First: Symptoms of the Gastrointestinal System

**Definition of a Symptom in GI Disease**

A gastrointestinal symptom is any subjective sensation or complaint originating from the alimentary tract (from mouth to anus) or its accessory organs (liver, gallbladder, pancreas) that a patient experiences and reports. These symptoms arise from disruption of the normal physiological functions of the GI system: motility, secretion, digestion, absorption, excretion, and visceral sensation.

- **Dysphagia**: Difficulty swallowing. Pathogenesis: Results from either oropharyngeal dysfunction (transfer dysphagia from neuromuscular disorders affecting the striated muscle of the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter, e.g., myasthenia gravis, stroke) or esophageal dysfunction (transport dysphagia from mechanical obstruction like carcinoma, stricture, or Schatzki ring; or motor disorders like achalasia where loss of inhibitory nitrergic neurons in the myenteric plexus prevents lower esophageal sphincter relaxation and abolishes peristalsis). The sensation of food "sticking" is due to stimulation of esophageal mechanoreceptors by a bolus that fails to traverse the lumen normally.[1]
- **Odynophagia**: Painful swallowing. Pathogenesis: Sharp, burning pain during swallowing indicates mucosal inflammation or ulceration. In infectious esophagitis (Candida, HSV, CMV), the organisms invade the squamous epithelium causing an inflammatory response with release of prostaglandins and bradykinin that sensitize nociceptors. In pill-induced esophagitis, prolonged contact of a caustic medication with the esophageal mucosa causes direct chemical injury and necrosis.[1]
- **Globus Sensation**: A persistent, non-painful feeling of a lump, retained food bolus, or tightness in the throat between meals. Pathogenesis: The exact mechanism is unclear but is linked to cricopharyngeal spasm, gastroesophageal reflux causing irritation of the pharyngeal mucosa with referred sensation, and hypervigilance of upper esophageal sphincter pressure. It is a functional disorder in most cases, with increased tone in the upper esophageal sphincter sensed as a lump. It is critical to note that the sensation is *relieved* by eating (unlike dysphagia for solids) because swallowing relaxes the cricopharyngeus. Anxiety amplifies perception of normal pharyngeal sensations.[1]
- **Heartburn (Pyrosis)**: A retrosternal burning sensation radiating upward from the epigastrium toward the neck. Pathogenesis: The primary mechanism is the reflux of acidic gastric contents (pH < 4) into the lower esophagus due to transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations (TLESRs), a hypotensive LES, or disruption of the esophagogastric junction (as in hiatal hernia). Gastric acid and pepsin diffuse across the esophageal stratified squamous epithelium, which lacks a protective mucus layer, directly injuring cells in the deeper layers of the epithelium. Acid stimulates chemosensitive nociceptors on pain-sensing afferent fibers (via TRPV1 and ASIC channels), producing the characteristic burning pain. The radiation pattern follows the dermatomes of the upper thoracic spinal nerves that also supply the esophagus. The symptom is often worse after meals (gastric distension triggers TLESRs), when supine (loss of gravitational clearance), and with increased intra-abdominal pressure (bending, lifting, obesity, pregnancy).[1]
- **Dyspepsia**: A complex of symptoms localized to the gastroduodenal region, including epigastric pain or burning, postprandial fullness, and early satiety. Pathogenesis: Epigastric pain arises from stimulation of visceral afferent nerves in the gastric and duodenal wall by acid, inflammation (gastritis, peptic ulcer), or abnormal sensitivity (functional dyspepsia where there is visceral hypersensitivity and impaired gastric accommodation to a meal, causing the fundus to fail to relax, which raises intragastric pressure and causes early satiety). Postprandial fullness results from delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) due to vagal neuropathy, loss of interstitial cells of Cajal (the gastric pacemaker cells), or pylorospasm. In functional dyspepsia, the duodenum is hypersensitive to acid and lipids, triggering abnormal reflexes that delay gastric emptying and increase gastric sensation.[1]
- **Nausea**: An unpleasant, subjective sensation of an imminent urge to vomit, felt in the epigastrium and throat. Pathogenesis: Nausea is generated by the brain, specifically the medullary vomiting center (area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius), which receives convergent inputs from: (1) Vagal afferents from the GI tract distension or inflammation (serotonin release from enterochromaffin cells activating 5-HT3 receptors on vagal endings), (2) Vestibular system input via histamine H1 and muscarinic M1 receptors in motion sickness, (3) The area postrema chemoreceptor trigger zone (outside the blood-brain barrier) detecting emetogenic toxins, drugs, and metabolic derangements via dopamine D2 and 5-HT3 receptors, (4) Higher cortical centers (fear, pain, sight, smell). The feeling of nausea is the conscious perception of the autonomic prodrome of vomiting: increased salivation, gastric relaxation, and retrograde giant contractions of the small intestine.[1]
- **Vomiting (Emesis)**: The forceful, involuntary expulsion of gastric contents through the mouth. Pathogenesis: The motor act of vomiting is a stereotyped, brainstem-mediated reflex. The vomiting center in the lateral medullary reticular formation coordinates the following sequence: (1) Nausea prodrome with hypersalivation and tachycardia, (2) Retching: rhythmic, synchronous contractions of the diaphragm, abdominal muscles, and external intercostals against a *closed* glottis. The antrum of the stomach contracts but the fundus and lower esophageal sphincter are relaxed, causing the gastric contents to oscillate between stomach and esophagus, (3) Expulsion: sustained contraction of abdominal and diaphragmatic muscles with sudden descent of the diaphragm, the glottis opens, the soft palate elevates sealing the nasopharynx, and gastric contents are forcefully expelled. The motor neurons are activated by the nucleus tractus solitarius. Key neurotransmitters include substance P (acting on NK1 receptors), serotonin (5-HT3), and dopamine (D2). Causes include GI obstruction (projectile vomiting from pyloric stenosis in infants), toxins (staphylococcal enterotoxin stimulates vagal afferents directly), and increased intracranial pressure (direct pressure on the medullary vomiting center causing projectile, non-nauseated vomiting).[1]
- **Regurgitation**: The effortless return of esophageal or gastric contents into the mouth without the forceful muscular contractions of vomiting. Pathogenesis: In gastroesophageal reflux disease, regurgitation results from a hypotensive lower esophageal sphincter allowing gastric contents to flow retrogradely up the esophagus and into the pharynx, particularly when the patient bends over or lies flat (loss of gravitational advantage). In achalasia, regurgitation is of undigested food and saliva that has accumulated in the dilated, aperistaltic esophagus. In rumination syndrome, the patient voluntarily (but often subconsciously) contracts the abdominal muscles, raising intragastric pressure and returning recently ingested food to the mouth for re-chewing. Zenker's diverticulum causes regurgitation of undigested food hours after eating, due to food trapped in the pharyngeal pouch.[1]
- **Rumination**: Repeated, effortless regurgitation of recently ingested food into the mouth, followed by re-chewing and re-swallowing or spitting out. Pathogenesis: This is a learned, habitual behavior and a functional gastroduodenal disorder. The mechanism is a voluntary (but often unconscious) contraction of the abdominal wall muscles that increases intragastric pressure, overcoming the lower esophageal sphincter resistance. Unlike GERD, there is no heartburn and the regurgitated food is not acidic. It is not preceded by nausea or retching, distinguishing it from vomiting. The behavior is thought to be a conditioned response to relieve an uncomfortable sensation of postprandial fullness.[1]
- **Hematemesis**: Vomiting of blood. Pathogenesis: Bleeding from any site proximal to the ligament of Treitz (upper GI tract: esophagus, stomach, duodenum). The appearance depends on the rate of bleeding and the gastric transit time: (1) *Bright red blood*: active, rapid, or large-volume bleeding that has not had time to be altered by gastric acid, (2) *Coffee-ground emesis*: blood that has been in the stomach for a period sufficient for hemoglobin to be oxidized by gastric acid and pepsin to hematin, which is brown-black and granular (this indicates slower bleeding or bleeding that has stopped). The most common causes are peptic ulcer disease (acid-peptic digestion of a submucosal artery), esophageal or gastric varices (portal hypertension causing high-pressure flow through thin-walled submucosal veins), Mallory-Weiss tears (mucosal lacerations at the gastroesophageal junction caused by violent retching), and erosive gastritis.[1]
- **Melena**: The passage of black, tarry, foul-smelling stool containing blood that has been altered by digestive enzymes and bacteria. Pathogenesis: Melena requires approximately 50-100 mL of blood to be produced. Blood in the GI lumen is broken down by intestinal and bacterial enzymes: hemoglobin is oxidized to hematin and other dark pigments, which give the characteristic black color. The tarry consistency comes from the digested blood products. Melena indicates an upper GI source (proximal to the ligament of Treitz) as it requires sufficient transit time for bacterial degradation. However, slow bleeding from the right colon can occasionally produce melena. It is pathognomonic for upper GI bleeding and persists for several days after bleeding has ceased as the altered blood is slowly evacuated.[1]
- **Hematochezia**: The passage of bright red or maroon blood per rectum. Pathogenesis: Bright red blood indicates a lower GI source (distal to the ligament of Treitz) because the blood has not been exposed to digestive enzymes or bacteria long enough to be degraded. Maroon or dark red blood suggests a more proximal colonic source (right colon) or a massive upper GI hemorrhage with rapid transit (the brisk bleed produces a cathartic effect, accelerating colonic transit so the blood is passed before it can turn black). Common causes include diverticulosis (rupture of a vasa recta at the neck of a diverticulum), angiodysplasia (thin-walled, ectatic submucosal veins in the cecum and right colon that bleed spontaneously), colorectal carcinoma, and ischemic colitis.[1]
- **Occult GI Bleeding**: The asymptomatic passage of microscopic amounts of blood in the stool, detectable only by chemical tests (fecal occult blood test or fecal immunochemical test). Pathogenesis: Slow, chronic blood loss from any lesion, most commonly colorectal adenomas and carcinomas (the abnormal vascularity and surface friability of these neoplasms causes continuous small-volume blood loss), angiodysplasia, or esophagitis/gastritis from chronic NSAID use. The occult blood loss over years leads to iron deficiency anemia.[1]
- **Abdominal Pain**: The subjective experience of noxious sensation originating from intra-abdominal structures. Pathogenesis: Abdominal pain is categorized into three types by neural pathway:
  - *Visceral pain*: Originates from stimulation of nociceptors in the walls of hollow viscera or the capsules of solid organs. Stimuli include distension (stretching mechanoreceptors), inflammation (chemical mediators like prostaglandins, bradykinin, substance P sensitizing nociceptors), and ischemia (tissue acidosis and lactate accumulation). Visceral pain is transmitted by unmyelinated C fibers that enter the spinal cord bilaterally at multiple levels. Consequently, visceral pain is poorly localized, described as dull, crampy, aching, or gnawing, and felt in the midline (because the viscera are innervated symmetrically). The foregut (stomach, duodenum, liver, pancreas, gallbladder) refers to the epigastrium (T5-T9). The midgut (small bowel, appendix, right colon) refers to the periumbilical region (T10). The hindgut (left colon, sigmoid, rectum) refers to the suprapubic region (T11-L1).
  - *Parietal (somatic) pain*: Originates from irritation of the parietal peritoneum (which covers the abdominal wall and diaphragm). The parietal peritoneum is innervated by myelinated somatic sensory fibers (the intercostal nerves and phrenic nerve) that enter the spinal cord at a single, unilateral spinal cord level. Parietal pain is therefore sharp, knifelike, precisely localized over the affected area, and aggravated by movement (which stretches the peritoneum). The classic example is the localized right lower quadrant pain of appendicitis when the inflamed appendix contacts the parietal peritoneum.
  - *Referred pain*: Pain perceived at a site distant from the diseased organ because the visceral afferent nerve enters the spinal cord at the same level as somatic sensory nerves from that remote cutaneous or muscular site. Convergence of these inputs onto spinothalamic tract neurons causes the brain to misattribute the visceral signal to the somatic region. Examples: diaphragmatic irritation (from splenic rupture, liver abscess, subphrenic abscess) referred to the shoulder tip (C3-C5, phrenic nerve); biliary disease referred to the right scapula (T7-T8); myocardial infarction referred to the epigastrium.[1]
- **Constipation**: Infrequent, difficult, or incomplete defecation. Pathogenesis: Constipation is classified as:
  - *Slow-transit constipation*: A colonic motor disorder where there is a reduction in the frequency of high-amplitude propagating contractions (mass movements) that normally propel stool from the right colon to the rectum. The underlying pathology includes a loss of or damage to the myenteric plexus neurons (which contain substance P and acetylcholine) and depletion of interstitial cells of Cajal (the pacemaker cells that generate the slow waves for smooth muscle contraction). The stool therefore remains in the proximal colon for a prolonged period, allowing excessive fluid absorption and leading to hard, dry stool.
  - *Dyssynergic defecation (outlet dysfunction / pelvic floor dysfunction)*: The patient has a normal urge to defecate but is unable to expel stool because of paradoxical contraction (or failure to relax) of the puborectalis muscle and external anal sphincter during attempted defecation. In health, defecation involves relaxation of the puborectalis (straightening the anorectal angle) and external anal sphincter. In dyssynergic defecation, these muscles contract, closing the anorectal angle and anal canal. This is a learned, functional disorder, often triggered by painful defecation in childhood or chronic avoidance of the defecation urge.[1]
- **Diarrhea**: An increase in stool water content leading to a stool weight >200 g/day combined with an increase in defecation frequency (>3 bowel movements per day) and/or a decrease in stool consistency (liquid). Pathogenesis is classified into four mechanisms:
  - *Osmotic diarrhea*: Ingestion of a poorly absorbed solute that remains in the intestinal lumen and osmotically retains water. The osmotic gap (measured as 290 - 2 × [stool Na + stool K]) is >125 mOsm/kg. The carbohydrate lactulose is a therapeutic example. Congenital lactase deficiency causes osmotic diarrhea after milk ingestion because undigested lactose remains in the lumen. Osmotic diarrhea ceases when the patient stops ingesting the offending substance.
  - *Secretory diarrhea*: Abnormal net secretion of fluid and electrolytes from the intestinal mucosa. The intestinal crypt cells actively secrete chloride (and thus water follows) due to stimulation by bacterial enterotoxins (Vibrio cholerae's cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates the Gsα protein, locking adenylate cyclase in the "on" state, causing supraphysiological cAMP levels that open the CFTR chloride channel; E. coli heat-stable toxin activates guanylate cyclase, increasing cGMP). Secretory diarrhea persists during fasting, has an osmotic gap <50 mOsm/kg, and the stool volume is typically >1 L/day.
  - *Inflammatory diarrhea*: Mucosal damage from infection, ischemia, or autoimmune disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis). The inflamed mucosa exudes blood, pus, and protein-rich fluid into the lumen. Inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, histamine) stimulate intestinal secretion and motility, further contributing. The stool contains leukocytes, blood (fecal lactoferrin positive), and is typically small in volume but frequent (tenesmus from rectal inflammation).
  - *Dysmotility diarrhea*: Increased intestinal transit due to hypermotility (e.g., hyperthyroidism, irritable bowel syndrome) reduces the contact time for absorption, delivering a large volume of fluid to the colon that overwhelms its absorptive capacity. Alternatively, hypomotility (scleroderma, diabetic enteropathy) permits small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), where bacteria deconjugate bile acids that stimulate colonic secretion and cause steatorrhea.[1]
- **Steatorrhea**: The passage of pale, greasy, foul-smelling, bulky stools that are difficult to flush (they float and leave an oil slick). Pathogenesis: Steatorrhea represents fat malabsorption from any cause: (1) Intraluminal maldigestion from pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (lack of pancreatic lipase; the most common cause, with steatorrhea occurring when >90% of the exocrine pancreas is destroyed as in chronic pancreatitis or cystic fibrosis), (2) Inadequate micelle formation from biliary obstruction or cholestatic liver disease (lack of bile salts to emulsify fat), (3) Small intestinal mucosal disease (celiac disease abrading villi, Crohn's disease, Whipple disease) preventing absorption of fatty acids and monoglycerides, (4) Lymphatic obstruction (lymphoma, lymphangiectasia) preventing transport of absorbed chylomicrons.[1]
- **Tenesmus**: A constant, painful sensation of incomplete rectal emptying with an urgent need to defecate, but producing only small amounts of stool, blood, or mucus. Pathogenesis: Tenesmus results from inflammation of the rectal mucosa and the internal anal sphincter complex. The inflamed rectum stimulates the defecation reflex (rectal distension triggers relaxation of the internal anal sphincter and the conscious urge to defecate), even when the rectum contains only a small amount of inflammatory exudate, pus, or blood. This is the classic symptom of proctitis (ulcerative colitis, radiation proctitis, infectious proctitis such as gonorrhea or chlamydia) and rectal carcinoma.[1]
- **Fecal Incontinence**: The involuntary loss of gas, liquid, or solid stool. Pathogenesis: Continence is maintained by a complex anatomical apparatus: (1) the puborectalis muscle sling, which maintains the anorectal angle; (2) the internal anal sphincter (a continuation of the circular smooth muscle of the rectum, under autonomic control, responsible for 70-80% of resting anal tone); (3) the external anal sphincter (striated muscle under voluntary control, responsible for squeeze pressure during acute urgency); (4) the anal cushions (hemorrhoidal plexuses) that provide a fine, watertight seal. Disruption of any component causes incontinence: obstetric trauma (third- or fourth-degree perineal tears dividing the external anal sphincter), diabetic neuropathy (impairing internal sphincter autonomic tone), spinal cord injury or cauda equina syndrome (abolishing sensation and motor control), fecal impaction with overflow (liquid stool bypasses the hard fecal mass obstructing the rectum), and radiation proctitis (fibrosis and stiffening of the rectal wall).[1]
- **Jaundice (Icterus)**: Yellow-green discoloration of the skin, sclerae, and mucous membranes due to the deposition of bilirubin. Pathogenesis: Bilirubin is a breakdown product of heme from senescent red blood cells (85%) and ineffective erythropoiesis (15%). Normal total serum bilirubin is <1.0 mg/dL (17 µmol/L). Hyperbilirubinemia becomes clinically visible (jaundice) at levels >2.5-3.0 mg/dL. The mechanisms are: (1) *Pre-hepatic (hemolytic)*: Massive hemolysis (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, G6PD deficiency crisis, sickle cell disease, spherocytosis) overwhelms the normal hepatocyte's capacity to conjugate bilirubin; the unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin is elevated, and it is largely bound to albumin so it does not cross the glomerulus (acholuric jaundice). (2) *Hepatic*: Hepatocellular injury (viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, cirrhosis) disrupts all three steps of bilirubin metabolism: uptake at the sinusoidal membrane, conjugation by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1) in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, and excretion at the canalicular membrane (the rate-limiting step which is most sensitive to injury). Both conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin are elevated. (3) *Post-hepatic (cholestatic/obstructive)*: Obstruction of the biliary tree (choledocholithiasis, pancreatic head carcinoma, primary sclerosing cholangitis) prevents the entry of already-conjugated bilirubin into the duodenum. Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and is filtered by the glomerulus, causing dark (tea- or cola-colored) urine. The stool lacks bilirubin breakdown products (urobilinogen and stercobilin), resulting in pale, clay-colored (acholic) stools. Because bile salts are also absent from the intestine, fat malabsorption and steatorrhea occur, and the retention of bile salts in the skin causes pruritus.[1]
- **Pruritus**: Generalized itching without primary skin rash (in GI context). Pathogenesis: Pruritus is a cardinal symptom of cholestasis (intrahepatic or extrahepatic). The retained substance that causes pruritus is not bilirubin, but a pruritogen normally excreted in bile. The leading candidates are: (1) Bile acids: elevated serum and tissue bile acid levels activate TGR5 receptors on sensory neurons and release autotaxin, an enzyme that generates lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), a potent pruritogen. (2) Endogenous opioids: cholestasis increases plasma levels of endogenous opioid peptides (methionine enkephalin) which act on central mu-opioid receptors to cause itching. The pruritus is typically most severe on the palms and soles (acral), worse at night, and relieved by mu-opioid antagonists (naloxone) or bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine).[1]
- **Dysgeusia**: A distorted or abnormal sense of taste, often described as a metallic or bitter taste. Pathogenesis: In GI disease, dysgeusia is most commonly associated with GERD, where acid reflux reaches the oropharynx and directly damages taste buds on the tongue. Hepatic disease (cirrhosis, acute hepatitis) can cause dysgeusia due to altered metabolism of taste-active amino acids and other metabolites. Gastritis from Helicobacter pylori is associated with dysgeusia, possibly from bacterial urease producing ammonia that alters oral pH and taste receptor function. It is also a common side effect of many medications (metronidazole, proton pump inhibitors, captopril) that alter the composition of saliva or directly stimulate bitter taste receptors.[1]
- **Halitosis**: Foul-smelling breath. GI-related causes include: Zenker's diverticulum (retained food particles decompose in the diverticulum, producing volatile sulfur compounds by bacterial action), achalasia (stasis and fermentation of retained esophageal contents), gastroparesis (fermentation of gastric contents), severe GERD, and Helicobacter pylori infection (the bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide and methyl mercaptan). Most halitosis originates from the oral cavity, not the GI tract.[1]
- **Sialorrhea (Hypersalivation)**: Excessive production or inability to clear oral saliva, causing drooling. In the GI context, sialorrhea occurs in achalasia (the esophagus cannot clear swallowed saliva, which pools in the dilated esophageal body and eventually overflows into the pharynx), esophageal stricture or carcinoma (obstruction prevents saliva passage), and as a prominent reflex in severe GERD (acid in the distal esophagus triggers an esophagosalivary reflex via vagal afferents, which stimulates the salivary nuclei in the brainstem to produce copious, watery, bicarbonate-rich saliva to neutralize the acid; clinically, this is called water brash).[1]
- **Water Brash**: A specific symptom of GERD characterized by the sudden, unpleasant filling of the mouth with clear, salty, watery fluid. Pathogenesis: This fluid is not regurgitated gastric contents; it is reflexively secreted saliva. Acid reflux into the distal esophagus stimulates vagal afferent nerve endings. The afferent signal travels to the nucleus tractus solitarius, which triggers a parasympathetic efferent (via the facial and glossopharyngeal nerves) to the salivary glands, causing a rapid, high-volume secretion of serous saliva (rich in bicarbonate). This is the esophagosalivary reflex, a protective mechanism to dilute and buffer the esophageal acid. The symptom is highly specific for GERD.[1]
- **Anorexia**: Loss of appetite or the desire to eat. Pathogenesis: Anorexia is a non-specific symptom with GI and systemic causes. In gastric carcinoma, the tumor produces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) that act directly on the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, stimulating the POMC/CART anorexigenic pathway and suppressing the NPY/AgRP orexigenic pathway (the same mechanism as cancer cachexia-anorexia syndrome). In chronic liver disease, elevated circulating levels of tryptophan (not cleared by the failing liver) cross the blood-brain barrier and are converted to serotonin in the hypothalamus, which suppresses appetite. In gastroparesis, gastric distension signals via vagal mechanoreceptors to the nucleus tractus solitarius, triggering satiety. Rapid, profound anorexia with aversion to meat is a classic clinical clue for gastric carcinoma.[1]
- **Early Satiety**: The inability to finish a normal-sized meal because of an early sensation of fullness. Pathogenesis: Early satiety indicates impaired gastric accommodation. Normally, when food enters the stomach, the gastric fundus relaxes (receptive relaxation and accommodation) via a vagovagal reflex that releases nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide from inhibitory motor neurons in the myenteric plexus. This fundic relaxation allows the stomach to accept a meal volume of 800-1200 mL without a significant increase in intragastric pressure. In disorders such as functional dyspepsia, diabetic gastroparesis, post-vagotomy states, and infiltrative gastric disease (linitis plastica from gastric adenocarcinoma), this fundic relaxation is impaired. The meal therefore rapidly increases intragastric pressure, stretching tension-sensitive mechanoreceptors in the gastric wall that signal satiety to the brain via vagal afferents, leading to premature meal termination.[1]
- **Postprandial Fullness**: A sensation of prolonged, uncomfortable retention of food in the stomach after a meal. Unlike early satiety, the meal is completed but the sensation of gastric distension persists for hours, as if the stomach is not emptying. Pathogenesis: This symptom reflects delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis). Gastric emptying is a highly coordinated process requiring normal function of the vagus nerve, the myenteric plexus, the interstitial cells of Cajal (generating the slow-wave electrical rhythm at 3 cycles per minute, which paces smooth muscle contraction in the distal stomach), and the pylorus (which must relax in synchrony with antral contraction to allow chyme to pass). In diabetic gastroparesis, hyperglycemia impairs vagal function, and there is loss of the nNOS-containing inhibitory neurons in the myenteric plexus, which are required for pyloric relaxation. The antrum contracts against a closed pylorus, and the food is not ground and emptied. Gastric stasis and fermentation may cause associated belching and halitosis.[1]
- **Belching (Eructation)**: The audible expulsion of gas from the esophagus or stomach through the mouth. Pathogenesis: Two types:
  - *Supragastric belching*: The most common in patients with bothersome belching (including functional dyspepsia and GERD). The patient subconsciously creates negative intrathoracic pressure (by contracting the diaphragm against a closed glottis), drawing air into the esophagus, then immediately expels it by raising intrathoracic pressure (contracting abdominal muscles). The air never enters the stomach. This is a behavioral disorder, a learned response to an uncomfortable sensation of fullness or bloating.
  - *Gastric belching*: Excess intragastric gas is expelled via a transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxation. Gas in the stomach arises from swallowed air (aerophagia) or from carbonated beverages. Normal individuals belch 20-30 times per day, mostly unconscious and unnoticed.[1]
- **Abdominal Distension / Bloating**: Bloating is the subjective sensation of increased abdominal pressure and girth. Distension is the objective, measurable increase in abdominal circumference. These are distinct phenomena that can occur independently. Pathogenesis:
  - *Gas trapping*: In irritable bowel syndrome, the normal postprandial movement of gas along the small intestine is impaired. Gas (whether swallowed air, CO2 from acid neutralization by bicarbonate, or hydrogen/methane from bacterial carbohydrate fermentation) becomes trapped in discrete pockets, and the bowel wall contracts abnormally around them, causing pain and the sensation of distension.
  - *Abdominophrenic dyssynergia*: In patients with IBS and functional bloating, the normal response to an increase in intra-abdominal gas volume is aberrant. Instead of relaxing the abdominal wall muscles and accommodating the gas, the diaphragm paradoxically contracts and descends, while the anterior abdominal wall muscles relax (viscero-somatic reflex). This pushes the abdominal contents anteriorly, increasing the objective abdominal circumference. This explains why patients with severe bloating exhibit visible distension that increases over the day and is absent upon waking.[1]
- **Borborygmi**: Audible, gurgling, rumbling sounds produced by the movement of gas and fluid through the intestines. Pathogenesis: Borborygmi are produced by the normal peristaltic activity of the small and large intestines. They are the sound of liquid and gas being mixed and propelled by smooth muscle contractions. They are loud in states of hyperperistalsis: gastroenteritis, irritable bowel syndrome, early intestinal obstruction (where the bowel proximal to the obstruction contracts vigorously against the resistance), and malabsorption states (where excess intraluminal fluid and gas from undigested nutrients magnify the acoustic resonance). Postprandial borborygmi are normal. Prolonged high-pitched, tinkling borborygmi occurring in bursts synchronously with abdominal cramps strongly suggest intestinal obstruction; the sound is produced by fluid and gas under tension passing through a partially obstructed segment.[1]
- **Flatulence**: The passage of intestinal gas per rectum. Pathogenesis: Intestinal gas comes from two sources: (1) Swallowed air (nitrogen and oxygen; nitrogen is not absorbed and comprises most of flatus volume), (2) Colonic bacterial fermentation of unabsorbed carbohydrates that reach the cecum, producing hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane, and trace volatile sulfur compounds (hydrogen sulfide, methanethiol) which produce the characteristic odor. Foods rich in non-absorbable oligosaccharides (beans, lentils, onions, cabbage) are classic flatus triggers because human small intestine lacks the enzymes (α-galactosidase) to break down the oligosaccharides raffinose and stachyose. The bacteria of the colon, especially Methanobrevibacter smithii and sulphate-reducing bacteria, produce the gases. Exaggerated flatulence occurs in lactose intolerance, celiac disease (carbohydrate malabsorption), small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and rapid intestinal transit (delivering more unabsorbed substrate to the colon).[1]
- **Fecal Urgency**: A sudden, overwhelming need to defecate immediately. Pathogenesis: Fecal urgency indicates reduced rectal compliance (the rectum is stiff and cannot distend to accommodate stool) or inflammation of the rectum and anal canal. Normally, arrival of stool in the rectum triggers the rectoanal inhibitory reflex (internal anal sphincter relaxes), but this does not cause immediate urgency because the external anal sphincter voluntarily contracts, and the compliant rectum distends to accommodate the stool. In ulcerative colitis, radiation proctitis, and irritable bowel syndrome, the rectal wall is inflamed, fibrotic, or hypersensitive to distension. Consequently, even a small volume of stool or gas triggers a powerful, urgent sensation of imminent defecation because the inflamed mucosa stimulates the defecation reflex at a lower-than-normal threshold of rectal distension. The patient has only seconds to reach a toilet.[1]
- **Mucus Per Rectum**: Passage of clear, gelatinous, or white material from the rectum, either coating the stool or passed alone. Pathogenesis: Mucus is normally produced by goblet cells throughout the colon to lubricate the passage of stool. Mucus production is regulated by cholinergic nerves and local immune mediators. In disease states, goblet cell hyperplasia and hypersecretion occur: (1) Irritable bowel syndrome: mucus is typically white and is passed with stool or alone; the mechanism is functional hypersecretion, not inflammation; (2) Ulcerative colitis/proctitis: copious mucus mixed with blood and pus, produced by the inflammatory response (cytokines IL-13 and IL-4 induce goblet cell metaplasia and MUC5AC mucin gene expression); (3) Villous adenoma of the rectum: profuse, voluminous, watery mucus secretion that can cause hypokalemia from potassium loss in the mucus (a classic, board exam-vital association). The polyp itself produces the mucus.[1]

## Second: Signs of the Gastrointestinal System

- **Cushing's Ulcer Sign**: A distinct pathophysiological entity, not a physical sign per se, but its presentation is a sign of severe intracranial pathology. Definition: Gastric, duodenal, or esophageal ulcers that arise in the setting of severe traumatic brain injury, increased intracranial pressure, or neurosurgical procedures. Mechanism: The increased intracranial pressure stimulates the vagal nuclei in the brainstem directly, causing massive, unopposed vagal stimulation of the gastric parietal cells, leading to extreme gastric acid hypersecretion. This hyperacidity overwhelms the gastric mucosal barrier, causing deep, penetrating ulcers that have a high risk of perforation. Differ from Curling's ulcers (burns) and stress ulcers (shock/sepsis) which are ischemic and result from splanchnic hypoperfusion leading to mucosal ischemia, reduced bicarbonate secretion, and disruption of the gastric mucosal barrier. Cushing's ulcers are primary acid-driven and vagally mediated.[1]
- **Curling's Ulcer Sign**: Gastric or duodenal stress ulcers occurring in patients with severe burns (>30% total body surface area). Mechanism: The massive burn injury causes systemic hypovolemia and a profound catecholamine and vasopressin release, leading to intense splanchnic vasoconstriction and mucosal ischemia. The resultant loss of the gastric mucosal barrier allows back-diffusion of gastric acid, producing multiple, shallow, bleeding erosions, primarily in the gastric fundus and body. The ulcers are a sign of severe burn pathophysiology (the burn itself is the visible sign; the endoscopic finding is the internal sign).[1]
- **Succession Splash**: An audible splashing sound heard on auscultation or with the ear near the epigastrium when the patient's abdomen is shaken from side to side. Definition: This sign indicates the presence of air and fluid in a distended stomach. Technique: The patient lies supine. The examiner places the stethoscope over the epigastrium and rapidly rocks the patient's pelvis or abdomen side to side. A splashing sound, heard 3+ hours after the last meal, is abnormal. Mechanism: Delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis, gastric outlet obstruction from peptic ulcer disease or malignancy) causes stasis of gastric contents, which mix with swallowed air. The fluid and air create an air-fluid level which, when agitated, produces the characteristic sound. A succession splash is normally present immediately after a meal; its persistence (or presence after fasting) confirms gastric stasis. It is a valuable bedside test, with a positive finding often correlating with a gastric residual volume >200 mL.[2]
- **Visible Gastric Peristalsis**: Wave-like movements seen across the upper abdomen (epigastrium), moving from left to right. Definition: The visible contraction of a hypertrophied gastric wall, propagating toward the pylorus. Mechanism: Gastric outlet obstruction (from chronic peptic ulcer disease at the pylorus, annular carcinoma of the antrum, or hypertrophic pyloric stenosis in infants) forces the stomach to generate high pressures to push chyme through the narrowed outlet. The gastric smooth muscle, particularly in the antrum, undergoes work hypertrophy (just as the left ventricle hypertrophies in aortic stenosis). The powerful, hypertrophied antral contractions become visible through the thin abdominal wall of a cachectic patient or a thin infant. The sign is pathognomonic for gastric outlet obstruction. It is best observed when the patient is lying flat with tangential lighting.[2]
- **Caput Medusae**: A cluster of dilated, tortuous, engorged veins radiating outward from the umbilicus. Definition: A cruciform arrangement of distended paraumbilical veins. Mechanism: Portal hypertension (commonly from cirrhosis) causes blood to seek collateral routes from the high-pressure portal system to the low-pressure systemic venous system. The paraumbilical veins (remnants of the fetal umbilical vein) in the falciform ligament recanalize, forming a connection between the left portal vein and the periumbilical systemic veins (superficial and inferior epigastric veins, which drain to the iliac vein and then the IVC). The direction of blood flow in a caput medusae is always *away* from the umbilicus, radiataing outward. This is a classic, specific sign of portal hypertension, though it is not common. It is more visible in thin or cachectic patients with advanced cirrhosis and portal pressure >10 mmHg.[2]
- **Spider Angiomata (Spider Telangiectasias)**: A central, bright red arteriole (the body of the spider) from which fine, radiating vessels (the legs) extend outward. Definition: A cutaneous vascular lesion. When blanched with pressure, the central arteriole refills from the center outward. Mechanism: Elevated serum estrogen levels (due to impaired hepatic metabolism of estrogens in chronic liver disease, particularly cirrhosis) cause vasodilation and proliferation of cutaneous arterioles. The distribution is in the territory of the superior vena cava (upper trunk, face, neck, arms, and hands). This is a classic cutaneous sign of liver disease (especially alcoholic cirrhosis). More than five spider angiomata in this distribution is a sign of underlying chronic liver disease, though they can occur in pregnancy and in healthy individuals (fewer than 3).[2]
- **Palmar Erythema**: Bilateral, symmetric, warm, reddening of the palms, most prominent over the thenar and hypothenar eminences, with sparing of the central palm. Definition: A cutaneous sign of hyperdynamic circulation and altered sex hormone metabolism in chronic liver disease. Mechanism: Elevated estrogen levels, combined with a hyperdynamic circulatory state (increased cardiac output, reduced peripheral vascular resistance, and arteriovenous shunting that occurs in advanced cirrhosis), lead to vasodilation of the palmar capillaries. It is a sensitive but non-specific sign; it also occurs in pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, and rheumatoid arthritis. It blanches on pressure, and the soles of the feet are often similarly affected (plantar erythema).[2]
- **Asterixis (Liver Flap)**: A sudden, brief, non-rhythmic loss of postural tone in the extended hands and wrists. Definition: A sign of metabolic encephalopathy, most characteristically hepatic encephalopathy, but also seen in uremia and hypercapnic respiratory failure. Technique: Ask the patient to extend their arms, dorsiflex their wrists (as if stopping traffic), and spread their fingers. Observe for 30 seconds. A positive sign is a series of sudden, arrhythmic flexion movements of the wrists and metacarpophalangeal joints ("flapping"). Mechanism: Asterixis is not a tremor. It results from a dysfunction of the diencephalic-motor regulatory centers that maintain continuous muscle postural tone. The pathophysiology is disputed but involves ammonia-induced dysfunction of the astrocytes in the basal ganglia and thalamus, impairing the smooth integration of proprioceptive input and motor output. The result is intermittent lapses in the maintenance of posture, followed by an overshoot correction. It is a negative myoclonus.[2]
- **Fetor Hepaticus**: A sweet, musty, slightly fecal odor to the breath. Definition: A specific odor signifying severe hepatocellular failure and portosystemic shunting. Mechanism: Methyl mercaptan and other volatile sulfur-containing compounds, produced by the bacterial metabolism of methionine in the colon, are normally extracted from the portal blood by the liver and metabolized. In liver failure and portosystemic shunting, these volatile substances bypass the liver, enter the systemic circulation, and are excreted in the breath. The odor is similar to that of a freshly opened corpse (also a mixture of volatile sulfur compounds) and is a reliable bedside sign of severe portosystemic shunting and hepatic encephalopathy risk.[2]
- **Jaundice (Physical Sign)**: Yellow-green discoloration of the skin, sclerae, and mucous membranes. Definition: A visible sign of hyperbilirubinemia. The scleral icterus is the first and most sensitive physical sign (appears at bilirubin >2.5 mg/dL) because the scleral elastin has a high affinity for bilirubin. It is examined in natural daylight, as artificial yellow light can mask it. As bilirubin rises, the skin becomes yellow, then eventually green (from oxidation of bilirubin to biliverdin, indicating prolonged, severe cholestasis). The distribution is assessed: cholestatic jaundice often has a greenish hue; hemolytic jaundice is often a pale lemon-yellow.[2]
- **Scratch Marks (Excoriations)**: Linear or angular, often crusted, erosions and excoriations on the skin from intense scratching. Definition: A sign of severe pruritus, most commonly due to cholestatic liver disease (primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, obstructive jaundice). The distribution is often on the back, shoulders, extensor surfaces of the limbs, and the shins. In cholestasis, the accumulation of bile acids and lysophosphatidic acid in the skin are the most potent pruritogens. Excoriations are often the presenting sign of PBC.[2]
- **Ascites**: The pathological accumulation of free fluid within the peritoneal cavity. Definition: A clinical sign detectable on physical examination by shifting dullness, fluid thrill, and bulging flanks. Pathogenesis: The primary mechanism in cirrhosis is portal hypertension causing increased hydrostatic pressure in the splanchnic capillaries, combined with splanchnic arterial vasodilation (from nitric oxide overproduction). This vasodilation leads to a reduction in the effective arterial blood volume. The kidney perceives this as hypovolemia and activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and the sympathetic nervous system, causing avid renal sodium and water retention. The retained fluid and the high portal pressure together form the transudative ascites of cirrhosis. Other causes: peritoneal carcinomatosis (exudative, due to tumor implants increasing capillary permeability and obstructing lymphatics), pancreatic ascites (exudative, from a leaking pancreatic pseudocyst), and chylous ascites (lymphatic obstruction from lymphoma or surgical trauma). The physical signs of ascites: abdominal distension, bulging flanks, shifting dullness (sensitivity 83%, specificity 56%), and a fluid thrill (specificity 82-92% but requires a large volume of ascites). Shifting dullness is the most reliable bedside test. The fluid must be at least 1500 mL to be detected by physical exam. Ultrasound detects as little as 100 mL.[2]
- **Hepatomegaly**: An enlarged liver, detected on physical examination by palpation of the liver edge below the right costal margin, or by percussion of a liver span exceeding 12 cm in the midclavicular line. Mechanism: The liver enlarges by several mechanisms:
  - *Congestion*: Right-sided heart failure, constrictive pericarditis, or Budd-Chiari syndrome cause increased hepatic venous pressure, engorging the liver with blood. The liver is firm, smooth, and tender, with a rounded edge. The hepatojugular reflux is positive.
  - *Inflammation*: Acute viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HEV), alcoholic hepatitis, drug-induced hepatitis (acetaminophen). The liver is enlarged and exquisitely tender (the capsule is stretched).
  - *Infiltration*: Fatty infiltration (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, alcohol) is the most common cause. Amyloidosis and sarcoidosis cause a firm, non-tender, smooth liver. Malignant infiltration (metastasis, hepatocellular carcinoma) causes a rock-hard, irregular, nodular liver.
  - *Cholestasis*: Extrahepatic biliary obstruction back-pressures bile into the liver, causing smooth, symmetric, tender enlargement.[2]
- **Splenomegaly**: Enlarged spleen, palpable below the left costal margin on inspiration. Mechanism: The spleen enlarges by five mechanisms, all related to its function as a lymphoid organ and a filter of senescent blood cells and microorganisms.
  - *Portal hypertension*: The most common cause worldwide. The spleen is engorged with blood due to increased splenic venous pressure (back-pressure from the portal vein). The spleen is firm, non-tender, and smooth.
  - *Work hypertrophy (reticuloendothelial hyperplasia)*: Chronic hemolytic anemias (hereditary spherocytosis, thalassemia, sickle cell disease) overwhelm the splenic macrophages with red cell debris; the macrophages proliferate, causing massive splenomegaly. Also in chronic infections (infective endocarditis, malaria, kala-azar).
  - *Infiltration*: Hematological malignancies (lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, acute lymphoblastic leukemia) infiltrate the splenic white and red pulp; myeloproliferative disorders (polycythemia vera, myelofibrosis, essential thrombocythemia) cause extramedullary hematopoiesis in the spleen. Infiltrative diseases: Gaucher disease, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis.
  - *Immune hyperplasia*: Autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis — Felty's syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus).
  - *Splenic congestion from isolated splenic vein thrombosis*: The spleen is enlarged but the liver is normal; portal pressure is normal; this is segmental (left-sided) portal hypertension.[2]
- **Cullen's Sign**: Periumbilical ecchymosis (bluish-black, purplish discoloration around the umbilicus). Definition: A sign of retroperitoneal hemorrhage, usually from hemorrhagic pancreatitis. Mechanism: The retroperitoneal blood, rich in methemoglobin, tracks from the retroperitoneum along the falciform ligament (which contains the paraumbilical veins and connects the retroperitoneum to the anterior abdominal wall at the umbilicus). The blood dissects through the fascial planes of the round ligament to reach the subcutaneous periumbilical tissue. This sign is not specific to pancreatitis; it can occur in any retroperitoneal bleed (ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, splenic rupture). It takes 24-48 hours to appear and is a late sign of severe retroperitoneal hemorrhage.[2]
- **Grey Turner's Sign**: Ecchymosis of the flanks (bluish-red, purple, or brown discoloration of the skin overlying the flanks). Definition: A classic sign of severe, necrotizing pancreatitis with retroperitoneal hemorrhage. Mechanism: The retroperitoneal blood extravasates from the inflamed, necrotic pancreas and tracks along the retroperitoneal fascial planes (the pararenal space, anterior and posterior renal fasciae) to the lateral edge of the quadratus lumborum muscle, where it reaches the subcutaneous tissue of the flanks. It is a sign of severe, usually fatal pancreatitis (present in <3% of cases, but associated with a mortality rate >30%). Also seen in ruptured ectopic pregnancy and retroperitoneal hemorrhage from any cause. It appears 24-72 hours after the onset of pancreatitis.[2]
- **Murphy's Sign**: The sudden cessation of inspiration due to pain when the examiner's fingers, placed under the right costal margin at the mid-clavicular line, contact an inflamed, distended gallbladder as the patient breathes deeply. Definition: The pathognomonic physical sign of acute cholecystitis. Technique: The examiner places their hand flat on the right subcostal region, with the fingers hooked under the costal margin at the anatomical location of the gallbladder (the intersection of the right costal margin and the lateral border of the rectus abdominis muscle). The patient is asked to take a deep inspiration. The descent of the diaphragm pushes the liver and the inflamed gallbladder inferiorly. When the inflamed gallbladder touches the examiner's fingers, the patient experiences a sharp, localized catch of pain and abruptly ceases inspiration. Sensitivity is 50-65%, specificity is 85-95%. The sign is absent if the gallbladder is not distended (e.g., in gangrenous cholecystitis where the nerves are infarcted).[2]
- **Courvoisier's Sign (Courvoisier's Law)**: A palpable, non-tender, distended gallbladder in a jaundiced patient. Definition: The presence of this sign (also called Courvoisier-Terrier sign) strongly argues against the diagnosis of chronic cholelithiasis as the cause of jaundice, and instead suggests malignant obstruction of the common bile duct. Mechanism: In chronic cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, recurrent inflammation causes the gallbladder wall to become thick, fibrotic, and scarred. The gallbladder is unable to distend when the cystic duct or common bile duct becomes obstructed by a stone because the fibrotic wall cannot stretch. In contrast, a malignant obstruction of the distal common bile duct (classically, adenocarcinoma of the head of the pancreas, or cholangiocarcinoma) occurs in a patient who previously had a normal, thin-walled, compliant gallbladder. The gradual, progressive obstruction allows the gallbladder to distend passively to a large size, and it is palpable through the abdominal wall. Since the inflammation is absent, it is painless. The classic presentation: painless jaundice with a palpable gallbladder is pancreatic head cancer until proven otherwise. The exceptions to Courvoisier's law (i.e., stones causing a palpable gallbladder) are primary common bile duct stones in a patient without prior cholecystitis, and Mirizzi syndrome.[2]
- **McBurney's Sign**: Point tenderness at McBurney's point (one-third of the distance from the right anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus). Definition: The classic physical sign of acute appendicitis. Mechanism: The inflamed appendix, when it is in its typical retrocecal or subcecal location, lies under this point. The deep palpation of this point brings the examiner's fingers into contact with the inflamed, distended, and irritated appendix and the overlying parietal peritoneum, which now detects pain with exquisite precision. This is a sign of somatic (parietal) pain, indicating that the inflammation has progressed beyond the visceral peritoneum of the appendix to involve the parietal peritoneum. The sensitivity is 50-75%, specificity 85-95% when combined with a suggestive history.[2]
- **Rovsing's Sign**: Palpation of the left lower quadrant produces pain in the right lower quadrant. Definition: A sign of peritoneal irritation, typically from acute appendicitis. Mechanism: The left-sided palpation pushes gas from the left colon retrograde into the transverse colon and then the cecum. The sudden distension of the cecum stretches the inflamed appendiceal wall, triggering pain. It indicates localized peritoneal inflammation. Sensitivity is low (22-68%), specificity is moderate (58-96%).[2]
- **Psoas Sign**: Pain on passive right hip extension (the patient lies on their left side and the examiner extends the right thigh backward). Alternatively, pain on active right hip flexion against resistance. Definition: A sign that the inflamed appendix lies against the psoas muscle (retrocecal appendicitis). Mechanism: The psoas major muscle lies immediately posterior to the retrocecal appendix. When the appendix is inflamed, stretching the psoas muscle (by extending the hip) or contracting it (by flexing the hip) irritates the inflamed appendix lying on its surface. This is a specific test for retrocecal appendicitis; a positive psoas sign increases the likelihood of appendicitis.[2]
- **Obturator Sign**: Pain on passive internal rotation of the flexed right hip. Technique: The patient lies supine. The examiner flexes the patient's right hip and knee to 90 degrees, then internally rotates the hip (foot moves laterally). Definition: A sign indicating a pelvic appendix that lies against the obturator internus muscle. Mechanism: Internally rotating the flexed hip stretches the obturator internus muscle across the pelvic brim. If an inflamed appendix lies adjacent to or against this muscle, the stretch causes pain. This is a less common but specific sign of pelvic appendicitis. Sensitivity is low, specificity is 67%.[2]
- **Rebound Tenderness (Blumberg's Sign)**: Pain that is greater on the sudden release of deep palpation than on the initial pressure. Definition: A sign of parietal peritoneal inflammation (peritonitis). Technique: The examiner's hand is pressed deeply and slowly into the area of the abdomen distant from the site of pain. The pressure is released suddenly. A positive sign is pain at the site of the primary pathology (not at the site of palpation) upon release. Mechanism: Deep palpation displaces the abdominal wall away from the inflamed parietal peritoneum. The sudden release causes the abdominal wall to snap back and strike the inflamed peritoneum, causing sharp, localized pain. This sign is a hallmark of peritonitis (appendicitis, perforated ulcer, diverticulitis). In modern surgical practice, this sign is considered cruel and unnecessary; gentle percussion over the area (percussion tenderness) provides the same information without causing severe, unnecessary pain. Percussion tenderness is equivalent to rebound but safer and more humane.[2]
- **Guarding (Défense Musculaire)**: Involuntary rigidity of the abdominal wall muscles overlying an area of peritoneal inflammation. Definition: Distinct from the voluntary guarding a patient may do in anticipation of pain. Involuntary guarding cannot be relaxed by persuasion or distraction. It is a reflex arc: the inflamed parietal peritoneum stimulates the somatic sensory nerves (intercostal nerves T7-T12), which synapse in the spinal cord and directly activate the motor neurons of those same segmental nerves, causing a reflex contraction of the abdominal muscles. This is a segmental, spinally-mediated reflex, and a highly reliable sign of peritoneal inflammation. The board-like rigidity of generalised peritonitis indicates a surgical emergency.[2]
- **Bowel Sounds**: Auscultated sounds produced by the movement of gas and fluid in the intestines. Definition: A dynamic sign reflecting intestinal motility. Normal bowel sounds: 5-35 high-pitched, irregular, gurgling sounds per minute. Pathogenesis of abnormal sounds:
  - *Hyperactive (borborygmi)*: Loud, high-pitched, rushing, tinkling sounds occurring in rushes synchronous with colicky pain. This is the classic sign of mechanical intestinal obstruction. The bowel proximal to the obstruction contracts vigorously against the resistance, producing high-pitched sounds as gas is forced through fluid under pressure in the dilated loops.
  - *Absent (silent abdomen)*: No bowel sounds after auscultating for a full 2 minutes (some references say 3-5 minutes). This is the sign of generalized ileus (paralytic ileus), where peristalsis is globally absent. Causes: post-laparotomy (physiological ileus for 24-72 hours), peritonitis, hypokalemia, use of anticholinergic or opioid medications, and severe systemic illness (sepsis, pneumonia). The absence of sound indicates a complete cessation of muscular activity.[2]
- **Digital Rectal Examination (DRE) Signs**: The DRE yields several critical GI signs:
  - *Guaiac-positive or FIT-positive stool*: The glove tip is smeared with stool and tested. A blue color change indicates heme.
  - *Palpable rectal mass*: A firm, irregular, fixed, or pedunculated mass is felt. The location, size, consistency, mobility, and relationship to the anal sphincter complex are assessed. A hard, non-tender, fixed, ulcerated mass is highly suggestive of rectal carcinoma.
  - *Hemorrhoids*: External hemorrhoids are visible and palpable; they are tender if thrombosed. Internal hemorrhoids are not palpable on DRE unless thrombosed or chronic (they feel like soft, compressible, pillowy masses above the dentate line).
  - *Anal sphincter tone*: Decreased resting tone (patulous anus) indicates a neurological insult (cauda equina syndrome, spinal cord injury, or pudendal nerve damage from obstetric trauma or chronic straining). Increased tone with a hypertonic, non-relaxing puborectalis is a physical finding in dyssynergic defecation.
  - *Bulging of the posterior bladder or a boggy mass anterior to the rectum*: In acute prostatitis, the prostate is exquisitely tender, boggy, and warm.
  - *Blood, mucus, or pus on the examining finger*: Indicative of proctitis.[2]

## Third: Clinical Examination of the Gastrointestinal System

### General Principles

The abdominal examination is performed with the patient supine, arms at their sides, head resting on a pillow (to relax the abdominal wall muscles), and the abdomen adequately exposed from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, with the groin visible. The examiner's hands must be warm, and the approach is gentle and systematic. The traditional sequence is Inspection, Auscultation, Palpation, Percussion. Note that auscultation precedes palpation and percussion in the abdomen because palpation and percussion can alter bowel sounds.[2,3]

### Inspection

### End-of-Bed Inspection

- General appearance: Does the patient look well or ill? Are they cachectic (malignancy, cirrhosis) or obese (NAFLD, metabolic syndrome)? Is there jaundice visible from the doorway? Is there a distended abdomen suggesting ascites, obstruction, or a massive tumor? Are they lying still (peritonitis, movement shakes the inflamed peritoneum causing pain) or writhing in pain (intestinal colic, where movement does not affect the pain)?
- Position: The patient with peritonitis lies perfectly still, often with the knees flexed to relax the abdominal wall and psoas muscles. The patient with intestinal obstruction or biliary colic writhes and constantly shifts position, unable to find comfort.
- Visible aids: Nasogastric tube (intestinal obstruction, pancreatitis, upper GI bleed), abdominal drains, stomas, abdominal wall VAC dressings, oxygen (sepsis, aspiration).
- Obvious masses: A massively distended abdomen with an everted umbilicus suggests ascites. A localized epigastric mass could be a pancreatic pseudocyst. Asymmetry from an incisional hernia or a large intra-abdominal tumor is noted.

### Close/Directed Inspection

- Skin: Jaundice, spider angiomata (superior vena cava distribution), palmar erythema, scratch marks (cholestasis), Cullen's sign (periumbilical ecchymosis), Grey Turner's sign (flank ecchymosis), surgical scars (previous laparotomy, cholecystectomy, appendectomy). Note the precise location, length, and character of scars (a thin, linear scar vs. a wide, hypertrophic scar). Scars provide a map of previous operations and potential adhesions that could cause obstruction.
- Umbilicus: Normally inverted. Everted in ascites ("portal of exit" for intra-abdominal pressure), or a mass (Sister Mary Joseph nodule: a firm, indurated, sometimes ulcerated periumbilical lymph node metastasis from an intra-abdominal malignancy, most classically gastric adenocarcinoma).
- Visible veins: Dilated abdominal wall veins are abnormal. In portal hypertension, the veins radiate from the umbilicus (caput medusae). In IVC obstruction, the collateral veins flow upward from the groin toward the axilla (and the direction of flow in veins below the umbilicus is upward). In SVC obstruction, flow is downward.
- Visible peristalsis: The wave-like movement of a distended, hypertrophied stomach or obstructed small bowel. Gastric peristalsis moves from left to right across the epigastrium. Small bowel peristalsis produces a visible step-ladder pattern of dilated, contracting loops crossing the central abdomen.
- Stomas: A stoma is inspected for its type (end ileostomy: budded, spouted; end colostomy: flush with the skin), its viability (pink and moist mucosa indicates good perfusion; dusky, black, or dry mucosa indicates ischemia, a surgical emergency), its output volume and character (watery, pasty, formed), and any peristomal skin breakdown.
- Hernias: Inspect the inguinal, femoral, umbilical, and incisional areas with the patient both supine and standing, and performing the Valsalva maneuver (which increases intra-abdominal pressure and makes a hernia protrude). An expansile cough impulse is visible.
- Abdominal contour: Flat, scaphoid (sunken, suggesting malnutrition or malignancy), protuberant (obesity, ascites, gas, feces, fetus, or fat — the five Fs). A localized bulge may be a hernia, a tumor, or an enlarged organ.

### Auscultation

The stethoscope diaphragm is placed gently on the abdomen. Auscultation precedes palpation and percussion because manipulation of the bowel stimulates peristalsis and artificially produces bowel sounds.

- **Bowel Sounds**: Listen in one place (usually the right lower quadrant) for at least 30 seconds. If absent, you must listen for a full 2-3 minutes (some guidelines say 5 minutes) in each quadrant before declaring an ileus. Hyperactive, high-pitched, tinkling, rushing sounds in a crescendo of frequency and amplitude that coincide with colicky abdominal pain are the classic auscultatory finding in mechanical small bowel obstruction. These are the sounds of peristaltic waves forcing gas and fluid through a narrowed, obstructed lumen in dilated bowel loops. In late obstruction, the bowel fatigues and sounds become infrequent ("quiet before the storm" before perforation). A silent abdomen for >5 minutes is the hallmark of paralytic ileus. Borborygmi are loud, prolonged gurgling sounds; they are normal and postprandial, but when exaggerated, suggest IBS or gastroenteritis.
- **Vascular Bruits**: The bell of the stethoscope is used to auscultate over the abdominal aorta (epigastrium), the renal arteries (2 cm superior and lateral to the umbilicus, bilaterally), the iliac arteries (below the umbilicus, along the lateral rectus border), and the liver (right upper quadrant). A renal artery bruit is a high-pitched, systolic (and sometimes continuous) sound that suggests renal artery stenosis, a potentially curable cause of hypertension. A hepatic bruit (continuous, roaring, machinery-like) is heard over the right upper quadrant and is pathognomonic for hepatocellular carcinoma (the tumor is highly vascular with arteriovenous shunting) or acute alcoholic hepatitis. An epigastric bruit may suggest an abdominal aortic aneurysm or mesenteric artery stenosis (chronic mesenteric ischemia).
- **Venous Hum**: A continuous, soft, low-pitched hum heard over the epigastrium/umbilicus in portal hypertension, from the high-velocity flow in the paraumbilical veins (Cruveilhier-Baumgarten murmur). A venous hum over the liver suggests an arteriovenous fistula (HCC). Murmurs referred from the heart can be heard in the epigastrium (tricuspid regurgitation).
- **Friction Rubs**: A rough, grating, leather-on-leather sound synchronous with respiration heard over the liver or spleen. A hepatic friction rub is heard in primary or metastatic liver cancer (due to the friction of the inflamed capsule against the diaphragm or peritoneum), perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome from chlamydial perihepatitis), or a liver abscess. A splenic friction rub is heard in splenic infarction (sickle cell disease, endocarditis with emboli).

### Percussion

Percussion is used to assess the distribution of gas, fluid, and solid structures in the abdomen. The abdominal viscera are percussed in all four quadrants.

- **General Percussion**: The normal abdomen is tympanitic due to the presence of gas-filled loops of bowel. Generalized tympany that is hyper-resonant indicates excess gas: aerophagia, intestinal obstruction (dilated loops), or functional bloating. Dullness indicates either a solid structure (enlarged liver or spleen, distended bladder, pregnant uterus, tumor) or free fluid (ascites) displacing bowel gas.
- **Liver Percussion**: The liver span is measured in the right midclavicular line. Start percussing from the right second intercostal space in the midclavicular line, working downward. Lung resonance transitions to liver dullness at the upper border (normally the 5th-7th intercostal space). Then, percuss upward from the right lower quadrant (where bowel is tympanitic) until tympany transitions to liver dullness at the lower border (normally at the costal margin). The normal liver span in the midclavicular line is 6-12 cm. A span >12 cm indicates hepatomegaly. A span <6 cm suggests a shrunken, cirrhotic liver (or a false-positive from an overlying loop of colon — Chilaiditi sign, where the colon is interposed between the liver and the diaphragm, causing loss of liver dullness). The accuracy of percussion for hepatomegaly is limited; ultrasound is the gold standard.[2,4]
- **Spleen Percussion**: The spleen is percussed in the left anterior axillary line, posterior to the midaxillary line. It is normally dull from the 9th to the 11th rib. The spleen must be two to three times its normal size to be palpable (it grows anteriorly, medially, and inferiorly). Percussion complements palpation. Detection of a splenic dullness extending to the left costal margin or below it supports splenomegaly. Traube's space: the area bounded by the left sixth rib superiorly, the left costal margin inferiorly, and the left anterior axillary line laterally. Normally, Traube's space is tympanitic because it overlies the gastric air bubble (fundus). In splenomegaly, the spleen fills this space, making it dull to percussion. A dull Traube's space has moderate sensitivity (62%) and specificity (72%) for splenomegaly.[4]
- **Ascites**: Two percussion signs:
  - *Shifting Dullness*: The patient lies supine. Gas-filled bowel loops float to the top of the fluid-filled abdomen, producing tympany in the center (periumbilical). The peritoneal fluid gravitates to the flanks, producing dullness. The point of transition from tympany to dullness is marked on the flank. The patient is then rolled to the opposite lateral decubitus position. After 30 seconds for the fluid to settle, the flank that was previously dull becomes tympanic (because the gas-filled bowel now floats to this side), and the dependent flank, which was previously tympanic, becomes dull (the fluid has shifted). A positive shifting dullness requires at least 1500 mL of ascites. Sensitivity 83%, specificity 56%.[4]
  - *Fluid Thrill*: An assistant (or the patient's hand) places the ulnar edge of their hand firmly in the midline of the abdomen to dampen the transmission wave through the abdominal wall fat. The examiner places one hand flat on one flank, and flicks the opposite flank with the index finger. If a palpable impulse (a "thrill") is transmitted through the fluid and felt by the stationary hand, free fluid is present. This sign requires a very large volume of ascites (>2000-3000 mL) and has high specificity (82-92%) but lower sensitivity.[4]
- **Bladder Percussion**: A distended bladder is percussed as a suprapubic, dull, rounded mass that rises out of the pelvis. It is dull to percussion, and cannot be felt below the symphysis (you cannot "get below" the mass). This distinguishes a distended bladder from a pelvic tumor or a gravid uterus, which can be palpated behind the pubic symphysis. Tympany above it is bowel. A post-void residual volume >150 mL is abnormal and indicates urinary retention, which can be caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia, urethral stricture, or neurogenic bladder.

### Palpation

Palpation is performed with the patient supine, breathing slowly and deeply through the mouth. The examiner's hand is flat, with the forearm horizontal. Palpation begins in the quadrant farthest from the site of pain, using light palpation first, then deep palpation. Watch the patient's face for signs of pain during palpation.

- **Light Palpation**: To assess for tenderness, guarding, and large superficial masses. The examining hand is depressed 1-2 cm with gentle, dipping motions. Guarding is involuntary rigidity; voluntary guarding is when the patient tenses the abdominal muscles in anticipation of pain (distinguished by asking the patient to breathe deeply and relax, or to breathe out and let the abdominal wall "sink"; voluntary guarding can be relaxed, involuntary guarding cannot).
- **Deep Palpation**: To delineate the liver edge, spleen, kidneys, and any deep masses. The hand is depressed 3-5 cm with steady, deep pressure. In an obese patient, bimanual palpation (one hand on top of the other) is used to increase depth. Palpation for the liver and spleen is timed with deep inspiration, which displaces the organs inferiorly toward the examiner's hand.
- **Liver Palpation**: The examiner's right hand is placed flat on the right lower quadrant, just below the area of liver dullness, with the fingers pointing upward and parallel to the midline. The patient breathes deeply. The diaphragm descends, pushing the liver inferiorly. As the patient inspires, the liver edge approaches the examiner's stationary fingers. A normal liver edge, if palpable, is soft, sharp, and non-tender, and slips under the finger pads. A diseased liver is firm, blunt, and tender (hepatitis), hard and irregular (cirrhosis), or rock-hard and nodular (malignant infiltration). The Riedel lobe is a normal anatomical variant: a tongue-like projection of the right hepatic lobe extending below the costal margin; it is soft and non-tender. A palpable liver has a sensitivity of 50-70% and specificity of 60-80% for true hepatomegaly; ultrasound is superior.[4]
- **Spleen Palpation**: The spleen must enlarge to two to three times its normal size to become palpable (from 150 g to >500 g). The patient lies supine or in the right lateral decubitus position (which brings the spleen anteriorly). The examiner's left hand is placed under the patient's left lower rib cage to pull the rib cage forward. The examiner's right hand is placed below the left costal margin. The patient breathes deeply. The splenic tip, if enlarged, descends to meet the examiner's fingertips on deep inspiration. The spleen is identified by a palpable notch on its medial border (the only palpable abdominal organ with a notch). A palpable spleen is dull to percussion. The two-handed maneuver with the patient in the right lateral position has the highest sensitivity (50-70%). The examination should begin at the left iliac fossa and work upward, to avoid missing massive splenomegaly that extends into the left lower quadrant.[4]
- **Kidney Palpation**: The kidneys are retroperitoneal and are balloted. For ballotment, the examiner's left hand is placed under the costovertebral angle and lifts the kidney upward. The right hand is placed below the costal margin. With deep inspiration, the kidney descends. The examiner's right hand palpates the kidney as it descends, and the left hand pushes it forward. Normally, the right kidney (lower and more accessible) may be palpated in a thin patient. The left kidney is rarely palpable. A palpable kidney indicates an enlarged kidney (hydronephrosis, polycystic kidney disease, renal cell carcinoma). The kidney is smooth, firm, and ballotable, and it moves inferiorly with inspiration. An enlarged spleen moves toward the right iliac fossa on inspiration (it follows the line of the 10th rib).
- **Aortic Palpation**: The abdominal aorta is palpated in the epigastrium, slightly to the left of the midline, by applying deep pressure with the flattened fingers of both hands on either side of the aorta. In a thin person, the normal aortic pulsation is transmitted. A pulsatile, expansile mass >3 cm in diameter suggests an abdominal aortic aneurysm. The sensitivity for detecting an AAA by palpation is low (30-50%), and it is dangerous to deeply palpate a suspected aneurysm; the diagnosis is confirmed by ultrasound. A non-pulsatile, rigid, calcified aorta suggests severe atherosclerosis.
- **McBurney's Point Palpation**: To diagnose appendicitis, deep palpation at McBurney's point is performed with one finger. The specificity for appendicitis increases when combined with a history of periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant, low-grade fever, and rebound or percussion tenderness.
- **Murphy's Sign Elicitation**: The examiner's hand is placed in the right subcostal region, fingers hooked under the costal margin at the midpoint of the costal margin. The patient inhales deeply. The sign is positive if the patient catches their breath due to sharp pain when the inflamed gallbladder hits the examiner's fingers. A sonographic Murphy's sign (pain when the ultrasound probe is pressed over the gallbladder) is more accurate, with a sensitivity of 85-90% and specificity of 90%.[4]
- **Rebound Tenderness Elicitation (Percussion Method Preferred)**: Instead of deep palpation and sudden release (Blumberg's sign), gentle percussion of each quadrant is used. Percuss firmly but gently with the tip of the middle finger over each quadrant. If the patient has a sharp, localized pain at the percussion site, this is percussion tenderness, a sign of localized peritonitis. This is more humane and equally sensitive as rebound.
- **Hernia Orifice Palpation**: The inguinal canal is palpated by invaginating the scrotal skin with the index finger and advancing into the external inguinal ring. The patient coughs or performs Valsalva. A palpable impulse that strikes the fingertip confirms an indirect inguinal hernia. The femoral ring is palpated below the inguinal ligament, medial to the femoral artery. An impulse here confirms a femoral hernia, which has a high risk of incarceration and strangulation.
- **Digital Rectal Examination (DRE) Technique**: The patient is placed in the left lateral decubitus position with hips and knees flexed (Sims position). After explanation and consent, the gloved, lubricated index finger is inserted into the anal canal. The following are systematically assessed: (1) Anal sphincter tone: assess resting tone (internal anal sphincter) and squeeze pressure (external anal sphincter). (2) Rectal vault: palpate the walls sequentially (anterior, posterior, lateral); feel for masses, polyps, ulcers. (3) Prostate (in males): palpated on the anterior wall, assessing size, symmetry, consistency, nodules, and tenderness. (4) Uterus/cervix (in females): palpable on the anterior wall; a retroverted uterus is a normal variant felt posteriorly. (5) Stool in the rectum: consistency (hard, soft, liquid), color, presence of blood or mucus. On withdrawal, inspect the glove for stool color and character. A fecal occult blood test is performed.

### Special Maneuvers

- **Hepatojugular Reflux (Abdominojugular Test)**: The patient breathes quietly with the head elevated to 45 degrees. The examiner applies firm, sustained pressure over the right upper quadrant (over the liver) for 10 seconds. The height of the jugular venous pressure (JVP) is observed. A positive test is defined as a sustained rise in the JVP of >3 cm for the duration of the compression, followed by an abrupt fall upon release. Mechanism: The increased abdominal pressure increases venous return to the right heart; a failing right ventricle cannot handle this added preload, causing the JVP to rise. This test is primarily a cardiac examination for right ventricular failure, but it is integrated into the abdominal exam for patients with hepatomegaly to differentiate congestive hepatopathy from primary liver disease. Sensitivity for right heart failure is 50-60%, specificity is high (>90%).[4]
- **Examination for Appendicitis**: The Alvarado score incorporates history and physical findings: Migration of pain to the right lower quadrant (1 point), anorexia (1 point), nausea/vomiting (1 point), tenderness in the right lower quadrant (2 points), rebound tenderness (1 point), fever (1 point), leukocytosis (2 points), shift to left (1 point). Score ≥7 suggests appendicitis; score ≤4 excludes it with high sensitivity.

## Fourth: Investigations Requested for the Gastrointestinal System

### Laboratory Investigations

- **Liver Function Tests (LFTs)**:
  - *Hepatocellular injury pattern*: Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are markedly elevated (>500 U/L in acute viral or toxic hepatitis; 2-10 times the upper limit of normal in chronic hepatitis). ALT is more specific to the liver. AST is also present in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, kidney, and red blood cells.
  - *Cholestatic pattern*: Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) are elevated. ALP is also produced by bone (osteoblasts), placenta, and intestine; GGT confirms the liver origin. Bilirubin is elevated, predominantly conjugated (direct) in cholestasis.
  - *Synthetic function*: Albumin (half-life 21 days, so reflects chronic liver disease) and prothrombin time/INR (half-life 6 hours; the most sensitive marker of acute liver failure because the liver synthesizes clotting factors II, VII, IX, X). A prolonged PT that does not correct with vitamin K suggests severe hepatocellular failure.
- **Serum Amylase and Lipase**: Elevated in acute pancreatitis. Lipase is more specific (originates almost exclusively from the pancreas) and has a longer half-life, so it remains elevated for days after amylase has normalized. A lipase >3 times the upper limit of normal is diagnostic for acute pancreatitis.
- **Serological Tests for Celiac Disease**:
  - *Anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) IgA*: The first-line screening test. Sensitivity >95%, specificity >98% for celiac disease. Total serum IgA must be measured concurrently, because 2-3% of celiac patients are IgA deficient (and will have a false-negative tTG-IgA). In IgA deficiency, anti-deamidated gliadin peptide (DGP) IgG or tTG-IgG is used.
  - *Anti-endomysial antibody (EMA) IgA*: Confirms a positive tTG-IgA. Specificity approaches 100%. Detected by indirect immunofluorescence.
- **Fecal Calprotectin**: A neutrophil-derived protein that is a marker of intestinal inflammation. It distinguishes inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) from irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). A value <50 µg/g effectively rules out IBD (negative predictive value >95%). A value >200 µg/g indicates active mucosal inflammation and mandates ileocolonoscopy.
- **Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT) and Fecal Immunochemical Test (FIT)**:
  - *Guaiac FOBT*: Detects the peroxidase activity of heme. Subject to false positives from dietary peroxidases (red meat, broccoli, horseradish) and false negatives from vitamin C. Sensitivity for colorectal cancer is 30-50%.
  - *Fecal Immunochemical Test (FIT)*: Uses antibodies specific to the globin portion of human hemoglobin. It is more sensitive (79% for CRC) and does not require dietary restriction. It detects only lower GI bleeding because globin is digested in the proximal gut. FIT is now the preferred stool-based screening test for colorectal cancer.
- **H. pylori Testing**:
  - *Urea Breath Test*: The patient ingests 13C- or 14C-labeled urea. H. pylori's urease enzyme hydrolyzes urea to ammonia and labeled CO2, which is absorbed into the bloodstream and exhaled. The labeled CO2 is measured in a breath sample. Sensitivity and specificity >95%. Used for initial diagnosis and to confirm eradication.
  - *Stool Antigen Test*: Detects H. pylori antigen in stool by enzyme immunoassay. Sensitivity and specificity >90%. Also used for diagnosis and confirmation of eradication (must be performed at least 4 weeks after completion of therapy and 2 weeks after stopping PPIs).
  - *Serology (IgG antibodies)*: Detects exposure, not active infection. It is not useful in areas of high prevalence because it remains positive after eradication. Not recommended for diagnosis unless the pretest probability is very high.

### Radiological Investigations

- **Abdominal Plain Film (Supine and Upright)**:
  - Indications: Suspected intestinal obstruction, perforated viscus, foreign body ingestion, toxic megacolon.
  - Findings in obstruction: Dilated loops of small bowel (>3 cm diameter) with air-fluid levels on the upright film and a "string of beads" sign (small pockets of gas in the valvulae conniventes of a fluid-filled bowel). Large bowel dilation is >6 cm (cecum >9 cm is a risk for perforation).
  - Pneumoperitoneum: Free air under the diaphragm on the upright film (or left lateral decubitus film in a patient who cannot stand). The left lateral decubitus film is the most sensitive for a small volume of free air, because the air rises to the right flank (the highest point) between the liver and the abdominal wall. A perforated viscus is a surgical emergency.
- **Abdominal Ultrasound (US)**:
  - First-line imaging for the gallbladder and biliary tree. The Murphy's sign can be elicited with the probe (sonographic Murphy's sign). Detects gallstones (echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing), gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid, and common bile duct dilation (>6 mm).
  - Detects ascites (anechoic free fluid in the dependent recesses: Morrison's pouch, paracolic gutters, pouch of Douglas).
  - Liver: Detects fatty infiltration (increased echogenicity compared to the renal cortex), cirrhosis (nodular surface contour, heterogeneous echotexture), and masses.
  - Doppler ultrasound: Assesses the patency and flow direction in the portal vein, hepatic veins, and splenic vein. Used to diagnose portal hypertension and Budd-Chiari syndrome.
- **Computed Tomography (CT) of the Abdomen and Pelvis**:
  - The highest-yield cross-sectional imaging modality for acute abdominal pain and trauma. Intravenous contrast enhances solid organs, tumors, and vessels; oral contrast opacifies the bowel lumen, distinguishing bowel from a mesenteric mass or abscess.
  - CT findings in acute pancreatitis: Pancreatic enlargement, peripancreatic fat stranding, and fluid collections (acute peripancreatic fluid collections, pseudocysts, walled-off necrosis).
  - CT findings in diverticulitis: Colonic diverticula (outpouchings) with wall thickening, pericolic fat stranding.
  - CT angiography (CTA) of the abdomen: The first-line test for suspected acute mesenteric ischemia. Findings: filling defect in the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) from an embolus or thrombus, or occlusion. The bowel wall may show pneumatosis intestinalis (air in the bowel wall) indicating infarction.
- **Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)**:
  - A heavily T2-weighted MRI sequence that visualizes the static fluid in the biliary and pancreatic ducts without the need for contrast or endoscopy. It is the best non-invasive test for choledocholithiasis (bile duct stones) and for evaluating biliary strictures (PSC, cholangiocarcinoma). Sensitivity for common bile duct stones is 90-95%, specificity 95%.
- **Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS)**:
  - A high-frequency ultrasound probe mounted on the tip of an endoscope. It is placed in the stomach or duodenum, immediately adjacent to the pancreas, bile duct, and gallbladder. It is the most sensitive test for small common bile duct stones (<5 mm, missed by MRCP), for pancreatic tumors (detects tumors <1 cm and allows fine-needle aspiration), and for local staging of esophageal, gastric, and rectal cancers (assesses the depth of wall invasion — T stage — and regional lymph node involvement — N stage).

### Endoscopic Investigations

- **Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)**: Direct visualization of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum. Indications: dysphagia, odynophagia, chronic GERD (for Barrett's esophagus screening), hematemesis, melena, iron-deficiency anemia, epigastric pain/dyspepsia unresponsive to treatment, persistent vomiting. It allows biopsy, polypectomy, variceal band ligation, and hemostasis (injection of epinephrine, clipping, thermal coagulation). It is the gold standard for diagnosing peptic ulcer disease, esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, esophageal and gastric cancer, and celiac disease (duodenal biopsy showing villous atrophy).
- **Colonoscopy**: Direct visualization of the entire colon and terminal ileum. Indications: colorectal cancer screening (starting at age 45 in average-risk adults), hematochezia, melena (if EGD is negative), iron-deficiency anemia, chronic diarrhea, evaluation of an abnormal CT or barium enema, surveillance of IBD. It allows biopsy, polypectomy, and stricture dilation. It is the gold standard for colorectal cancer and adenoma detection. The bowel requires a complete cleansing preparation (polyethylene glycol, split-dose regimen). The adenoma detection rate (ADR) is the primary quality metric for the endoscopist.
- **Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)**: A side-viewing duodenoscope is used to cannulate the ampulla of Vater. Radiopaque contrast is injected retrograde into the common bile duct and pancreatic duct, and fluoroscopic images are obtained. It is primarily a therapeutic procedure, not diagnostic. Indications: choledocholithiasis (stone extraction with a balloon or basket after sphincterotomy), biliary obstruction from malignancy (stent placement for decompression), bile leak (sphincterotomy to reduce the trans-sphincteric pressure gradient, promoting internal drainage). Complications include post-ERCP pancreatitis (3-5%), bleeding, perforation, and cholangitis. MRCP is preferred for diagnosis; ERCP is reserved for therapy.
- **Video Capsule Endoscopy**: The patient swallows a pill-sized camera that transmits images as it traverses the GI tract. It is the gold standard for evaluating the small bowel (the "black box" not reachable by standard EGD or colonoscopy). Indications: obscure GI bleeding (after negative EGD and colonoscopy), suspected Crohn's disease of the small bowel, and small bowel tumors. Contraindication: suspected stricture or obstruction (risk of capsule retention).

### Physiological Tests

- **Esophageal Manometry**: A catheter with pressure sensors is passed transnasally into the esophagus. It measures the pressure, coordination, and relaxation of the esophageal body and the lower and upper esophageal sphincters. Indications: diagnosis of achalasia (absent peristalsis with failed LES relaxation, elevated integrated relaxation pressure), evaluation of dysphagia after a normal endoscopy and biopsy, and prior to anti-reflux surgery to ensure the diagnosis is GERD and not an alternative motor disorder.
- **Ambulatory 24-hour pH Monitoring (or 96-hour Wireless pH Capsule / Bravo)**: A transnasal pH probe is placed 5 cm above the manometrically defined LES, or a wireless capsule is endoscopically attached to the distal esophagus. The test measures the total percentage of time the esophageal pH is <4.0. It is the gold standard for objectively diagnosing GERD when endoscopy is normal and symptoms are atypical (non-cardiac chest pain, chronic cough, laryngopharyngeal reflux). A DeMeester score (composite score of six pH parameters) >14.7 is diagnostic of pathological acid reflux.
- **Anorectal Manometry**: A balloon-tipped catheter measures the resting and squeeze pressures of the anal sphincter complex, the rectoanal inhibitory reflex (RAIR; the reflex relaxation of the internal anal sphincter upon distension of the rectum, which is absent in Hirschsprung's disease), rectal sensation (threshold volumes for first sensation, urge, and maximum tolerable volume), and the ability to expel a balloon filled with 50 mL of water (balloon expulsion test). Indicated for the evaluation of chronic constipation and fecal incontinence.
- **Hydrogen Breath Test**: The patient ingests a defined dose of a carbohydrate substrate (lactose, glucose, or lactulose) and samples of exhaled breath are collected at intervals. The colonic bacterial fermentation of unabsorbed carbohydrate produces hydrogen gas, which diffuses into the bloodstream and is exhaled. A rise in breath hydrogen >20 ppm from baseline indicates either carbohydrate malabsorption (e.g., lactose intolerance for a lactose test) or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO, for a glucose or lactulose test where an early peak indicates proximal bacterial fermentation in the small intestine). It is simple, non-invasive, and widely used.

## Fifth: Assessment of GI System Health and Screening

### Clinical Assessment of a Healthy GI System

- No dysphagia, odynophagia, or globus.
- No heartburn or regurgitation.
- No abdominal pain, bloating, or distension.
- Normal appetite, no early satiety.
- Bowel habit: regular, formed, brown stools without blood or mucus. Normal frequency ranges from 3 per day to 3 per week.
- No nausea or vomiting.
- No jaundice, pruritus, or skin stigmata of liver disease.
- Physical examination: Soft, non-tender, non-distended abdomen. No organomegaly. Normal bowel sounds. No hernias. Normal DRE.

### Screening Procedures for GI Diseases

- **Colorectal Cancer Screening**:
  - *Average-risk adults (without family history or genetic syndrome)*: Begin screening at age 45 (as per the 2021 ACS and 2021 USPSTF recommendations; some guidelines use age 50). Options: (1) Colonoscopy every 10 years (the gold standard; detection and prevention by polypectomy); (2) FIT annually (fecal immunochemical test, highly sensitive for CRC and advanced adenomas); (3) Multitarget stool DNA test (Cologuard) every 3 years (combines FIT with DNA methylation markers); (4) Flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years; (5) CT colonography every 5 years. A positive stool-based test or CT colonography mandates a colonoscopy.
  - *First-degree relative with CRC or advanced adenoma diagnosed at age <60*: Colonoscopy starting at age 40, or 10 years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis age, repeat every 5 years.
  - *Hereditary syndromes*: Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer): colonoscopy every 1-2 years starting at age 20-25 (or 2-5 years before the earliest CRC in the family). Familial adenomatous polyposis: flexible sigmoidoscopy annually starting at age 10-12; once polyps are identified, colectomy is planned. Inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis, Crohn's colitis): colonoscopy with chromoendoscopy (spraying indigo carmine or methylene blue to highlight dysplastic lesions) and systematic random biopsies every 1-2 years after 8 years of disease duration.
- **Screening for Esophageal Adenocarcinoma**:
  - GERD with additional risk factors (white male >50 years, chronic GERD >5 years, hiatal hernia, central obesity, smoking history): one-time screening endoscopy to evaluate for Barrett's esophagus. If Barrett's is found with intestinal metaplasia and no dysplasia, surveillance endoscopy every 3-5 years; low-grade dysplasia, every 6-12 months; high-grade dysplasia, endoscopic eradication (radiofrequency ablation or endoscopic mucosal resection).
- **Screening for Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)**:
  - Cirrhotic patients (any etiology) and non-cirrhotic patients with chronic hepatitis B (Asian male >40 years, Asian female >50 years, family history of HCC, or African origin over 20 years): abdominal ultrasound with or without serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) every 6 months. This screening reduces HCC-related mortality by 37%. A liver lesion >1 cm found on surveillance ultrasound warrants cross-sectional imaging (multiphase CT or MRI with contrast) for definitive diagnosis.
- **Screening for Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**:
  - CDC and USPSTF recommend one-time, universal screening for all adults aged 18-79 years. The anti-HCV antibody test is used; if positive, HCV RNA PCR confirms active infection. The rationale is the high cure rate with direct-acting antivirals and the long asymptomatic period during which liver damage (cirrhosis) occurs silently.
- **Screening for Celiac Disease**:
  - Not recommended for the general asymptomatic population. Screen symptomatic patients (chronic diarrhea, weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, osteoporosis, dermatitis herpetiformis, infertility, family history of celiac disease) with tTG-IgA and total serum IgA.

## Sixth: Diseases of the Gastrointestinal System

**1. Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)**

*Pathogenesis*: Pathological reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus, causing troublesome symptoms or complications. The primary motility abnormality is an increased frequency of transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations (TLESRs), which are normal vagally mediated reflexes that permit belching. In GERD, these occur independently of swallowing and when the stomach is distended. A hypotensive LES at rest, a hiatal hernia (which traps acid in the hernia sac and impairs the crural diaphragm's external sphincter function), obesity (increased intra-abdominal pressure), impaired esophageal clearance (peristaltic dysfunction, hyposalivation), and delayed gastric emptying all contribute. The esophagus's stratified squamous epithelium, lacking the protective mucus layer and tight junctions of the stomach, is vulnerable to acid-peptic injury. Chronic reflux leads to Barrett's esophagus (replacement of the damaged squamous epithelium with an acid-resistant, specialized columnar intestinal metaplasia), which is the obligate precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma.[1]

*Typical Presentation*: Heartburn (retrosternal burning radiating upward, postprandial and supine) and regurgitation (effortless return of sour or bitter-tasting fluid into the mouth). Atypical symptoms: non-cardiac chest pain (can precisely mimic angina), chronic cough (from microaspiration or vagally mediated esophagobronchial reflex), globus sensation, hoarseness (reflux laryngitis), and dental erosions on the lingual surfaces (acid damage to enamel).

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: Symptoms are reliably triggered by recumbency, large meals, bending, and are relieved (at least transiently) by antacids. A PPI trial (empiric proton pump inhibitor for 8 weeks) is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Definitive diagnosis if atypical or refractory: ambulatory pH monitoring showing an esophageal acid exposure time >6% (pH <4 for >6% of the 24-hour period).

**2. Achalasia**

*Pathogenesis*: A primary esophageal motility disorder characterized by the progressive loss of the inhibitory nitrergic neurons (neurons that produce nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide, VIP) in the myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus of the esophageal body and lower esophageal sphincter. The cause of this selective neuronal loss is unknown but is likely an autoimmune process triggered by a viral infection (possibly HSV-1 or varicella zoster) in genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DQw1). The loss of NO-mediated inhibition results in two pathophysiological hallmarks: (1) failure of the LES to relax during swallowing (due to unopposed cholinergic excitation, causing a tonically contracted, hypertensive LES), and (2) absent peristalsis in the body of the esophagus (because the inhibitory neurons are required for the normal descending inhibition that propels the peristaltic wave).[1]

*Typical Presentation*: Progressive dysphagia, paradoxically more for liquids than solids early in the disease (unlike mechanical obstruction where solids always cause more difficulty). This is a critical distinguishing feature. The patient describes food "sticking" in the retrosternal area, which they may try to wash down with water. Regurgitation of undigested, non-acidic, non-bilious food and saliva (because the food never reached the stomach) is common, especially at night ("nocturnal pillow soiling"). Retrosternal chest pain from esophageal spasm in early achalasia. Weight loss from inability to eat. Aspiration pneumonia from nocturnal regurgitation. A long-standing history (years) of dysphagia is typical of achalasia or a benign stricture, whereas a short history (weeks to months) with rapid progression is classic of esophageal cancer.[1]

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: Dysphagia to liquids equal to or worse than solids. Barium swallow shows a "bird's beak" sign (smooth, symmetric tapering of the distal esophagus at the gastroesophageal junction with a dilated, tortuous esophageal body). Esophageal manometry is the gold standard: absent peristalsis and impaired LES relaxation (elevated integrated relaxation pressure).

**3. Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)**

*Pathogenesis*: An imbalance between aggressive factors (gastric acid, pepsin) and defensive factors (the gastric and duodenal mucus-bicarbonate barrier, epithelial restitution, prostaglandins, and mucosal blood flow). The two most common etiologies are: (1) Helicobacter pylori infection (70-90% of duodenal ulcers, 50-70% of gastric ulcers): The spiral bacterium resides in the mucus layer overlying the gastric epithelium. Its urease produces ammonia from urea, neutralizing gastric acid in its microenvironment and allowing it to survive. It injects the CagA protein into gastric epithelial cells via a type IV secretion system, disrupting the tight junctions, promoting inflammation (gastritis with a predominant Th1 and Th17 response with IL-8 release), and inhibiting the host's immune clearance. H. pylori induces hypergastrinemia by destroying the somatostatin-producing D cells in the gastric antrum. Elevated gastrin stimulates the parietal cells to secrete excessive acid, which is delivered to the duodenal bulb, causing gastric metaplasia and then ulceration. (2) NSAIDs: They inhibit cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), which produces prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2) that are critical for maintaining the gastric mucosal barrier, stimulating mucus and bicarbonate secretion, and maintaining mucosal blood flow. Without prostaglandins, the mucosa is vulnerable to acid back-diffusion and subsequent injury.[1]

*Typical Presentation*: Epigastric pain, described as "gnawing" or "burning," that has a rhythmic temporal relationship to meals. Duodenal ulcers classically cause pain that awakens the patient at night (from the circadian peak in acid secretion), and the pain is relieved within minutes by eating or taking antacids (food buffers the gastric acid). In contrast, gastric ulcers cause pain that is exacerbated by eating (as the meal distends the stomach and stimulates acid secretion directly onto the ulcer bed). Nausea, vomiting, bloating, and dyspepsia are common. The natural history is chronic, relapsing, and remitting.

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: The temporal meal-pain relationship (duodenal: pain-food-relief pattern; gastric: pain-food-pain pattern). Complications are the presenting feature in up to 25% of patients: melena (chronic bleeding), hematemesis (acute bleeding from erosion into the gastroduodenal artery, a branch of the common hepatic artery in the posterior duodenal bulb — the most feared complication), and an acute abdomen with board-like rigidity from a perforated ulcer (most commonly in the anterior duodenal bulb). EGD with antral and corpus biopsies (for H. pylori) and a urease-based rapid test (CLO test) is the diagnostic gold standard.

**4. Celiac Disease**

*Pathogenesis*: An immune-mediated systemic disorder triggered by dietary gluten (the gliadin fraction of wheat gluten and related prolamins in rye, barley, and contaminated oats) in genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8, which are necessary but not sufficient). The enzyme tissue transglutaminase (tTG) in the lamina propria deamidates gluten peptides, converting glutamine residues to glutamic acid, which introduces negative charges. These deamidated peptides fit with high affinity into the peptide-binding groove of the HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 molecules on antigen-presenting cells. The gluten-HLA complex activates CD4+ T helper cells, which produce a Th1 cytokine profile (IFN-γ, IL-21). This T-cell response orchestrates the destruction of the small intestinal mucosa: intraepithelial lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells) infiltrate and kill enterocytes via the NKG2D-MICA pathway, and the villous architecture is destroyed. The result is total or subtotal villous atrophy with crypt hyperplasia, leading to malabsorption of nutrients, iron, calcium, and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).[1]

*Typical Presentation*: The classic presentation is chronic diarrhea with steatorrhea, abdominal distension, bloating, and failure to thrive in an infant after the introduction of gluten-containing cereals. In adults, iron-deficiency anemia (from impaired iron absorption in the duodenum) is the single most common presentation, and celiac disease must be excluded in every patient with unexplained iron deficiency. Other presentations: osteoporosis or osteomalacia (vitamin D and calcium malabsorption), dermatitis herpetiformis (an intensely pruritic, blistering, papulovesicular rash on the extensor surfaces of the elbows, knees, and buttocks, pathognomonic for celiac disease and caused by IgA deposition in the dermal papillae), infertility, recurrent aphthous ulcers, and elevated transaminases (celiac hepatitis). Many patients are asymptomatic (silent celiac disease).

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: Elevated anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) with a serum IgA level to confirm no IgA deficiency. Duodenal biopsy (via EGD) is the gold standard: the histology shows villous atrophy with crypt hyperplasia and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes (Marsh stage 3). A definitive diagnosis requires histological improvement and serological normalization on a gluten-free diet. The response to a gluten-free diet is dramatic and diagnostic.

**5. Acute Pancreatitis**

*Pathogenesis*: Premature, intracellular activation of the digestive proenzymes (zymogens) within the pancreatic acinar cells. Normally, pancreatic proteases are synthesized as inactive proenzymes (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase) and are packaged in zymogen granules, separated from the lysosomal hydrolases. In pancreatitis, the zymogen granules colocalize with lysosomes in the acinar cell. Lysosomal cathepsin B cleaves trypsinogen to trypsin, and the trypsin then activates all other proenzymes, initiating autodigestion of the pancreatic parenchyma. The two most common triggers are gallstones (40-70%) and alcohol (25-35%). Gallstone pancreatitis: a stone passing from the gallbladder through the common bile duct transiently obstructs the ampulla of Vater, causing bile reflux into the pancreatic duct or edema that obstructs the pancreatic duct. Alcohol: ethanol directly stimulates pancreatic acinar secretion, causes spasm of the sphincter of Oddi, and precipitates protein plugs that obstruct the small pancreatic ductules. Once acinar injury begins, the release of cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8) and chemokines recruits neutrophils, which release reactive oxygen species and proteases, amplifying the necrosis. The severe form is necrotizing pancreatitis, which has a 20-30% mortality rate.[1]

*Typical Presentation*: Sudden, severe, constant epigastric pain that radiates directly through the back (in a band-like pattern) and is relieved (characteristically) by leaning forward (the "Mohammedan praying position") because this reduces the tension on the retroperitoneal pancreas. Nausea and vomiting are prominent. In severe pancreatitis: hypotension, tachycardia, fever, and tachypnea (SIRS or sepsis). Physical signs: mild epigastric tenderness in mild interstitial pancreatitis; Cullen's sign and Grey Turner's sign in severe necrotizing pancreatitis (late signs). A palpable epigastric mass suggests a pancreatic pseudocyst (a walled-off collection of enzyme-rich fluid that takes >4 weeks to form). The Ranson criteria, APACHE II, or the Bedside Index of Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP) score are used to predict severity.

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: Serum lipase >3 times the upper limit of normal (more specific and sensitive than amylase because lipase is almost exclusively pancreatic; it rises within 4-8 hours, peaks at 24 hours, and remains elevated for 1-2 weeks). A contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen with a pancreatic protocol is the imaging gold standard for assessing necrosis and complications, performed 48-72 hours after admission (peak necrosis occurs by day 3). The pancreas appears edematous with peripancreatic fat stranding and fluid collections. Normal lipase effectively excludes acute pancreatitis.

**6. Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis)**

*Pathogenesis*: A chronic, relapsing immune-mediated inflammation of the intestinal tract. The prevailing model involves a dysregulated mucosal immune response to the commensal intestinal microbiota in a genetically susceptible host, triggered by environmental factors. Genetic susceptibility: NOD2/CARD15 mutations impair the intracellular sensing of muramyl dipeptide (a component of bacterial peptidoglycan) by Paneth cells and macrophages, reducing the production of antimicrobial defensins and impairing autophagy, leading to the persistence of intracellular bacteria. The adaptive immune response in Crohn's disease is a Th1/Th17 pattern with excess IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-23, and IL-17. In ulcerative colitis, the response is an atypical Th2 pattern with increased IL-5 and IL-13, and the epithelial barrier itself is defective (impaired mucus barrier from MUC2 mutations, defective tight junctions). The intestinal microbiome is altered in both diseases (dysbiosis), with a reduced diversity and an expansion of pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria. TNF-α is a central orchestrating cytokine in both diseases, promoting granuloma formation (pathognomonic for Crohn's), matrix metalloproteinase-mediated tissue destruction, and angiogenesis.[1]

*Typical Presentation*:

- *Crohn's Disease*: Can affect any part of the GI tract from mouth to anus, but the terminal ileum is most commonly involved (ileocolitis). The classic triad is right lower quadrant abdominal pain (from transmural inflammation and stricturing of the terminal ileum), chronic diarrhea (non-bloody unless the colon is involved), and weight loss. The disease is characterized by skip lesions (areas of normal mucosa between diseased segments), transmural inflammation (extending through all layers of the bowel wall), and granulomas (non-caseating, seen in 30-50% of biopsies). The complications are strictures (from fibrosis, causing obstruction) and fistulas (the transmural inflammation creates a sinus tract that connects to another loop of bowel, the bladder, the skin, or the perianal area). Perianal disease (fissures, abscesses, complex fistulas, and large, edematous skin tags) is present in up to 40% of patients and is highly suggestive of Crohn's disease. Extraintestinal manifestations: erythema nodosum (red, tender subcutaneous nodules on the shins), pyoderma gangrenosum (a deep, necrotic ulcer with undermined violaceous edges, typically on the leg), ankylosing spondylitis, sacroiliitis (HLA-B27 associated), uveitis, episcleritis, and primary sclerosing cholangitis (more commonly with UC, but also in Crohn's colitis).
- *Ulcerative Colitis*: The inflammation is confined to the mucosa and submucosa of the colon, always starting in the rectum (proctitis) and extending proximally in a continuous, circumferential pattern without skip lesions. The classic presentation is bloody diarrhea with urgency and tenesmus. The stool is frequent (10-20 small-volume bloody bowel movements per day), mixed with mucus and pus, and the blood is mixed with the stool (unlike the hematochezia of hemorrhoids, which coats the stool or is on the toilet paper). Abdominal pain is less prominent; when present, it is crampy, left-sided, and relieved by defecation. Severe disease: >6 bloody stools per day with systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated inflammatory markers); toxic megacolon is a life-threatening complication where the colon dilates >6 cm transabdominally (or cecum >9 cm) with systemic toxicity; the inflammation and nitric oxide-mediated smooth muscle relaxation cause the colon to dilate, lose its tone, and risk perforation.
- *Key Distinguishing Feature*: Colonoscopy with ileoscopy and biopsy is the gold standard. In UC, the rectum is always involved; the inflammation is continuous, circumferential, granular, friable, and ulcerated, with loss of the normal vascular pattern. Biopsies show crypt architectural distortion, crypt abscesses, basal plasmacytosis, and a diffuse mixed lamina propria infiltrate. In Crohn's, the rectum is often spared, the inflammation is discontinuous (skip lesions) and segmental, with aphthous ulcers, a cobblestone appearance (criss-crossing longitudinal and transverse ulcers with islands of edematous mucosa), and the terminal ileum is often nodular and stenosed. Biopsies show transmural inflammation with lymphoid aggregates and non-caseating granulomas. Fecal calprotectin is the best non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation to distinguish IBD from IBS and to monitor disease activity.

**7. Acute Appendicitis**

*Pathogenesis*: Luminal obstruction of the appendix is the inciting event in 50-80% of cases. Causes of obstruction: a fecalith (a hard, calcified mass of stool), lymphoid hyperplasia (in children and adolescents, often post-viral), foreign body, or neoplasm (carcinoid tumor at the tip causing obstruction). The appendix is a narrow, blind-ended tube with a small lumen. Once obstructed, the mucosal secretions accumulate, and the appendix distends. The intraluminal pressure rises, impairing venous and lymphatic drainage and causing edema. The distension stimulates the visceral afferent pain fibers, producing the initial poorly localized periumbilical pain. As pressure exceeds capillary perfusion pressure, the mucosa becomes ischemic and ulcerates. The normal mucosal barrier is breached, and the luminal bacteria (E. coli, Bacteroides fragilis, Pseudomonas) invade the wall. The inflammatory response intensifies (neutrophilic infiltration of the wall), and a serosal exudate of fibrin and pus forms, which irritates the parietal peritoneum. The pain then localizes to the right lower quadrant (the somatic, precisely localized phase). If untreated, the necrotic wall perforates, releasing the luminal contents and pus into the peritoneal cavity (generalized peritonitis) or forming a localized abscess (appendix mass, or phlegmon) if the omentum and adjacent bowel loops wall off the infection. Perforation risk is directly related to time: <10% at 24 hours, 15-50% at 36 hours, >80% at 48-72 hours. The appendix's blood supply is the appendicular artery, a terminal branch of the ileocolic artery. Because it is an end-artery, thrombosis leads to gangrenous necrosis and rapid perforation.[1]

*Typical Presentation*: The classic history (present in only 50% of patients) is the migration of pain: diffuse, crampy, periumbilical pain of visceral origin, followed by nausea, vomiting, and anorexia. Within 4-6 hours, the pain localizes to the right lower quadrant (at McBurney's point) and becomes constant, sharper, and worse with movement (parietal peritoneal irritation). The patient lies still, often with the hips flexed (to relax the psoas and iliacus muscles crossing the inflamed area). Physical examination: low-grade fever (37.5-38.5°C; a high fever >39°C suggests perforation or an alternative diagnosis), focal tenderness with guarding at McBurney's point, percussion tenderness, positive Rovsing's sign, positive psoas sign (retrocecal appendix), positive obturator sign (pelvic appendix). Laboratory: leukocytosis (12,000-18,000 WBC/µL) with a left shift (bandemia). CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast is the gold-standard imaging test (sensitivity 95-98%, specificity 95-100%). Findings: dilated, fluid-filled appendix >6 mm in diameter, wall thickening, periappendiceal fat stranding, and possibly an appendicolith. Ultrasound is the first-line test in children and pregnant women to avoid radiation (sensitivity 75-90%, specificity 85-95%).

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: The dynamic, progressive, temporal sequence: diffuse periumbilical pain → anorexia and nausea → localized RLQ pain. This temporal evolution, when present, is highly specific for appendicitis. A fixed, tender, non-compressible appendix >6 mm on ultrasound or CT is the imaging hallmark.

**8. Colorectal Cancer**

*Pathogenesis*: Colorectal cancer (CRC) arises from the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic mutations in the colonic epithelial stem cells, transforming normal epithelium into an adenomatous polyp and then into invasive carcinoma over a period of 5-15 years (the adenoma-carcinoma sequence). There are three major molecular pathways:

- *Chromosomal Instability (CIN) Pathway (70-85% of sporadic CRCs)*: The classic adenoma-carcinoma sequence. The initiating mutation is typically in the APC (Adenomatous Polyposis Coli) tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 5q. The APC protein is a key scaffold in the β-catenin destruction complex; loss of APC function leads to accumulation of β-catenin, which translocates to the nucleus, binds TCF/LEF transcription factors, and drives the transcription of pro-proliferation genes (c-MYC, cyclin D1). This drives the formation of a tubular adenoma. Subsequent mutations accumulate in KRAS (uncontrolled proliferation, progression to a larger, more dysplastic adenoma), loss of 18q (SMAD4 and DCC, advancing to high-grade dysplasia), and finally, loss of TP53 (the p53 protein is the guardian of the genome; its loss allows the accumulation of additional mutations and the transition from adenoma to invasive carcinoma).
- *Microsatellite Instability (MSI) Pathway (15% of sporadic CRCs)*: This results from a failure of the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system to correct errors that occur during DNA replication (slippage errors, especially in repetitive microsatellite sequences). In the sporadic form, the promoter of the MLH1 gene is hypermethylated (CpG island methylator phenotype, CIMP), epigenetically silencing it. In Lynch syndrome, there is a germline mutation in one of the MMR genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2). The failure to repair frameshift mutations in microsatellites within the coding regions of genes like TGF-βRII and BAX leads to their inactivation and promotes carcinogenesis. MSI-high tumors are more commonly right-sided, poorly differentiated, mucinous, with a prominent tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) response, and have a better prognosis (because the immune system recognizes the abundant neoantigens generated by the hypermutation).
- *Serrated Pathway*: Arises from sessile serrated adenomas/polyps, which are flat, pale, mucus-cap-covered lesions in the right colon, driven by BRAF V600E mutations and CIMP (extensive promoter hypermethylation). Progression to carcinoma is rapid.[1]
- *Typical Presentation*: Right-sided colon cancers (cecum, ascending colon): present late because the stool is liquid and does not obstruct. Iron-deficiency anemia from chronic, occult blood loss (fatigue, pallor, dyspnea on exertion) is the classic presentation. Occult fecal blood. Left-sided colon cancers (descending, sigmoid): present with a change in bowel habit (especially progressive constipation or pencil-thin stools), hematochezia (bright red blood coating the stool), and symptoms of obstruction (colicky abdominal pain, distension, obstipation). Rectal cancers: hematochezia, tenesmus, and a sensation of incomplete evacuation are the cardinal symptoms. The patient feels an urge to defecate but passes only a small volume of blood and mucus. Any patient over 45 (or any adult with significant symptoms) with new-onset iron-deficiency anemia or hematochezia must be evaluated for colorectal cancer. Advanced disease presents with weight loss, cachexia, a palpable abdominal mass, or liver metastases (the colon's venous drainage is the portal vein, so the liver is the first site of hematogenous metastasis).[1]
- *Key Distinguishing Feature*: Iron-deficiency anemia in an older adult (men >45 years, postmenopausal women) is colorectal cancer until proven otherwise by complete colonoscopy. Colonoscopy is the diagnostic gold standard. The tumor is an exophytic, fungating mass in the right colon, and an annular, "apple-core" constricting lesion in the left colon (seen on double-contrast barium enema or CT colonography). A biopsy confirming invasive adenocarcinoma is required for diagnosis. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) is a serum tumor marker that is not used for diagnosis (poor sensitivity and specificity) but is used for post-resection surveillance (a rising CEA suggests recurrence). Staging is with CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, and for rectal cancer, pelvic MRI or EUS for local T and N staging.

**9. Liver Cirrhosis**

*Pathogenesis*: Cirrhosis is the end-stage, irreversible consequence of chronic liver injury from any cause. The pathological hallmark is diffuse hepatic fibrosis that distorts the normal lobular architecture into structurally abnormal regenerative nodules, with loss of normal sinusoidal fenestrations. The hepatic stellate cell (Ito cell), located in the space of Disse, is the central effector of fibrosis. In response to chronic injury and inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, PDGF, TNF-α from Kupffer cells, hepatocytes, and infiltrating lymphocytes), the quiescent, vitamin A-storing stellate cell undergoes activation, transforming into a myofibroblast. It loses its vitamin A, proliferates, becomes contractile (expressing α-smooth muscle actin), and produces massive amounts of extracellular matrix (collagen types I and III, fibronectin, proteoglycans). The extracellular matrix accumulates in the space of Disse, forming a basement membrane that was not previously present (capillarization of the sinusoids). This physical barrier impedes the diffusion of substrates between the hepatocyte and the sinusoidal blood, causing hepatocyte dysfunction and portal hypertension. At the same time, the organized fibrous septa that connect portal tracts to central veins form vascular shunts that bypass the hepatocytes entirely. Portal hypertension results from both an increase in intrahepatic resistance (the fixed component from fibrosis and a dynamic component from stellate cell contraction and a deficiency of intrahepatic nitric oxide) and an increase in splanchnic blood flow (from splanchnic vasodilation, mediated by an excess of circulating vasodilators — nitric oxide, carbon monoxide, endocannabinoids — produced as a result of the portosystemic shunting and systemic inflammation). The hyperdynamic circulatory syndrome further increases portal inflow, worsening the portal hypertension. The most common causes are alcoholic liver disease, chronic hepatitis C that has progressed to cirrhosis, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which is now the leading cause due to the obesity pandemic.[1]

*Typical Presentation*: Cirrhosis has two clinical stages: compensated (the patient is asymptomatic or has non-specific symptoms, with the diagnosis made incidentally by imaging, lab abnormalities, or at endoscopy) and decompensated (the development of clinically evident complications of portal hypertension and liver failure). Decompensation is defined by the onset of ascites, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice. Ascites presents with progressive abdominal distension, which may be accompanied by dyspnea from diaphragmatic splinting and early satiety. Esophageal varices: asymptomatic until they rupture, causing a massive, life-threatening upper GI bleed (painless, copious hematemesis and melena, and hemodynamic collapse). Hepatic encephalopathy: a spectrum of neuropsychiatric abnormalities ranging from subtle changes in sleep-wake cycle, personality changes, and asterixis to confusion, disorientation, stupor, and coma. Jaundice: a late and ominous sign of decompensation. Hepatocellular carcinoma is a lethal complication of cirrhosis (risk is 1-4% per year for all etiologies, highest in HBV-related cirrhosis). Physical signs: stigmata of chronic liver disease (spider angiomata, palmar erythema, gynecomastia, testicular atrophy in men, loss of axillary and pubic hair). The liver texture on palpation is firm, nodular, and may be enlarged or shrunken. Splenomegaly, caput medusae, ascites, and asterixis are signs of portal hypertension and decompensation.

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: The presence of portal hypertension (esophageal varices, ascites, splenomegaly, caput medusae) with a firm, nodular liver, a prolonged INR that does not correct with vitamin K, thrombocytopenia (from hypersplenism and reduced thrombopoietin production), and a low serum albumin. A liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosis and staging, but it is invasive; the definitive non-invasive method is a FibroScan (transient elastography), which measures liver stiffness in kilopascals (kPa). A value >12.5 kPa is highly suggestive of cirrhosis. Ultrasound reveals a nodular surface, a coarse, heterogeneous echotexture, and a dilated portal vein (>13 mm).

**10. Cholecystitis (Acute Calculous Cholecystitis)**

*Pathogenesis*: The initiating event is persistent cystic duct obstruction by an impacted gallstone, preventing the gallbladder from emptying. The bile stasis leads to chemical irritation of the gallbladder mucosa by the concentrated bile salts (especially lysolecithin) and an increase in intraluminal pressure that impedes venous and lymphatic drainage, causing mural edema. The mucosal injury releases phospholipase A2 from the mucosal cells, which hydrolyzes biliary lecithin to lysolecithin, a toxic compound that directly injures the mucosa. The ischemic, injured, distended gallbladder wall becomes secondarily infected by enteric bacteria (E. coli, Enterococcus, Klebsiella, and anaerobes, especially Clostridium perfringens) in 50-75% of cases. The inflammatory response is mediated by prostaglandins, which cause the smooth muscle spasm (biliary colic) and the subsequent somatic pain as the inflamed serosa contacts the parietal peritoneum. The gallbladder wall becomes thickened, edematous, hyperemic, and, if untreated, necrotic and gangrenous (which can result in perforation and biliary peritonitis). Emphysematous cholecystitis is a variant where gas-forming organisms (C. perfringens) produce gas in the wall of the gallbladder, seen on CT or plain film as air in the gallbladder wall. It has a high mortality rate and requires emergent surgery.

*Typical Presentation*: Acute onset of severe, constant right upper quadrant pain that lasts >6 hours, often radiating to the right scapula or shoulder tip (due to phrenic nerve irritation from the inflamed diaphragm). This pain is distinct from the intermittent, self-limited biliary colic (which resolves within 6 hours) because the obstruction is now persistent and the organ is inflamed. Fever (38-39°C), nausea, vomiting, and anorexia are common. Physical examination: the patient is ill-appearing and febrile. A positive Murphy's sign is the pathognomonic sign. A palpable, tender, distended gallbladder mass is present in 30-40% of cases. Laboratory findings: leukocytosis with a left shift. Mild elevations of bilirubin, ALP, and aminotransferases may occur from the adjacent inflammation involving the common bile duct; however, a markedly elevated bilirubin or a dilated common bile duct suggests a concomitant common bile duct stone (choledocholithiasis with ascending cholangitis). Ultrasound is the first-line and most sensitive test: findings include gallbladder wall thickening >3 mm, pericholecystic fluid, a sonographic Murphy's sign (focal tenderness when the transducer is pressed over the gallbladder), and an impacted stone in the cystic duct or gallbladder neck. A HIDA scan (cholescintigraphy) is definitive when ultrasound is equivocal: the radiotracer (99mTc-HIDA) is taken up by hepatocytes and excreted into bile, but a non-visualized gallbladder 4 hours after injection (and after morphine augmentation) indicates cystic duct obstruction. This has a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 99%.[1]

*Key Distinguishing Feature*: Acute, sustained RUQ pain >6 hours with fever and Murphy's sign, combined with ultrasound findings of a distended, thick-walled gallbladder with an impacted stone. The combination of a positive sonographic Murphy's sign and a gallstone on ultrasound has a positive predictive value >90% for acute cholecystitis.

## Seventh: Emergency Conditions of the Gastrointestinal System

**1. Acute Esophageal Variceal Hemorrhage**

*Overview*: The most lethal complication of portal hypertension, with a mortality rate of 15-20% per episode. Varices are dilated, thin-walled portosystemic collateral veins in the submucosa of the distal esophagus, which develop when the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) exceeds 10 mmHg. Bleeding risk increases at an HVPG >12 mmHg. The veins have no external support tissue. Rupture occurs when the wall tension exceeds the tensile strength of the varix (LaPlace's law: wall tension = [transmural pressure × radius] / wall thickness). Therefore, large varices (large radius) with high portal pressure and thin walls are at highest risk of rupture.

*Red Flags*: Painless, massive, sudden upper GI bleeding with hematemesis (bright red blood because the bleeding is rapid and large volume; it is not coffee-ground) and melena. There is hemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia, shock). The patient may have stigmata of chronic liver disease (jaundice, ascites, spider angiomata). The absence of epigastric pain and the massive volume of hemorrhage distinguish variceal hemorrhage from a bleeding peptic ulcer.

*Detection*: Emergency EGD (within 12 hours of presentation) is the diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard. Active bleeding is visualized: a spurting or oozing varix, or a "white nipple sign" (a fibrin plug on a varix that has recently bled). Management: Resuscitation (cautious volume resuscitation with crystalloid and packed RBCs to target hemoglobin of 7-8 g/dL; over-resuscitation increases portal pressure and worsens bleeding), vasoactive drugs (octreotide or terlipressin, which are splanchnic vasoconstrictors that reduce portal inflow and portal pressure), prophylactic antibiotics (ceftriaxone for 7 days to prevent spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and other infections, which occur in 20-50% of cirrhotic patients with variceal bleeding), and endoscopic variceal band ligation.

**2. Perforated Peptic Ulcer**

*Overview*: A full-thickness defect in the wall of a gastric or duodenal ulcer, allowing the spillage of acidic gastroduodenal contents, air, and bacteria into the peritoneal cavity, causing acute generalized peritonitis. The anterior duodenal bulb is the most common site. The initial chemical peritonitis from the acidic gastric juice is intensely painful, but after a few hours, the pain may transiently improve, only to be followed by bacterial peritonitis (from the growth of enteric organisms). Surgical repair is mandatory within 12-24 hours.

*Red Flags*: A patient with a known history of PUD or NSAID use who suddenly develops excruciating, knifelike, periumbilical or epigastric pain that quickly becomes generalized. The patient lies rigid and immobile, with shallow breaths (any movement stretches the inflamed peritoneum and worsens the pain). The abdominal wall is board-like and rigid (involuntary guarding) without any ability to relax. Rebound tenderness is present, and bowel sounds are absent (paralytic ileus from the chemical peritonitis).

*Detection*: An upright chest X-ray (or left lateral decubitus abdominal film if the patient cannot stand) demonstrates pneumoperitoneum (free air under the diaphragm) in 75-80% of cases, but its absence does not exclude the diagnosis. A CT scan with IV and oral contrast is far more sensitive for detecting small volumes of free air and the site of the perforation. Emergency surgical exploration is the definitive diagnostic and therapeutic step.

**3. Acute Mesenteric Ischemia**

*Overview*: Sudden interruption of the blood supply to a segment of the intestine, leading to mucosal ischemia, then full-thickness bowel necrosis within 6-8 hours if untreated. It has an extraordinarily high mortality (50-80%) primarily because the diagnosis is delayed. The SMA is the most common vessel affected. The etiology is either an embolus from a cardiac source (atrial fibrillation, recent MI, valvular disease) that lodges at the origin of the SMA, or acute thrombosis of a pre-existing atherosclerotic plaque. Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia occurs in low-flow states (cardiogenic shock, sepsis) from intense splanchnic vasoconstriction.

*Red Flags*: The classic, pathognomonic presentation is "pain out of proportion to the physical exam." The patient has sudden, severe, diffuse periumbilical pain that is agonizing, yet the abdomen is soft, non-tender, and without guarding in the early hours. This is because the initial ischemia is visceral, not involving the parietal peritoneum. The patient may vomit or have an urgent, explosive bowel movement (evacuation of the ischemic mucosa). The pain is constant, not colicky. After 6-8 hours, as transmural infarction develops, the pain localizes, peritonitis develops (guarding, rebound), and the patient becomes septic (fever, hypotension, lactic acidosis). The key is to diagnose mesenteric ischemia in the early, pain-only phase, before the development of peritonitis.

*Detection*: CT angiography of the abdomen is the first-line, gold-standard test, with a sensitivity of 95-100% and sensitivity of 95-100%. It shows a filling defect in the SMA (embolus, thrombosis) and may show bowel wall thinning (early), edema (later), pneumatosis intestinalis (air in the bowel wall, a sign of infarction), and portal venous gas (ominous sign). Laboratory findings: elevated serum lactate (a late finding, but an elevated lactate in a patient with severe abdominal pain and a normal exam mandates CTA), metabolic acidosis, and leukocytosis. A normal lactate does not exclude mesenteric ischemia. A plain film is normal in the early stages. Do not wait for peritonitis or a rising lactate, as necrosis will be advanced. This is a time-critical diagnosis; the window for revascularization is 4-6 hours from symptom onset.

**4. Acute Cholangitis (Ascending Cholangitis)**

*Overview*: A life-threatening bacterial infection of the biliary tree, resulting from biliary obstruction (stones, stricture, stent blockage, or malignancy) with superimposed bacterial infection. The obstruction raises the intrabiliary pressure, which causes bacteria to reflux from the infected bile into the hepatic sinusoids (cholangio-venous reflux) and then into the systemic circulation, causing sepsis. The organism is typically enteric Gram-negative rods from the duodenum (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter) and anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium). The presentation ranges from mild to severe, life-threatening septic shock. The mortality rate in severe cholangitis is 20-40%.

*Red Flags*: Charcot's triad: (1) right upper quadrant pain, (2) fever with rigors (shaking chills), and (3) jaundice. This triad is present in 50-70% of cases. The addition of hypotension (from septic shock) and altered mental status (from sepsis-associated encephalopathy) is Reynolds' pentad, which is present in <15% of cases but indicates severe, life-threatening cholangitis with a very high mortality.

*Detection*: The initial test is an abdominal ultrasound, which shows a dilated common bile duct (>6 mm, or >10 mm in the elderly or post-cholecystectomy) and often identifies the cause (stones, mass). Laboratory findings: leukocytosis with a left shift, elevated ALP and GGT (cholestatic pattern), hyperbilirubinemia (conjugated), elevated aminotransferases (from the hepatocellular injury), and positive blood cultures (bacteremia is present in 50-90%). The definitive diagnostic and therapeutic procedure is ERCP, which is urgent (within 24-48 hours for moderate disease, within 12-24 hours for severe disease with organ dysfunction). The ERCP directly visualizes the biliary obstruction, obtains bile cultures, and provides decompression through sphincterotomy, stone extraction, and/or stent placement, which is the urgent, life-saving treatment.

**5. Acute Toxic Megacolon**

*Overview*: A life-threatening, acute colonic dilation (diameter >6 cm total colon or >9 cm cecum) with systemic toxicity, occurring as a complication of severe colitis (ulcerative colitis, Crohn's colitis, infectious colitis from C. difficile or CMV). The severe, transmural inflammation and the release of inflammatory mediators (nitric oxide, VIP) cause a profound paralysis of the colonic smooth muscle. The dilated, thin-walled colon is at high risk of perforation, which has a mortality rate of 40-50%.

*Red Flags*: In a patient with known severe colitis (acute severe ulcerative colitis is defined by the Truelove and Witts criteria: >6 bloody stools/day plus one of: fever >37.8°C, tachycardia >90 bpm, anemia (Hb <10.5 g/dL), or elevated ESR >30 mm/hr), there is a sudden reduction or cessation of diarrhea (a falsely reassuring sign) because the paralyzed colon cannot expel its contents. The abdomen becomes distended and tympanitic. The patient is toxic: fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status. Abdominal tenderness, reduced bowel sounds, and rebound tenderness suggest impending perforation.

*Detection*: A plain abdominal X-ray (supine) is the diagnostic test of choice, showing a dilated, featureless colon with loss of haustral markings. CT scan confirms the dilation and rules out perforation. The white blood cell count is markedly elevated, and albumin is low. This is a medical and surgical emergency. Immediate management: cessation of all antidiarrheal agents and opiates, IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and IV corticosteroids (or cyclosporine/infliximab for steroid-refractory IBD). Surgical consultation is mandatory; failure to improve within 24-48 hours mandates emergency colectomy.
