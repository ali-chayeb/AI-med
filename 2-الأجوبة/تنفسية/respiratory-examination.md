## First: Examination Overview & Preparation

### Definition & Anatomical Boundaries

The respiratory system examination is a systematic clinical assessment of the upper and lower airways, lung parenchyma, pleura, and chest wall. The anatomical boundaries for surface examination extend from the apices of the lungs (approximately 2–4 cm above the medial third of the clavicles anteriorly, and the spinous process of C7 posteriorly) to the bases (the costodiaphragmatic recesses, which reach the level of T10 posteriorly, T8 laterally, and T6 anteriorly at the midclavicular line). The diaphragm moves inferiorly during inspiration, descending by 3–5 cm in normal tidal breathing and up to 10 cm with deep inspiration. The thoracic cage is bounded by the sternum anteriorly, the twelve thoracic vertebrae posteriorly, and the ribs and intercostal muscles laterally. [1,2]

### Clinical Objectives

The respiratory examination aims to answer the following focused clinical questions:

- Is there evidence of respiratory distress or impending respiratory failure?
- Is the airway patent, and is there any upper airway obstruction (stridor)?
- Is there consolidation (pneumonia), pleural fluid (effusion), air in the pleural space (pneumothorax), or loss of lung volume (atelectasis, collapse)?
- Is there diffuse airway obstruction (asthma, COPD) or interstitial lung disease (pulmonary fibrosis)?
- Are there signs of chronic respiratory disease (clubbing, barrel chest, cyanosis, cachexia)? [1]

### General Principles

Before beginning, the clinician must perform hand hygiene. The patient must be informed about the nature of the examination and consent obtained. The examination room should be warm, private, and well-lit. A chaperone should be offered if intimate exposure is required. Dignity is preserved by covering the anterior chest with a sheet or gown when examining the posterior chest, and vice versa. The complete respiratory examination is always performed on bare skin; auscultation through clothing renders the examination clinically useless and is a fundamental error. [1,2]

### Patient Positioning & Exposure

- **Ideal Position:** The patient should be sitting upright on the edge of the bed or a chair, at approximately 45–90 degrees. This allows full access to the anterior, lateral, and posterior chest. If the patient is supine (e.g., in the ICU), the examination must be adapted, acknowledging that the posterior chest will be inaccessible and that basal lung findings will be altered by position.
- **Exposure:** The patient must be undressed to the waist. For female patients, a gown or sheet should cover the anterior chest during the posterior examination, and then be moved to cover the posterior chest during the anterior examination. This preserves dignity while enabling a complete examination. [1]

### Equipment Required

- **Stethoscope:** A high-quality acoustic stethoscope with a bell (for low-frequency sounds) and a diaphragm (for high-frequency sounds). The diaphragm is used for the vast majority of auscultation including breath sounds, crackles, and wheezes.
- **Pulse Oximeter:** A portable device for immediate, non-invasive measurement of arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation (SpO₂).
- **Peak Flow Meter (Optional but Ideal):** A handheld device to measure peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) in liters per minute, particularly if an acute exacerbation of asthma is suspected.
- **Ruler or Tape Measure:** For measuring diaphragmatic excursion and chest expansion.
- **Watch with a Second Hand:** For counting respiratory rate.
- **Sputum Pot (if available):** For inspecting the color, volume, and consistency of sputum produced by the patient.
- **Disposable Tissues:** For patient use during the examination. [1,2]

### Examination Sequence Overview

The standard framework for the respiratory system is Inspection – Palpation – Percussion – Auscultation (IPPA). This sequence is performed in exactly this order. The examination is conducted in a "ladder-like" fashion, moving from the apices to the bases, comparing the right and left hemithorax at each level. The sequence is: anterior chest, lateral chest, and finally posterior chest. This is the only acceptable method. Skipping from the anterior to the posterior without a systematic level-by-level comparison is a common and avoidable error. [1]

## Second: Inspection

### End-of-Bed Inspection

This begins the moment the clinician enters the room and continues while taking the history, before any hands-on examination. The objective is to identify overt signs of respiratory distress or chronic disease.

- **General Appearance:** Is the patient alert, confused, or drowsy? Confusion or drowsiness can indicate hypercapnia (CO₂ narcosis) or severe hypoxemia. Is the patient cachectic (chronic disease, lung cancer) or obese (obesity hypoventilation syndrome, restrictive defect)?
- **Posture and Position:** The tripod position (sitting upright, leaning forward with arms braced on the knees or a table) is a classic sign of severe respiratory distress, as it fixes the shoulder girdle and optimizes the mechanical advantage of the accessory muscles of respiration. Orthopnea (dyspnea when supine, relieved by sitting up) suggests left ventricular failure, severe airflow obstruction, or diaphragmatic paralysis. An inability to lie flat and an insistence on sitting bolt upright should immediately raise alarm. [1]
- **Audible Sounds:** Listen for sounds audible without the stethoscope.
  - *Stridor:* A harsh, high-pitched, predominantly inspiratory crowing sound, signifying a critical, life-threatening narrowing of the extrathoracic upper airway (larynx or trachea). This is an emergency.
  - *Wheeze:* A musical, polyphonic expiratory sound, indicating diffuse airway narrowing, typical of asthma or COPD.
  - *Hoarse Voice (Dysphonia):* Suggests laryngeal pathology (carcinoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, inflammation).
  - *Gurgling (Death Rattle):* The sound of air passing through pooled secretions in the major airways and hypopharynx, typically in a severely obtunded patient unable to swallow or cough effectively.
  - *Explosive, Barking Cough:* A productive, hacking cough of chronic bronchitis, or a dry, irritating cough of early interstitial lung disease or an ACE inhibitor. [1]
- **Visible Aids and Equipment:** Note immediately any oxygen delivery devices (nasal cannula, Venturi mask, non-rebreather mask), nebulizer, inhalers, sputum pot, or chest drains. Assess the oxygen flow rate and the device's appropriateness for the clinical state.
- **Breathing Pattern and Rate:** Count the respiratory rate silently for a full 60 seconds while appearing to take the radial pulse. This prevents the patient from consciously altering their breathing. The normal adult rate is 12–20 breaths per minute.
  - *Tachypnea (>20 breaths/min):* The single most sensitive sign of a respiratory or metabolic crisis (pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, metabolic acidosis, anxiety).
  - *Bradypnea (<12 breaths/min):* Narcotic overdose, raised intracranial pressure, severe fatigue/pre-terminal state in respiratory failure.
  - *Abnormal Patterns:* Kussmaul breathing (deep, sighing, regular breaths of severe metabolic acidosis, e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis); Cheyne-Stokes respiration (crescendo-decrescendo pattern of breaths followed by a period of apnea, seen in heart failure, stroke, and at altitude); Biot's (ataxic) breathing (irregular, unpredictable breaths of medullary injury). [1]

### Hands and Upper Body

Proceed to a directed inspection of the hands, face, and neck.

- **Clubbing:** Inspect the fingers for the loss of the normal Lovibond angle (the angle between the nail plate and the proximal nail fold, normally <165°). Perform Schamroth's window test: oppose the dorsal surfaces of the terminal phalanges of both thumbs. Normally, a diamond-shaped window of light is visible between the nail beds. In clubbing, this window is obliterated. The nail bed is spongy and fluctuant on palpation. Lung cancer is the most common cause of acquired clubbing. Clubbing is also a key sign in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, bronchiectasis, lung abscess, and cyanotic congenital heart disease. [1,3]
- **Peripheral Cyanosis:** Bluish-purple discoloration of the fingertips and nail beds. This is a sign of vasoconstriction and sluggish peripheral blood flow with increased oxygen extraction, often due to cold exposure or low cardiac output. It is not a primary sign of central hypoxemia.
- **Central Cyanosis:** Inspect the mucous membranes of the lips and the underside of the tongue. A bluish discoloration here indicates at least 5 g/dL of deoxygenated hemoglobin in the arterial blood, signifying severe hypoxemia (PaO₂ typically < 45 mmHg, SpO₂ < 80–85%). This is a pre-terminal sign in respiratory disease. It will be absent in severe anemia, where the total hemoglobin mass is too low to reach 5 g/dL of deoxyhemoglobin.
- **Tar Staining:** Yellow-brown nicotine stains on the index and middle fingers, pathognomonic for heavy cigarette smoking, the single most important risk factor for COPD and lung cancer.
- **CO₂ Retention Flap (Asterixis):** Ask the patient to hold their arms outstretched and wrists dorsiflexed (the "stop traffic" position). A coarse, arrhythmic flapping tremor at the wrists is a sign of severe hypercapnic respiratory failure, hepatic encephalopathy, or uremia. Its presence in a patient with COPD indicates life-threatening CO₂ retention.
- **Face and Eyes:** Inspect for conjunctival pallor (anemia, which exacerbates dyspnea), plethora (facial redness, a sign of secondary polycythemia in chronic hypoxemia), and Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, suggesting a superior sulcus Pancoast tumor). [1]

### Chest Inspection

With the patient sitting up and chest adequately exposed, conduct a systematic inspection of the thorax, anteriorly and posteriorly.

### Shape and Symmetry:

  - *Barrel Chest:* The anteroposterior (AP) diameter is increased, approaching the lateral diameter (a ratio of 1:1 instead of the normal 1:2). This is the hallmark of chronic hyperinflation, most commonly due to severe emphysema (COPD).
  - *Pectus Excavatum:* A funnel chest, with a concave depression of the lower sternum. This can cause a restrictive ventilatory defect and cardiac compression in severe cases.
  - *Pectus Carinatum:* A pigeon chest, with a convex, protruding sternum.
  - *Kyphoscoliosis:* A combination of an abnormal posterior curvature (kyphosis) and lateral curvature (scoliosis) of the thoracic spine. This causes a severe restrictive ventilatory defect by mechanically limiting the ability of the ribs to expand the thoracic cavity.
- **Chest Wall Expansion:** Observe the movement of the chest with tidal breathing. Expansion must be symmetrical. Unilateral lag or decreased expansion on one side suggests a pleural effusion, pneumothorax, lobar collapse, or splinting from pleuritic chest pain. Note the pattern of breathing: males normally exhibit abdominothoracic breathing (diaphragmatic movement is prominent), while females exhibit thoracoabdominal breathing. In diaphragmatic paralysis, the abdomen moves inward on inspiration (paradoxical abdominal breathing), while the chest expands, a sign of impending respiratory arrest.
- **Accessory Muscle Use and Retractions:** Look for visible contraction of the sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles during inspiration. This indicates severe work of breathing. Note intercostal, supraclavicular, and subcostal retractions (indrawing of the soft tissues), which are particularly significant in infants and children but indicate severe airway obstruction in adults.
- **Scars and Deformities:** Note the presence of scars from a prior thoracotomy (lung cancer surgery, lobectomy) or a small scar at the 5th intercostal space in the mid-axillary line from a prior chest drain insertion. Note radiotherapy tattoos (small, dark ink dots), indicating prior treatment for lung or breast cancer.
- **Skin and Soft Tissues:** Inspect for visible subcutaneous emphysema (surgical emphysema), particularly on the neck and upper chest. Palpation confirms the characteristic crackling sensation, but visible swelling of the skin without erythema should trigger the search. It indicates a pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum with air dissecting into the tissue planes. Inspect for dilated veins on the chest wall, a sign of superior vena cava obstruction (SVC syndrome), often due to a mediastinal tumor. [1,2]

## Third: Palpation

Palpation provides critical information about the structural integrity of the chest wall, the position of the mediastinum, the symmetry of lung expansion, and the transmission of sound through the lung parenchyma. The palmar surface of the hands is used for assessing tactile fremitus, the fingertips for localizing points of tenderness and lymph nodes, and the dorsal surface for temperature. [1]

### Tracheal Position

This is a life-saving, rapid bedside test to assess for mediastinal shift.

- **Technique:** Gently insert the index finger into the suprasternal notch (between the heads of the two sternocleidomastoid muscles) and palpate the tracheal cartilage rings. Alternatively, place the index and ring fingers on the sternal heads of each sternocleidomastoid and use the middle finger to palpate the space between the trachea and each muscle.

### Interpretation:

  - *Central:* Normal.
  - *Deviation Away (Push):* Indicates a space-occupying lesion on the opposite side of the deviation. The most critical cause is a tension pneumothorax. Other causes include a massive pleural effusion or a large mediastinal tumor.
  - *Deviation Toward (Pull):* Indicates volume loss on the side of the deviation. This is most commonly due to lobar or whole lung collapse (e.g., from a mucus plug or endobronchial tumor) or a previous pneumonectomy. Extensive pleural thickening (fibrothorax) can also pull.
  - *Clinical Utility: Tier 1.* A trachea deviated to one side with absent breath sounds on the other side is a tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise and demands immediate needle decompression. [1,2]

### Chest Expansion

This tests the symmetry and degree of thoracic cage movement, reflecting the expansion of the underlying lungs.

### Anterior (Upper Lobe) Expansion:

  - *Technique:* Stand in front of the patient. Place both hands firmly on the anterolateral chest wall, with the fingers extended around the sides and the thumbs meeting in the midline, at the level of the 4th intercostal space (lower border of the pectoralis major). Raise a loose skin fold between the thumbs. Ask the patient to take a deep breath and then exhale fully. Observe the distance the thumbs diverge from each other at full inspiration. Normally, it is symmetrical and more than 4 cm.

### Posterior (Lower Lobe) Expansion:

  - *Technique:* Stand behind the patient. Place the hands over the lower posterolateral chest wall, with the fingers extended anteriorly and the thumbs meeting in the midline at the level of the 10th thoracic vertebra (T10). Raise a skin fold between the thumbs. Ask the patient to take a deep breath and exhale fully. Observe the divergence of the thumbs.
- **Interpretation:** Asymmetrical reduction in expansion on one side indicates unilateral pleural disease (effusion), lung pathology (consolidation, collapse, fibrosis), or splinting from pleuritic pain. Symmetrically reduced expansion suggests diffuse hyperinflation (COPD, severe asthma) or restrictive disease. [1]

### Tactile (Vocal) Fremitus

This test exploits the physical principle that sound is transmitted better through a solid or liquid medium than through air.

- **Technique:** Using the ulnar border of the hand or the palmar base of the fingers, palpate the chest wall firmly. Ask the patient to repeat the phrase "ninety-nine" or "toy boat" (the latter is richer in low-frequency vibrations). Start at the lung apices and move systematically down, comparing exactly symmetrical points on the right and left hemithorax, covering the anterior, lateral, and posterior chest.

### Interpretation:

  - *Normal:* The vibration is felt symmetrically, being slightly more prominent in the right upper lobe (due to the more direct, shorter right main bronchus) and in the interscapular region (proximity to the main bronchi).
  - *Increased Fremitus:* The sound waves travel directly through a consolidated, airless lung (e.g., lobar pneumonia), making the vibrations louder and clearer on the chest wall. This is a classic sign of pneumonia with a patent bronchus. Sensitivity for consolidation is approximately 82%, specificity around 79%. [4]
  - *Decreased or Absent Fremitus:* The sound waves are blocked or attenuated by an insulating layer of air (pneumothorax) or fluid (pleural effusion), or by a severely hyperinflated lung (emphysema), which dampens sound transmission. It is also absent when the bronchus to that area is obstructed (tumor, mucus plug).
  - *Clinical Utility: Tier 1.* [1]

### Chest Wall Palpation and Assessment

- **Tenderness:** Palpate the ribs and intercostal muscles systematically. Focal tenderness suggests a rib fracture (acute trauma, pathological fracture from tumor metastasis) or costochondritis.
- **Subcutaneous Emphysema:** Palpate the skin of the neck and anterior chest wall. A pathognomonic "rice krispies" crackling sensation under the fingertips indicates air in the subcutaneous tissue planes. This is a sign of a significant air leak from a pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum. It is a Tier 1 sign. [1]

### Lymph Node Examination

The respiratory physical examination is incomplete without examination of the cervical and supraclavicular lymph nodes. These nodes drain the thorax and mediastinum and are a critical site of metastasis for lung cancer.

- **Technique:** Stand behind the seated patient. Examine the submental, submandibular, tonsillar, pre-auricular, post-auricular, superficial and deep cervical chain, posterior cervical, and supraclavicular nodes. Use the pads of the index and middle fingers to palpate with gentle, circular motions.
- **Virchow's Node (Troisier's Sign):** An enlarged, hard, left supraclavicular node. This is a pathognomonic, though late, sign of metastatic abdominal malignancy (gastric cancer being the most classic) or lung cancer, traveling via the thoracic duct. Palpate deeply in the left supraclavicular fossa, ideally while the patient performs a Valsalva maneuver (which elevates the node). This is a Tier 1, "do not miss" finding. [1]

## Fourth: Percussion

Percussion of the chest is a clinically useful technique for mapping the density of the underlying tissue. In the era of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), its role is diminishing for some specific questions, but it remains a rapid, cost-free, Tier 1 bedside tool for the detection of a pneumothorax and large pleural effusions. It is a cornerstone of the physical examination. [1,2]

### Percussion Technique

- **Indirect (Mediate) Percussion:** This is the standard method. Place the middle finger (pleximeter finger) of the non-dominant hand firmly against the chest wall, positioned in an intercostal space. The other fingers must be lifted off the skin, as contact with them will dampen the sound. The tip of the middle finger (plexor finger) of the dominant hand strikes the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint of the pleximeter finger with a loose, snapping motion of the wrist. The strike is quick, sharp, and perpendicular, with immediate withdrawal. Percuss systematically from apex to base, comparing the right and left sides at every level: anteriorly (patient supine if possible or sitting), laterally, and posteriorly (patient sitting, arms crossed in front to move scapulae apart). [1]

### Percussion Notes & Interpretation

- **Resonant:** The normal note over healthy, air-filled lung. Low-pitched, hollow, and sustained.
- **Hyper-resonant:** Louder, lower-pitched, and longer than resonant. It indicates increased air content relative to solid tissue. This note over one hemithorax in a dyspneic patient is highly specific for a pneumothorax. It is also heard bilaterally in emphysema and acute severe asthma.
- **Dull:** Soft, high-pitched, short note, indicating decreased air content. This is heard over consolidation (pneumonia), atelectasis, lobar collapse, thickened pleura, or a fibrotic zone.
- **Stony Dull:** An extreme, flat, and absolutely non-resonant note with high-pitched quality, resembling the sound of percussing a rock or a solid piece of wood. This is the classic percussion finding of a pleural effusion.
- **Tympanitic:** A drum-like, musical, hollow note heard over a gas-filled hollow viscus. It is heard over the normal gastric bubble (left lower anterior chest). A tympanitic note over the right lower anterior chest is highly suggestive of free intraperitoneal air (pneumoperitoneum from a perforated viscus), a critical surgical sign. [1]

### Organ-Specific Percussion: Diaphragmatic Excursion

This test assesses the movement of the diaphragm with respiration, most useful when phrenic nerve palsy or diaphragmatic paralysis is suspected.

- **Technique:** Percuss down the posterior chest wall during quiet breathing until the note changes from resonant to dull. Mark this point as the resting level of the diaphragm. Then, ask the patient to take a maximal deep inspiration and hold it. Percuss down again quickly until the note becomes dull, and mark this point. Finally, ask the patient to exhale fully and hold it, and percuss up from the dull area, marking the point where it becomes resonant.
- **Normal Finding:** The diaphragm descends 3–5 cm on maximal inspiration. An excursion of less than 3 cm is abnormal. In severe COPD with a flattened, hyperinflated diaphragm, the resting position is low and excursion is minimal. In phrenic nerve palsy, the diaphragm may rise paradoxically on inspiration (detected by a paradoxical upward movement of the dullness margin).
- **Sensitivity & Specificity:** The sensitivity is low for mild disease, and POCUS is now the definitive bedside test. Percussion of diaphragmatic excursion is considered a Tier 2 maneuver, useful in specific contexts but supplanted by imaging. [1]

## Fifth: Auscultation

Auscultation is the most informative component of the respiratory examination. It is performed using the diaphragm of the stethoscope, pressed firmly against the bare skin. The bell is rarely used in respiratory auscultation. The sequence is identical to percussion: apex to base, comparing right to left sides. The patient should be instructed to breathe deeply through an open mouth, but cautioned to report any lightheadedness (hypocapnia from hyperventilation). [1,2]

### Normal Breath Sounds

- **Vesicular Breath Sounds:** Soft, low-pitched, rustling, heard throughout inspiration and into approximately the first third of expiration. They are produced by turbulent airflow in the lobar and segmental bronchi, heavily filtered and attenuated by the normal air-filled alveoli and lung parenchyma. This is the normal sound heard over the majority of the lung fields.
- **Bronchovesicular Breath Sounds:** Intermediate in pitch and intensity, with inspiratory and expiratory phases of nearly equal duration. They are normally heard over the main bronchi, specifically over the upper sternum (manubrium) anteriorly and between the scapulae (interscapular area) posteriorly.
- **Tracheal (Bronchial) Breath Sounds:** Loud, high-pitched, harsh, and tubular, with a distinct gap (pause) between the long expiratory phase and the inspiratory phase. They are heard normally over the trachea in the neck. When heard at a location over the lung periphery, this is an abnormal finding and is termed bronchial breathing. [1]

### Adventitious (Abnormal) Sounds

- **Bronchial Breathing:** The presence of tracheal-quality breath sounds over the peripheral lung fields. This is the hallmark of lung consolidation (e.g., lobar pneumonia). The solid, airless lung transmits high-frequency sounds from the central airways directly to the stethoscope without the normal acoustic filtering by air-filled alveoli. The airway must be patent for this sound to be produced; if a mucus plug occludes the bronchus leading to the consolidation, the breath sounds will be absent.
  - *Sensitivity & Specificity:* Specificity for consolidation is high (up to 96% when combined with increased fremitus and egophony). Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [4]
- **Crackles (Crepitations, Rales):** Discontinuous, brief, explosive sounds. Their timing is the key to diagnosis.
  - *Fine, End-Inspiratory Crackles:* High-pitched, velcro-like, occurring in showers during the latter part of inspiration. They are produced by the sudden, explosive opening of small, previously collapsed, fluid-obstructed distal airways and alveoli, equalizing a pressure gradient between the closed and open segments. They are the cardinal auscultatory finding in interstitial pulmonary fibrosis and early pulmonary edema. They do not typically clear with coughing. Clinical Utility: Tier 1.
  - *Coarse, Early-Inspiratory Crackles:* Lower-pitched, bubbling, gurgling sounds heard in early inspiration, often clearing or changing in character with a cough. They are produced by air bubbling through excessive secretions (mucus, pus, edema fluid) in the medium-to-large airways. This typifies chronic bronchitis and bronchiectasis. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1]
- **Wheeze:** A continuous, high-pitched, musical sound, usually louder on expiration.
  - *Mechanism:* Oscillation of apposed airway walls as airflow velocity increases through a critically narrowed segment (Bernoulli principle). The narrowing arises from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, intraluminal secretions, or extrinsic compression.
  - *Polyphonic Wheeze:* Multiple pitches heard simultaneously. This is the classic sound of diffuse small airway narrowing in asthma and COPD exacerbation.
  - *Monophonic (Fixed) Wheeze:* A single, unchanging pitch. This indicates a localized, fixed obstruction of a single large airway, most classically by an endobronchial tumor or a foreign body. This is a critical "do not miss" finding that demands investigation (CT chest, bronchoscopy) in a non-asthmatic patient or in an asthmatic whose wheeze has changed pattern.
  - *Clinical Utility: Tier 1.* A silent chest (no audible wheeze) in a patient in severe respiratory distress is a life-threatening, pre-arrest sign indicating airflow too low to generate a wheeze. [1,5]
- **Rhonchi:** Low-pitched, sonorous, snoring-like continuous sounds. They are essentially coarse crackles overlapping in time or wheezes generated by secretions in larger airways. They frequently change or clear completely with vigorous coughing, a key feature distinguishing them from parenchymal crackles and true wheezes.
- **Pleural Friction Rub:** A loud, leathery, creaking, or grating sound heard during both inspiration and expiration, localized to one area, and not cleared by coughing. It is produced by the inflamed, roughened, fibrin-coated parietal and visceral pleural surfaces rubbing against each other. It is pathognomonic for pleuritis. The sound disappears when a pleural effusion develops and separates the two layers and may reappear as the effusion resolves. Clinical Utility: Tier 1.
- **Stridor:** A harsh, high-pitched, continuous, monophonic, predominantly inspiratory sound heard loudest over the neck. It signifies critical, life-threatening narrowing of the extrathoracic upper airway (larynx or trachea). It is not strictly a lung sound but an airway sound, and it is a Tier 1 emergency requiring immediate action. [1]

### Vocal Resonance

This is the auscultatory equivalent of tactile fremitus, assessing spoken voice transmission.

- **Bronchophony:** Ask the patient to say "ninety-nine." Over a consolidation, the spoken numbers are louder and clearer than normal.
- **Whispering Pectoriloquy:** Ask the patient to whisper a phrase (e.g., "one-two-three"). Normally, a faint, distant, muffled, and indistinct sound is heard. In consolidation, the whispered numbers are heard clearly and distinctly. This is a highly specific sign of consolidation.
- **Egophony:** Ask the patient to say the vowel "E." Over a zone of consolidation or at the upper border of a pleural effusion, the sound "E" is transformed into a nasal, bleating "A" (from the goat-like sound). This is a classic, pathognomonic sign of lobar pneumonia.
  - *Sensitivity & Specificity:* Egophony has a sensitivity of 21% but a specificity of 96% for pneumonia; when present, it strongly rules in the diagnosis. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [4]

## Sixth: Special Tests, Maneuvers & Eponymous Signs

### Tests for Vocal Cord Dysfunction and Laryngeal Assessment

- **Laryngeal Auscultation:** Suspect vocal cord dysfunction (VCD) or upper airway pathology when a patient (often a young female with "refractory asthma") reports stridor or inspiratory difficulty. With the diaphragm of the stethoscope, auscultate over the larynx (thyroid cartilage, the "Adam's apple") in the anterior neck, comparing the sound with the lung fields. In VCD, the loudest sound is over the larynx, and it is a harsh inspiratory noise, while lung sounds may be quiet and vesicular.
- **Forced Expiratory Maneuver:** In a patient with suspected intrathoracic airway obstruction (e.g., tracheal tumor), a forced expiration may convert a subtle inspiratory stridor into a loud expiratory central wheeze, as the positive intrathoracic pressure causes dynamic collapse of the airway around the lesion. Clinical Utility: Tier 2.

### Maneuvers to Distinguish Cardiac vs. Respiratory Dyspnea

- **Valsalva Maneuver:** Not routinely performed, but understanding its phases can help at the bedside. In left ventricular failure, the normal systolic pressure rise is absent, and there is a "square wave" response, indicating a failing, fluid-overloaded left ventricle.
- **Orthopnea Assessment:** Observing the patient during the history (how many pillows do you sleep on? Why?). The inability to lie flat without coughing or dyspnea is a powerful sign. Orthopnea is a Tier 1 sign for left heart failure but also occurs in severe COPD, diaphragmatic paralysis, and massive ascites.

### Tests for Pulmonary Hypertension

- **Right Ventricular Heave:** Place the heel of the hand over the left parasternal region (3rd-4th intercostal space). A sustained, forceful lift against the hand during systole indicates right ventricular pressure overload, a key sign of pulmonary hypertension. Clinical Utility: Tier 1.
- **Loud P2 Component of the Second Heart Sound:** Auscultate the second heart sound (S2) in the left 2nd intercostal space (pulmonary area). Normally, the aortic (A2) component is louder. A palpable or loud, accentuated pulmonary component (P2) that equals or exceeds the A2 intensity indicates pulmonary hypertension. This is a Tier 1 sign.
- **Graham Steell Murmur:** A high-pitched, early diastolic decrescendo murmur of pulmonary regurgitation, heard in the left 2nd–4th intercostal spaces, due to a dilated pulmonary valve annulus in severe pulmonary hypertension. This is a Tier 2 sign. [1,2]

## Seventh: Synthesis & Clinical Pattern Recognition

### Completing the Examination

The respiratory examination is not complete until the following adjacent systems are assessed:

- **Cardiovascular System:** Examine the JVP (elevated in cor pulmonale, tension pneumothorax), heart sounds (accentuated P2, right ventricular heave), and peripheral edema (sacral and pedal).
- **Peripheral Signs:** Examine for pallor, cyanosis, and clubbing. Check pulse oximetry.
- **Sputum Examination:** If the patient has a sputum pot, note the color, volume, and consistency (purulent, mucopurulent, mucoid, blood-stained, or frothy pink).

### Common Syndromes & Their Physical Signatures

### Lobar Pneumonia (Consolidation):

  - *Inspection:* Tachypnea, splinting on the affected side.
  - *Palpation:* Normal central trachea, reduced expansion, increased tactile fremitus.
  - *Percussion:* Dull.
  - *Auscultation:* Bronchial breath sounds, bronchophony, egophony, whispering pectoriloquy, and fine end-inspiratory crackles at the borders of the consolidation.

### Pleural Effusion (Large):

  - *Inspection:* Tachypnea, reduced expansion on the affected side.
  - *Palpation:* Trachea deviated away from the affected side (if massive), decreased tactile fremitus.
  - *Percussion:* Stony dull.
  - *Auscultation:* Absent or markedly diminished breath sounds. Bronchial breathing and egophony may be heard just above the fluid level.

### Tension Pneumothorax:

  - *Inspection:* Severe respiratory distress, tachypnea, cyanosis, distended neck veins, possible tracheal deviation (late sign).
  - *Palpation:* Trachea deviated away from the affected side, absent fremitus, hypotension.
  - *Percussion:* Hyper-resonant.
  - *Auscultation:* Absent breath sounds.

### Acute Severe Asthma:

  - *Inspection:* Tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, inability to speak in full sentences, tripod position.
  - *Palpation:* Tachycardia, pulsus paradoxus (>10 mmHg).
  - *Percussion:* Bilaterally hyper-resonant.
  - *Auscultation:* Loud, diffuse polyphonic expiratory wheeze. In a life-threatening attack, the chest may be silent (too little airflow for a wheeze).

### Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis:

  - *Inspection:* Tachypnea, clubbing.
  - *Palpation:* Trachea central, normal expansion (early) or symmetrically reduced (late).
  - *Percussion:* Normal or dull at bases.
  - *Auscultation:* Fine, "velcro" end-inspiratory crackles at the lung bases, typically unchanged by coughing. [1,4]

### Documentation

A normal respiratory examination note:

"On inspection, the chest is clear, with no respiratory distress, a resting respiratory rate of 14/min, and symmetrical expansion. No clubbing or cyanosis. Trachea is central on palpation, chest expansion is full and symmetrical, and tactile fremitus is normal and symmetric. Percussion note is resonant throughout. Auscultation reveals normal vesicular breath sounds with no crackles, wheezes, or rubs. SpO₂ 98% on room air."

An abnormal note (e.g., left lower lobe pneumonia):

"Patient is tachypneic (24/min) with visible splinting of the left side. Trachea central. Expansion is reduced on the left base. Fremitus is increased over the left lower zone posteriorly. Percussion is dull in this region. Auscultation reveals bronchial breath sounds, bronchophony, and fine end-inspiratory crackles over the left base. SpO₂ 91% on room air."

## Eighth: Common Errors, Pitfalls & Tricks

### Frequent Mistakes Students Make

- **Auscultation Through Clothing:** This is the single most common and most serious error. It blocks the transmission of all high-frequency sounds (crackles, bronchial breathing), rendering the entire auscultation useless. The chest must be bare.
- **Incorrect Stethoscope Use:** Using the bell for lung sounds (no contact) or pressing too lightly with the diaphragm (loses skin contact). The diaphragm must be pressed firmly.
- **Palpating Trachea with Only One Finger:** The examiner must use two fingers (on both sternocleidomastoid heads) to feel for asymmetry. Using one finger centrally will miss a subtle deviation.
- **Percussing with an Errant Pleximeter Finger:** All other fingers of the pleximeter hand must be lifted off the chest wall. If they are resting on the skin, they dampen the percussion wave and produce a dull note everywhere, simulating pathology.
- **Ignoring the Upper Border of a Pleural Effusion:** Students often diagnose an effusion, fail to auscultate just above the fluid level, and miss the key sign of compressed lung (bronchial breathing and egophony).

### Tricks of the Trade

- **The "Silent Chest" Audit:** In any patient with severe dyspnea, a silent chest is a pre-arrest sign. Do not document "clear chest" and walk away. Document "diminished breath sounds, silent chest" and summon the crash team immediately.
- **Eliciting a Subtle Rub:** A patient with sharp pleuritic pain and a suspicious finding but no audible rub should be asked to breathe deeply. The pain may make them splint, so their tidal volume is too low to generate the sound. Reassure them, give analgesia if time permits, and then ask them to take a deep breath. The rub will become evident.
- **Using Schamroth's Window:** Do not just eyeball clubbing. Perform Schamroth's window test; it is objective and reproducible, and it impresses senior clinicians. [1,2]

## Ninth: Key Guidelines & Evidence Base

- **Evidence-Based Physical Diagnosis:** The landmark JAMA series "The Rational Clinical Examination" and the textbook *Evidence-Based Physical Diagnosis* (McGee, 5th ed.) systematically review the sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios of respiratory physical signs. Key findings define egophony and bronchial breathing as highly specific for consolidation (Sp ~96%), while the absence of any abnormal auscultatory finding has a strong negative predictive value in ruling out significant parenchymal disease in low-risk ambulatory settings. [4]
- **GOLD and GINA Guidelines:** The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD 2025) and the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA 2024) emphasize the role of physical examination in the initial assessment of symptom severity and the identification of life-threatening features (silent chest, use of accessory muscles, pulsus paradoxus) during an acute exacerbation. They mandate immediate transfer to an acute care facility when these signs are present. [6,7]
- **The Role of Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS):** POCUS is now the Tier 1 reference standard for the detection of a pneumothorax (absence of lung sliding) and pleural effusion (anechoic space above the diaphragm), with a sensitivity and specificity exceeding 95% for both, significantly outperforming physical examination and chest radiography. While the traditional physical examination remains the first step in immediate, initial assessment, POCUS is the definitive bedside decision-making tool in the hands of a trained operator, particularly in the emergency department and ICU. [1]
