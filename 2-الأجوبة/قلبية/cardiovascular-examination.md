## First: Examination Overview & Preparation

### Definition & Anatomical Boundaries

The cardiovascular physical examination assesses the structure and function of the heart and great vessels through inspection, palpation, and auscultation. The anatomical boundaries of the precordium are defined by the surface projections of the heart: the right border extends from the right 3rd costal cartilage to the right 6th costal cartilage approximately 1-2 cm lateral to the sternum; the left border extends from the left 2nd intercostal space (pulmonary artery) to the left 5th intercostal space at the midclavicular line (apex); the inferior border is formed primarily by the right ventricle along the 6th costal cartilage; and the superior border is at the level of the 2nd intercostal spaces. The great vessels project behind the manubrium (aorta) and the left 2nd intercostal space (pulmonary artery). [1,2]

The cardiac silhouette on the anterior chest wall is approximately 10-12 cm in transverse diameter. The right atrium forms the right border, the left ventricle and left atrial appendage form the left border, the right ventricle forms the anterior surface, and the left atrium is the most posterior chamber, lying anterior to the esophagus. Surface landmarks for auscultation do not correspond precisely to the anatomical locations of the valves. Instead, they represent the sites where murmurs originating from each valve are best transmitted and heard. [1,2]

### Clinical Objectives

The cardiovascular examination aims to answer several specific clinical questions. Is there evidence of cardiac enlargement (cardiomegaly)? Is there evidence of abnormal ventricular filling pressures (elevated jugular venous pressure, S3 gallop, hepatojugular reflux)? Is there evidence of valvular heart disease (murmurs, thrills, abnormal heart sounds)? Is there evidence of heart failure (elevated JVP, S3, displaced apex, crackles, edema)? Is there evidence of pericardial disease (friction rub, muffled heart sounds, pulsus paradoxus)? Is there evidence of impaired peripheral perfusion (cool extremities, prolonged capillary refill, cyanosis, diminished pulses)? Is there evidence of an arrhythmia (irregularly irregular pulse, pulse deficit)? [1,3]

### General Principles

Before beginning the examination, the clinician must introduce themselves, explain the purpose of the examination, and obtain informed verbal consent. Hand hygiene must be performed before and after patient contact. A chaperone should be offered for all examinations, and their presence documented. The patient's privacy and dignity must be preserved throughout. The examination room should be quiet (essential for auscultation), warm, and well-lit. [1,3]

### Patient Positioning & Exposure

The cardiovascular examination requires multiple positions to optimize the detection of specific findings. The primary position is supine with the head of the bed elevated to 30-45 degrees. This position is optimal for assessing the jugular venous pressure and waveform, palpating the precordium, and performing baseline auscultation. The left lateral decubitus position (patient rolled partially onto the left side) brings the left ventricular apex closer to the chest wall, facilitating palpation of the apical impulse and auscultation of low-pitched diastolic sounds (S3, S4, and the murmur of mitral stenosis) with the bell of the stethoscope. The seated position, with the patient leaning forward in held end-expiration, is essential for detecting the high-pitched diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation at the left sternal border. [1,2,3]

The patient must be exposed from the waist up. In female patients, a gown or sheet should be used to cover the breasts when not being directly examined, preserving dignity. The lower extremities must be accessible for the assessment of peripheral pulses, edema, and skin changes. [1,3]

### Equipment Required

The following equipment is essential for a complete cardiovascular examination: [1,3]

- A high-quality stethoscope with both a diaphragm (for high-pitched sounds: S1, S2, murmurs of aortic and mitral regurgitation, ejection murmurs, opening snaps, clicks) and a bell (for low-pitched sounds: S3, S4, diastolic rumble of mitral stenosis).
- A sphygmomanometer with an appropriately sized cuff (cuff bladder width should be 40% of the arm circumference; bladder length should be 80% of arm circumference).
- A penlight or tangential light source for JVP assessment.
- A watch with a second hand or timer.
- A ruler or disposable tape measure.

### Examination Sequence Overview

The cardiovascular examination follows a logical, systematic sequence, beginning with general inspection at the end of the bed and progressing to specific, focused maneuvers. The overall framework is Inspection, Palpation, and Auscultation. Percussion of the heart is not a clinically useful maneuver in modern practice and has been supplanted by echocardiography. It is omitted from the core cardiovascular examination. The recommended sequence is: [1,3]

- General inspection (end-of-bed)
- Hand examination (peripheral stigmata of cardiovascular disease)
- Arterial pulse examination (radial, brachial, carotid)
- Blood pressure measurement (both arms, orthostatic if indicated)
- Jugular venous pressure and waveform assessment
- Precordial inspection
- Precordial palpation (apex beat, parasternal region, thrills)
- Auscultation (aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid, mitral areas; with maneuvers; with dynamic positioning)
- Back examination (lung bases, sacral edema)
- Peripheral vascular examination (lower extremity pulses, edema, bruits)

## Second: Inspection

### End-of-Bed Inspection

The examination begins at the foot of the bed, observing the patient from a distance. This provides an immediate, global assessment of the patient's cardiorespiratory status. [1,3]

- **General Appearance and Habitus:** Is the patient well or unwell? Do they appear distressed, tachypneic, diaphoretic, or cachectic? Cachexia in the setting of known heart disease suggests advanced heart failure (cardiac cachexia), a grave prognostic sign caused by chronic elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6), increased resting metabolic rate, and intestinal edema causing malabsorption. [4]
- **Body Habitus and Syndromic Features:** Marfanoid features (tall stature, long thin extremities, arachnodactyly, pectus excavatum or carinatum) suggest an increased risk of aortic root dilation, aortic regurgitation, and mitral valve prolapse. Down syndrome facies (upslanted palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, flat nasal bridge) are associated with congenital heart disease, particularly atrioventricular septal defect and ventricular septal defect. Turner syndrome (short stature, webbed neck, widely spaced nipples, low posterior hairline) is associated with coarctation of the aorta and bicuspid aortic valve. [1,2]
- **Position and Work of Breathing:** The patient with acute pulmonary edema will be sitting bolt upright (orthopnea), using accessory muscles of respiration (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, intercostals), and may be gasping for breath. The patient with pericarditis may be leaning forward (the position that relieves pleuritic chest pain by reducing the tension on the inflamed pericardium). Cheyne-Stokes respiration (a cyclical crescendo-decrescendo pattern of breathing alternating with periods of apnea) is a sign of advanced heart failure and indicates a low cardiac output with prolonged circulation time and altered central respiratory center sensitivity to CO2. [1,4]
- **Visible Aids and Devices:** Note the presence of supplemental oxygen (nasal cannula, face mask, non-rebreather, high-flow nasal cannula), medication patches (nitroglycerin), intravenous lines and infusions, cardiac monitor leads, a visible implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) or pacemaker bulge in the infraclavicular area, a left ventricular assist device (LVAD) driveline emerging from the abdominal wall, or a urinary catheter and its bag contents. [1,2]
- **Visible Pulsations:** An abnormally prominent arterial pulsation in the suprasternal notch suggests an aortic arch aneurysm or a hyperdynamic circulation. A visible right ventricular heave (parasternal lift) in the left 3rd to 5th intercostal spaces indicates right ventricular hypertrophy or dilation, most commonly from pulmonary hypertension. [1,2]
- **Skin and Mucous Membranes:** Central cyanosis (bluish discoloration of the warm mucous membranes of the lips and tongue) indicates arterial desaturation and is most commonly caused by a right-to-left cardiac or intrapulmonary shunt, or severe pulmonary disease. Peripheral cyanosis (bluish discoloration of the cool extremities, earlobes, and nose with pink mucous membranes) indicates low cardiac output with increased peripheral oxygen extraction or peripheral vasoconstriction. [1,2]

### Close/Directed Inspection

After the end-of-bed assessment, the clinician moves closer to examine the patient's hands, face, and anterior chest wall systematically. [1,3]

### Hands:

- **Temperature and Perfusion:** Assess the dorsal aspect of both hands. Cool, clammy hands suggest low cardiac output (cardiogenic shock, decompensated heart failure) with compensatory sympathetic vasoconstriction. Warm, well-perfused hands suggest adequate resting cardiac output. Asymmetric temperature (one hand cooler than the other) may indicate a vascular occlusion or dissection.
- **Peripheral Cyanosis:** Inspect the nail beds and fingertips for a bluish hue. This indicates reduced blood flow to the extremities.
- **Clubbing:** Inspect the profile of the nail (Schamroth's window test: when the dorsal surfaces of the terminal phalanges of corresponding fingers are opposed, a diamond-shaped window of light is normally visible between the nail beds; loss of this window suggests clubbing). Clubbing is caused by increased capillary density and soft tissue hypertrophy at the fingertip, mediated by platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) from megakaryocytes that bypass the pulmonary capillary bed (where they normally fragment into platelets) and lodge in the digital capillaries. In cardiovascular disease, clubbing is associated with cyanotic congenital heart disease (right-to-left shunt allowing megakaryocytes to bypass the lung) and infective endocarditis. [1,2,5]
- **Splinter Hemorrhages:** Linear, dark-red or brown streaks under the nail beds, oriented along the long axis of the nail. They represent microhemorrhages into the nail bed capillaries. In infective endocarditis, they are caused by septic microemboli lodging in the capillaries. However, the most common cause is trauma (e.g., manual labor, gardening), and they are also seen in vasculitis, psoriasis, and trichinosis. In endocarditis, they are typically located in the mid to distal nail bed. [1,5]
- **Osler's Nodes:** Painful, tender, erythematous or violaceous papules on the pads of the fingers and toes. They are immunologic (immune complex-mediated) phenomena of subacute infective endocarditis, representing vasculitis and perivasculitis with microabscess formation in the dermal vessels. They typically last for hours to days. [1,5]
- **Janeway Lesions:** Non-tender, flat, irregular, erythematous or hemorrhagic macules on the palms and soles. They are caused by septic microemboli in the dermal capillaries with microabscess formation, without the immune vasculitic component. Bacteria can be cultured from the lesions. Janeway lesions are more common in acute infective endocarditis, particularly from Staphylococcus aureus. [1,5]
- **Xanthomata:** Yellow cholesterol deposits. Tendon xanthomata (firm, nodular thickening of the extensor tendons of the hands, the Achilles tendons, or the patellar tendons) are pathognomonic for familial hypercholesterolemia and indicate markedly elevated LDL cholesterol and very high cardiovascular risk. Eruptive xanthomata (small, yellow, pruritic papules on the extensor surfaces of the arms, buttocks, and back) are seen in severe hypertriglyceridemia. Palmar xanthomata in the creases of the palms are characteristic of type III hyperlipoproteinemia (dysbetalipoproteinemia). Xanthelasma (soft, yellow plaques on the medial canthi of the eyelids) can be seen with or without hyperlipidemia. [1,2,5]
- **Nicotine Staining:** A yellow-brown discoloration of the middle and index fingers is a sign of chronic cigarette smoking, a major risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
- **Capillary Refill Time:** Press firmly on the nail bed for 5 seconds, then release. Count the time for the color to return to the blanched area. Normal capillary refill time is less than 2 seconds. A prolonged time (>2-3 seconds) indicates reduced peripheral perfusion, which may be due to hypovolemia, low cardiac output, shock, or peripheral vasoconstriction. It is a simple, rapid bedside test of end-organ perfusion. [1,3]

### Face and Eyes:

- **Xanthelasma:** As described above.
- **Corneal Arcus:** A gray-white or yellow opacity around the peripheral cornea, separated from the limbus by a clear zone. In patients under 40-50 years of age, it is strongly associated with hyperlipidemia and increased cardiovascular risk. In older patients, arcus senilis is a common degenerative finding of uncertain predictive value. [1,5]
- **Conjunctival Petechiae:** Small, bright-red hemorrhagic spots on the palpebral or bulbar conjunctiva. In infective endocarditis, they represent microemboli or immune complex-mediated vasculitis.
- **Roth Spots:** Retinal hemorrhages with a pale, white center. They are seen on fundoscopic examination and represent retinal capillary rupture with a central area of fibrin and platelet deposition. In infective endocarditis, they can be caused by either septic microemboli or immune complex vasculitis. They are also seen in leukemia, diabetes, hypertension, and HIV retinopathy. [1,5]
- **Malar Rash:** An erythematous rash across the cheeks and bridge of the nose (butterfly distribution) sparing the nasolabial folds is characteristic of systemic lupus erythematosus, which can cause Libman-Sacks endocarditis, pericarditis, myocarditis, and accelerated atherosclerosis.
- **Dental Hygiene:** Severe periodontal disease and dental caries are risk factors for infective endocarditis in patients with structural heart disease, due to the risk of transient bacteremia with oral flora (viridans group streptococci). [1,5]

### Anterior Chest Wall:

- **Precordial Inspection:** With the patient supine at 30-45 degrees, the clinician inspects the anterior chest wall tangentially, using a penlight if needed, looking for visible pulsations. The normal apex beat is a brief, localized systolic tap visible in the left 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line (or 7-9 cm from the midsternal line). It may not be visible in obese patients or those with emphysema.
- **Displaced Apex Beat:** A visible apex beat displaced inferolaterally (to the 6th or 7th intercostal space, anterior or mid-axillary line) indicates left ventricular dilation. A visible parasternal lift (right ventricular heave) is an abnormal, sustained systolic pulsation to the left of the lower sternum (3rd-5th intercostal spaces) indicating right ventricular hypertrophy or dilation.
- **Surgical Scars:** A median sternotomy scar is the hallmark of prior open-heart surgery (coronary artery bypass grafting, valve replacement, cardiac transplantation, congenital defect repair). A left lateral thoracotomy scar suggests prior mitral valve surgery, repair of coarctation of the aorta, or ligation of a patent ductus arteriosus. Infraclavicular or infra-axillary scars may indicate pacemaker or ICD implantation; the device itself may be palpable as a rectangular subcutaneous mass. Saphenous vein harvest for CABG leaves a long scar along the medial aspect of the calf or thigh; endoscopic vein harvest leaves multiple small, linear scars. Radial artery harvest for CABG leaves a scar on the volar aspect of the left or right forearm.
- **Chest Wall Deformities:** Pectus excavatum (funnel chest) may displace the apex beat laterally and can produce a systolic murmur from compression of the right ventricular outflow tract. Pectus carinatum (pigeon chest) and straight back syndrome (loss of the normal thoracic kyphosis) can narrow the anteroposterior diameter of the chest, potentially causing a pulmonary flow murmur.

## Third: Palpation

### General Palpation Technique

Palpation provides tactile information about cardiac size, contractile function, and the presence of turbulent blood flow (thrills). The palmar surface of the hand, particularly the metacarpophalangeal joints and the proximal palm, is most sensitive for detecting low-frequency vibrations such as thrills and right ventricular heaves. The fingertips are best for localizing the point of maximal impulse (PMI) and for assessing the size, location, and character of the apical impulse. The ulnar border of the hand can also be used to detect thrills. The dorsal surface of the hand is most sensitive to temperature. [1,2,3]

Light palpation is performed first, followed by deeper palpation if the apex beat is not readily appreciated. The clinician should avoid pressing so firmly that the chest wall is displaced, as this can dampen vibrations and obscure the findings. [1,3]

### System-Specific Palpation Sequence

### The Apex Beat (Left Ventricular Impulse)

- **Anatomical Location:** The normal apex beat is the most lateral and inferior point on the chest wall where a definite cardiac impulse can be palpated. It is normally located in the left 5th intercostal space, in the midclavicular line, or 7-9 cm lateral to the midsternal line. The area of the apex beat is normally less than 2.5 cm in diameter (about the size of a quarter). [1,2,3]
- **Technique:** Position the patient supine at 30-45 degrees. Place the palmar surface of the right hand over the left precordium. Systematically identify the most lateral and inferior point where a definite outward systolic impulse is palpated. Mark this point mentally. Then, using the fingertips, define the size, amplitude, duration, and character of the impulse. If the apex beat is not palpable in the supine position, reposition the patient into the left lateral decubitus position (the patient rolls partially onto the left side, bringing the left ventricle into contact with the chest wall), and palpate again. The left lateral decubitus position always displaces the apex beat further laterally, so the location should always be verified supine. [1,2,3]
- **Normal Finding:** A brief, gentle, localized systolic tap that occupies less than half of systole. It is not sustained and does not lift the examining fingers. The amplitude is light-to-moderate.

### Abnormal Findings & Interpretation:

  - *Inferolaterally Displaced Apex Beat:* The apex beat is found in the 6th or 7th intercostal space and lateral to the midclavicular line (anterior or mid-axillary line). This indicates left ventricular dilation (eccentric hypertrophy). Causes include dilated cardiomyopathy, chronic aortic regurgitation, chronic mitral regurgitation, and ischemic cardiomyopathy. The displaced apex beat is a highly sensitive sign of significant left ventricular enlargement. [1,2]
  - *Sustained (Heaving) Apex Beat:* The impulse is forceful, sustained through more than half of systole, and literally "heaves" or lifts the palpating fingers. The location may be normal or displaced. This indicates left ventricular pressure overload (concentric hypertrophy), most commonly from hypertension, aortic stenosis, or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. A sustained apex beat has a high positive likelihood ratio for left ventricular hypertrophy. [1,2,6]
  - *Hyperdynamic (Bounding) Apex Beat:* The impulse is brief, very forceful, and of large amplitude, but not sustained. The impulse is felt as a sharp, vigorous tap. This indicates left ventricular volume overload or a hyperdynamic circulation. Causes include aortic regurgitation, mitral regurgitation, severe anemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, and patent ductus arteriosus. [1,2]
  - *Dyskinetic Apex Beat:* A broad, diffuse, disorganized impulse that may have a paradoxical outward bulge during early systole. This indicates a left ventricular aneurysm or a large regional wall motion abnormality, typically from a previous transmural anterior myocardial infarction. The impulse is often palpable medial and superior to the expected location, in the 3rd or 4th intercostal space. [1,2]
  - *Double Impulse:* Two distinct outward impulses during a single cardiac cycle. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the initial impulse is from vigorous atrial contraction against a stiff, non-compliant ventricle (a palpable S4), followed by the sustained systolic impulse from dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. A double impulse in the left lateral decubitus position may be a palpable S3 in a young, thin individual (physiological). [1,2]
  - *Tapping Apex Beat:* A palpable, sharp, first heart sound. This is characteristic of mitral stenosis, where a loud, palpable S1 (tapping quality) is caused by the wide-open, pliable mitral valve leaflets snapping closed at the onset of systole. The presence of a tapping apex beat indicates a mobile, non-calcified anterior mitral valve leaflet. [1,2]
  - *Absent Apex Beat:* The apex beat may not be palpable due to obesity, emphysema (hyperinflated lungs interposed between the heart and chest wall), pericardial effusion (fluid insulating the heart from the chest wall), or dextrocardia. In pericardial effusion, the apex beat may be palpable but the heart sounds are muffled, and the JVP is elevated. [1,2]

### Right Ventricular Heave (Parasternal Lift)

- **Anatomical Location:** The left parasternal region, specifically the 3rd, 4th, and 5th intercostal spaces just to the left of the sternum.
- **Technique:** Place the heel of the right palm firmly over the left parasternal area. A sustained systolic lift that pushes the hand away from the chest wall is a right ventricular heave. The patient should be breathing quietly. The heave may be accentuated during inspiration. [1,2,3]
- **Normal Finding:** The right ventricle is a low-pressure chamber located posterior to the sternum. In normal individuals, it is not palpable.
- **Abnormal Finding & Interpretation:** A palpable parasternal lift indicates right ventricular hypertrophy or dilation. This is a highly specific sign of elevated right ventricular systolic pressure or volume overload. Causes include pulmonary hypertension (the most common cause), pulmonic stenosis, atrial septal defect, tricuspid regurgitation, and massive pulmonary embolism. A palpable parasternal lift correlates with a right ventricular systolic pressure of at least 40-50 mmHg. [1,2,6]
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** The presence of a right ventricular heave is a highly specific bedside indicator of right ventricular pressure or volume overload and directly alters clinical probability.

### Thrills

- **Anatomical Location:** All four valvular areas and the suprasternal notch should be palpated for thrills.
- **Technique:** Use the palmar surface of the metacarpophalangeal joints or the ulnar border of the hand, applying firm pressure. The patient should be in the supine position at 30-45 degrees. A thrill is a palpable vibratory sensation, often described as feeling like a purring cat's throat. It indicates turbulent blood flow that produces a murmur of grade 4/6 or louder. The location and timing (systolic, diastolic, or continuous) of the thrill identify the causative lesion. [1,2,3]
- **Aortic Area (Right 2nd Intercostal Space):** A systolic thrill here indicates severe aortic stenosis.
- **Pulmonic Area (Left 2nd Intercostal Space):** A systolic thrill indicates severe pulmonic stenosis. A continuous thrill indicates a patent ductus arteriosus.
- **Tricuspid Area (Left 4th-5th Intercostal Spaces):** A systolic thrill indicates a ventricular septal defect or severe tricuspid regurgitation.
- **Mitral Area (Apex):** A systolic thrill at the apex, often radiating to the axilla, indicates severe mitral regurgitation. A diastolic thrill at the apex is the hallmark of severe mitral stenosis.
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** A thrill is always pathological and signifies significant structural heart disease. Its presence mandates echocardiography.

### Palpable Heart Sounds

- **Palpable S1:** A palpable first heart sound at the apex is a normal finding in young, thin individuals with a hyperdynamic circulation. In mitral stenosis, a palpable, tapping S1 indicates a pliable, mobile anterior mitral valve leaflet that is suitable for balloon valvotomy. [1,2]
- **Palpable P2 (Pulmonary Component of S2):** A palpable impulse in the left 2nd intercostal space synchronous with S2 indicates a loud P2, which is a specific sign of pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary artery systolic pressure >50-60 mmHg). The normal P2 is audible but not palpable. [1,2]
- **Palpable S3:** A palpable early diastolic impulse at the apex, corresponding to an audible S3 gallop, indicates elevated left ventricular filling pressures and a non-compliant, volume-overloaded ventricle. It is felt as a brief outward impulse after the systolic impulse. [1,2]
- **Palpable S4:** A palpable late diastolic (presystolic) impulse at the apex, corresponding to an audible S4 gallop, indicates a stiff, non-compliant ventricle and decreased diastolic compliance (e.g., from hypertension, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or ischemic heart disease). [1,2]

## Fourth: Percussion

**Percussion: Historical/Obsolete — Not Clinically Practiced.**

Percussion of the heart to determine the cardiac borders was a technique developed in the 19th century before the advent of modern imaging. It is a highly inaccurate and unreliable method for detecting cardiomegaly or chamber enlargement, with very low sensitivity and specificity compared to the gold standard, echocardiography. The cardiac dullness to percussion is affected by lung inflation, body habitus, and the presence of a pericardial effusion, all of which are better assessed with imaging. The American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology do not include cardiac percussion in their recommended clinical examination guidelines for any cardiovascular condition. The time that would have been spent on percussion is redirected to a more detailed assessment of the JVP waveform, dynamic auscultation maneuvers, and the peripheral vascular examination, which provide high-yield clinical information. [1,2,3]

## Fifth: Auscultation

### The Stethoscope

Auscultation is the most important skill in the cardiovascular physical examination and requires a high-quality stethoscope used correctly. The modern stethoscope has two chest pieces: the diaphragm and the bell. [1,2,3]

- **Diaphragm:** The larger, flat side of the chest piece. It is designed to filter out low-frequency sounds and transmit high-frequency sounds. It must be pressed firmly against the skin to create a seal and dampen the skin's own low-frequency vibrations. The diaphragm is used to listen to: S1, S2, the systolic murmurs of aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, and ventricular septal defect; the early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation; the continuous murmur of patent ductus arteriosus; ejection clicks; opening snaps; and pericardial friction rubs. [1,2,3]
- **Bell:** The smaller, concave side of the chest piece. It is designed to transmit low-frequency sounds and filter out high-frequency sounds. It must be applied very lightly to the skin—just enough to create an air seal without compressing the skin. Compressing the skin with the bell stretches the skin, converting it into a diaphragm and filtering out the very low-frequency sounds the bell is designed to detect. The bell is used to listen to: S3 (ventricular gallop), S4 (atrial gallop), and the low-pitched, mid-diastolic rumbling murmur of mitral stenosis. [1,2,3]

### Auscultation Points & Systematic Sequence

The heart should be auscultated in a consistent, systematic order at each of the four classic valvular areas. The clinician should "inch" the stethoscope from one area to the next rather than jumping, to maintain focus and ensure all areas are covered. [1,2,3]

- **Aortic Area:** Right 2nd intercostal space, just lateral to the sternum. Listen with the diaphragm.
- **Pulmonic Area:** Left 2nd intercostal space, just lateral to the sternum. Listen with the diaphragm.
- **Tricuspid Area:** Left lower sternal border (4th and 5th intercostal spaces). Listen with the diaphragm, then repeat with the bell.
- **Mitral Area (Apex):** Left 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line. Listen with the diaphragm, then reposition the patient to the left lateral decubitus position and listen with the bell.

At each area, the clinician must systematically analyze: (1) the intensity and splitting of S1; (2) the intensity and splitting of S2; (3) the presence of extra heart sounds in systole (ejection clicks, midsystolic clicks) and diastole (opening snap, S3, S4, pericardial knock); and (4) the presence, timing, intensity, pitch, quality, shape, and radiation of any murmurs. [1,2,3]

### Normal Sounds

- **S1 (First Heart Sound):** "Lub." Produced by the closure of the mitral (M1) and tricuspid (T1) valves at the onset of ventricular systole. The sound is generated by the sudden deceleration of blood and the vibration of the valve leaflets, chordae tendineae, and adjacent ventricular walls. M1 slightly precedes T1. S1 is normally a single sound, best heard at the apex. [1,2]
- **S2 (Second Heart Sound):** "Dub." Produced by the closure of the aortic (A2) and pulmonic (P2) valves at the onset of ventricular diastole. A2 normally precedes P2 because left ventricular systole is shorter than right ventricular systole. During quiet expiration, A2 and P2 are separated by less than 30 milliseconds, which is perceived as a single sound. During inspiration, the increased venous return to the right ventricle prolongs right ventricular ejection, delaying P2, and the A2-P2 interval widens to 40-60 milliseconds. This produces physiological splitting of S2, which is heard as a "lub-T-dub" sound, best appreciated in the left 2nd intercostal space. The splitting is during inspiration only; the components fuse during expiration. [1,2]

### Adventitious (Abnormal) Sounds

- **Loud S1:** Mechanism: The mitral valve leaflets are wide open at the onset of systole because of a persistently high left atrial-to-left ventricular pressure gradient (mitral stenosis) or increased flow velocity (hyperdynamic circulation, short PR interval). Wide-open leaflets close with greater excursion and velocity, producing a louder sound. A loud S1 in mitral stenosis indicates a pliable, non-calcified anterior mitral valve leaflet. Clinical Utility: Tier 1 for assessing mitral valve pliability. [1,2]
- **Soft S1:** Mechanism: The mitral leaflets are nearly closed at the onset of systole (long PR interval, severe aortic regurgitation) or the valve is rigid and calcified (severe calcific mitral stenosis). Soft S1 is a sign of severe calcific mitral stenosis and indicates that the valve is no longer suitable for balloon valvotomy. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1,2]
- **Loud P2 (Accentuated P2):** Mechanism: Increased pulmonary artery pressure at the time of pulmonic valve closure generates a louder sound. A loud P2 is a specific sign of pulmonary hypertension. If the P2 component of S2 is louder than the A2 component in the pulmonic area, pulmonary hypertension is likely. A palpable P2 is even more specific. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1,2]
- **Wide, Fixed Splitting of S2:** The A2-P2 interval is prolonged and does not vary with respiration. The splitting is audible during both inspiration and expiration. Mechanism: Constant, nearly equal right and left ventricular stroke volumes throughout the respiratory cycle. This is pathognomonic for atrial septal defect, where the left-to-right shunt fixes the pulmonary blood flow. Clinical Utility: Tier 1; pathognomonic. [1,2]
- **Paradoxical (Reversed) Splitting of S2:** The A2-P2 interval splits during expiration and fuses into a single sound during inspiration—the opposite of normal physiology. Mechanism: Aortic valve closure is delayed (left bundle branch block, severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) so that P2 occurs before A2. During inspiration, the normal delay in P2 brings it closer to the already-delayed A2, closing the split. Paradoxical splitting is a reliable sign of severe left ventricular outflow obstruction or delayed left ventricular activation. Clinical Utility: Tier 1 for detecting left bundle branch block or severe aortic stenosis. [1,2]
- **S3 (Ventricular Gallop):** A low-pitched, early diastolic sound, occurring 120-180 milliseconds after S2, during the rapid ventricular filling phase. Heard best at the apex with the bell in the left lateral decubitus position. The rhythm is "ken-tuck-y" (S1-S2-S3). Mechanism: Sudden deceleration of the column of blood entering a volume-overloaded, non-compliant ventricle during early diastole, causing the ventricular wall to vibrate. A left ventricular S3 indicates elevated left ventricular filling pressures (PCWP >20 mmHg) and reduced ejection fraction. In adults over 40, an S3 is pathological and is a poor prognostic sign in heart failure. An S3 is a normal finding in children and healthy young adults (physiological S3) due to vigorous, elastic ventricular filling. Clinical Utility: Tier 1; high specificity for left ventricular dysfunction when detected in an adult with heart failure symptoms. [1,2,6]
- **S4 (Atrial Gallop):** A low-pitched, late diastolic (presystolic) sound, occurring just before S1. Heard best at the apex with the bell in the left lateral decubitus position. The rhythm is "ten-nes-see" (S4-S1-S2). Mechanism: Atrial contraction forcing blood into a stiff, non-compliant ventricle, causing the ventricular wall to vibrate. An S4 is always pathological. It indicates decreased ventricular compliance (diastolic dysfunction) from hypertrophy, ischemia, or fibrosis. An S4 requires the presence of atrial contraction and is therefore absent in atrial fibrillation. Clinical Utility: Tier 1; a sensitive sign of significant diastolic dysfunction. [1,2]
- **Opening Snap:** A high-pitched, early diastolic sound heard best between the apex and left lower sternal border with the diaphragm. It occurs 0.04-0.12 seconds after S2. Mechanism: The sudden, abrupt halt of the opening motion of a stenotic, but still pliable, mitral valve. The A2-OS interval is inversely proportional to left atrial pressure. The shorter the interval, the higher the left atrial pressure and the more severe the mitral stenosis. An opening snap confirms that the mitral stenosis is of rheumatic origin and that the valve is still pliable enough for balloon valvotomy. Clinical Utility: Tier 1 for detecting rheumatic mitral stenosis and assessing its severity and suitability for intervention. [1,2]
- **Ejection Click:** A high-pitched, early systolic click heard best at the apex or base with the diaphragm. Mechanism: The abrupt opening of a stenotic, but mobile, semilunar valve (aortic or pulmonic stenosis) or the dilation of the great artery. An aortic ejection click is characteristic of a bicuspid aortic valve. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1,2]
- **Midsystolic Click:** A high-pitched click heard best at the apex or left lower sternal border in mid-to-late systole, often followed by a late systolic murmur. Mechanism: Prolapse of one or both mitral valve leaflets into the left atrium during systole, causing sudden tensing of the redundant leaflet and chordae tendineae. The click moves earlier in systole with maneuvers that reduce left ventricular volume (standing, Valsalva strain) and later with maneuvers that increase volume (squatting). This is the hallmark of mitral valve prolapse. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1,2]
- **Pericardial Friction Rub:** A high-pitched, scratchy, leathery sound with a superficial, close-to-the-ear quality, heard best at the left lower sternal border with the diaphragm, with the patient sitting upright and leaning forward. It has three components corresponding to atrial systole, ventricular systole, and ventricular diastole. It is pathognomonic for acute pericarditis. The rub may be fleeting and positional. Clinical Utility: Tier 1; pathognomonic. [1,2]

### Murmurs

- **Midsystolic (Crescendo-Decrescendo) Ejection Murmur:** A harsh, diamond-shaped murmur beginning after S1, peaking in midsystole, and ending before S2. Heard best at the right upper sternal border (aortic stenosis) or left upper sternal border (pulmonic stenosis). The murmur of aortic stenosis radiates to the carotids. The carotid upstroke is parvus et tardus. The A2 component of S2 may be soft or absent. [1,2]
- **Holosystolic (Pansystolic) Plateau Murmur:** A blowing, uniform-intensity murmur beginning with S1, extending throughout systole, and ending at or beyond S2. Heard best at the apex, radiating to the axilla (mitral regurgitation), or at the left lower sternal border, increasing with inspiration (tricuspid regurgitation), or at the left lower sternal border with a thrill (ventricular septal defect). [1,2]
- **Early Diastolic, Decrescendo Murmur:** A high-pitched, blowing murmur beginning immediately after S2, decrescendo throughout diastole. Heard best at the left sternal border with the diaphragm, with the patient seated, leaning forward, in held end-expiration. This is the murmur of aortic regurgitation. The carotid upstroke is bounding and collapsing (water-hammer pulse). [1,2]
- **Mid-to-Late Diastolic, Low-Pitched Rumbling Murmur:** A low-pitched murmur heard best at the apex with the bell in the left lateral decubitus position. It begins after an opening snap (if present) and has presystolic accentuation in sinus rhythm. This is the murmur of mitral stenosis. [1,2]
- **Continuous Murmur:** A machinery-like murmur beginning in systole, continuing through S2, and extending into diastole. Heard best at the left infraclavicular area. This is pathognomonic for patent ductus arteriosus. [1,2]

### Dynamic Auscultation Maneuvers

Maneuvers that alter preload, afterload, and contractility are essential for distinguishing between similar-sounding murmurs. [1,2,3]

- **Inspiration:** Increases venous return to the right heart. All right-sided murmurs and sounds (tricuspid regurgitation, tricuspid stenosis, pulmonic regurgitation, pulmonic stenosis, right-sided S3 and S4) increase in intensity. Left-sided murmurs are unchanged or decrease. This is the physiological basis of Carvallo's sign: the inspiratory accentuation of the holosystolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation. Clinical Utility: Tier 1. [1,2]
- **Expiration:** Increases venous return to the left heart. Left-sided murmurs become louder. Expiration during leaning forward is the optimal position to hear the aortic regurgitation murmur. [1,2]
- **Valsalva Strain Phase:** The patient bears down against a closed glottis. This increases intrathoracic pressure and decreases venous return to both ventricles. The murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy INCREASES in intensity because the reduced left ventricular volume worsens the dynamic outflow tract obstruction. The murmur of mitral valve prolapse occurs EARLIER in systole, and the click becomes more prominent. Most other murmurs (aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation) decrease in intensity. [1,2]
- **Squatting:** The patient squats from a standing position. This increases venous return (increased preload) and increases systemic vascular resistance (increased afterload). The murmur of aortic stenosis INCREASES (increased stroke volume). The murmur of mitral regurgitation INCREASES (increased afterload augments regurgitant flow). The murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy DECREASES (increased ventricular volume widens the outflow tract). [1,2]
- **Isometric Handgrip:** Sustained, firm handgrip for 20-30 seconds increases systemic vascular resistance (afterload). The murmurs of mitral regurgitation and aortic regurgitation INCREASE. The murmur of aortic stenosis DECREASES. [1,2]

## Sixth: Special Tests, Maneuvers & Eponymous Signs

### Tests for Systolic Murmurs

- **Name:** Response to a Post-Extrasystolic Beat.
- **Technique:** During auscultation, observe the murmur on the beat following a premature ventricular contraction (PVC) or a long pause in atrial fibrillation.
- **Positive Test Definition:** The murmur intensity increases on the post-extrasystolic beat.
- **Mechanism:** The compensatory pause after a PVC allows increased diastolic filling time and increased ventricular filling volume. This increases stroke volume on the subsequent beat. A stenotic valve has a fixed orifice; the increased stroke volume produces a higher transvalvular gradient and a louder murmur.
- **Disease Association:** Aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (the murmur of mitral regurgitation from a flail leaflet does not increase).
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** This is a powerful bedside tool to differentiate aortic stenosis from mitral regurgitation.
- **Name:** Carvallo's Sign.
- **Technique:** Auscultate the left lower sternal border for a holosystolic murmur while the patient breathes normally. Instruct the patient to take a slow, deep inspiration and hold it for 2-3 seconds.
- **Positive Test Definition:** The murmur is louder during inspiration and softer during expiration.
- **Mechanism:** Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, augmenting right ventricular stroke volume and regurgitant flow in tricuspid regurgitation.
- **Disease Association:** Tricuspid regurgitation.
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** Carvallo's sign is the definitive bedside maneuver for differentiating the holosystolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation (which increases with inspiration) from mitral regurgitation (which does not).

### Tests for Diastolic Murmurs

- **Name:** Austin Flint Murmur Maneuver.
- **Technique:** In a patient with severe aortic regurgitation, auscultate the apex with the bell in the left lateral decubitus position. Administer amyl nitrite (a potent vasodilator) and re-auscultate.
- **Positive Test Definition:** The diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex (Austin Flint murmur) DECREASES in intensity after amyl nitrite, whereas the murmur of organic mitral stenosis INCREASES.
- **Mechanism:** Amyl nitrite reduces systemic vascular resistance, which reduces the regurgitant volume in aortic regurgitation. The smaller regurgitant jet strikes the anterior mitral leaflet with less force, reducing the functional obstruction. In organic mitral stenosis, the reduced afterload increases cardiac output, increasing flow across the stenotic mitral valve and making the murmur louder.
- **Disease Association:** Used to distinguish an Austin Flint murmur from organic mitral stenosis.
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 2):** Amyl nitrite is rarely available. Echocardiography is the definitive test.

### Tests for Pericardial Disease

- **Name:** Pulsus Paradoxus Measurement.
- **Technique:** Inflate the blood pressure cuff above the systolic pressure. Deflate slowly, noting the pressure at which the first Korotkoff sounds are heard only during expiration. Continue to deflate until sounds are audible during both inspiration and expiration.
- **Positive Test Definition:** The inspiratory drop in systolic blood pressure exceeds 10 mmHg.
- **Mechanism:** Exaggerated ventricular interdependence. In cardiac tamponade, the inspiratory increase in right ventricular filling shifts the interventricular septum leftward, impeding left ventricular filling and reducing left ventricular stroke volume during inspiration.
- **Disease Association:** Cardiac tamponade, severe asthma/COPD exacerbation, constrictive pericarditis (present in ~30%).
- **Sensitivity & Specificity:** For detecting cardiac tamponade, pulsus paradoxus >12 mmHg has a sensitivity of 98% and a specificity of 83%. [6]
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** This is a mandatory, life-saving bedside test for suspected cardiac tamponade.
- **Name:** Kussmaul's Sign.
- **Technique:** Inspect the JVP during quiet, spontaneous respiration at 45 degrees.
- **Positive Test Definition:** A rise (or failure to fall) in the JVP during inspiration.
- **Mechanism:** The right ventricle cannot accommodate the inspiratory increase in venous return due to a rigid, non-compliant pericardium.
- **Disease Association:** Constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, right ventricular infarction, and severe right-sided heart failure. Critically, it is NOT present in cardiac tamponade.
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** The presence of Kussmaul's sign is the key distinguishing feature between constrictive pericarditis and cardiac tamponade.

### Tests for Peripheral Arterial Disease

- **Name:** Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI).
- **Technique:** With the patient supine, measure systolic blood pressure in both brachial arteries using a handheld Doppler probe. Measure the systolic pressure at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries in both ankles. The ABI for each leg is the higher ankle pressure divided by the higher brachial pressure.
- **Positive Test Definition:** ABI ≤0.90.
- **Disease Association:** Peripheral arterial disease (PAD).
- **Sensitivity & Specificity:** For detecting >50% stenosis on angiography, the sensitivity is 79-95% and the specificity is 95-100%. [6]
- **Clinical Utility (Tier 1):** The ABI is a simple, non-invasive bedside test with excellent performance for diagnosing and ruling out PAD.

### Obsolete Signs

- **Levine Sign (Clenched Fist Over Sternum):** The patient clenches a fist over their sternum to describe chest pain. Historically taught as a sign of ischemic chest pain, it is non-specific and no more valuable than a careful history. The character of the pain (pressure, heaviness) and its relationship to exertion are far more discriminating. [1,3]
- **Hill's Sign (Poplietal-Cuff Sign):** A higher systolic blood pressure in the lower extremity compared to the upper extremity in aortic regurgitation. The sign is non-specific, occurs in other hyperdynamic states, and echocardiography directly visualizes the regurgitant jet with high sensitivity and specificity. [1,3]

## Seventh: Synthesis & Clinical Pattern Recognition

### Completing the Examination

The cardiovascular examination is never complete without examining the following related systems: [1,3]

- Complete peripheral pulse examination (carotid, radial, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial).
- Auscultation of the carotid and femoral arteries for bruits.
- Examination of the abdomen for an aortic aneurysm, renal bruits, hepatomegaly, and ascites.
- Fundoscopic examination in patients with hypertension or diabetes to assess for retinopathy.
- Examination of the lower extremities for dependent edema, venous stasis changes, and signs of chronic arterial insufficiency.

### Common Syndromes & Their Physical Signatures

### Severe Aortic Stenosis:

- Inspection: May be normal or reveal a sustained apical impulse.
- Palpation: Pulsus parvus et tardus (carotid upstroke is weak and delayed). Sustained, heaving apex beat (non-displaced). Systolic thrill at the right upper sternal border.
- Auscultation: Harsh, crescendo-decrescendo midsystolic murmur at the right upper sternal border, radiating to the carotids. The murmur peaks late in systole. Soft or absent A2. Paradoxical splitting of S2 may be present. S4 gallop. [1,2]

### Chronic Severe Mitral Regurgitation:

- Inspection: Hyperdynamic, displaced precordial impulse.
- Palpation: Hyperdynamic, displaced, diffuse apex beat (inferolaterally displaced).
- Auscultation: Holosystolic, blowing murmur at the apex, radiating to the left axilla. Soft S1. Wide, often physiological, splitting of S2 (early A2). S3 gallop (volume overload). The murmur is constant in intensity (plateau). [1,2]

### Pericardial Tamponade:

- Inspection: Tachypneic, distressed, using accessory muscles. Neck veins are markedly distended.
- Palpation: Pulsus paradoxus (>12 mmHg). Apex beat may be difficult to palpate.
- Auscultation: Muffled, distant heart sounds (Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, elevated JVP). Clear lung fields (no crackles).
- Key Distinguishing Feature: The combination of shock (hypotension), elevated JVP, and clear lung fields on auscultation strongly suggests cardiac tamponade or right ventricular infarction. [1,2]

### Documentation

**Normal Examination:**

"The patient is in no acute distress. The heart has a regular rate and rhythm. The JVP is 2 cm above the sternal angle at 45 degrees. The carotid upstrokes are brisk and full. The apex beat is palpable in the left 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line, and is brief and non-sustained. There are no parasternal lifts or thrills. Auscultation reveals a normal S1 and physiologically split S2. There are no S3, S4, murmurs, clicks, or rubs. The peripheral pulses are full and symmetric. There is no edema."

**Abnormal Examination (e.g., Severe Aortic Stenosis):**

"The patient appears fatigued. The heart rate is regular. The carotid upstrokes are weak and delayed (pulsus parvus et tardus). The JVP is normal. The apex beat is sustained but not displaced. A systolic thrill is palpable at the right upper sternal border. Auscultation reveals a grade 4/6 harsh, late-peaking, crescendo-decrescendo midsystolic murmur, loudest at the right upper sternal border, radiating to both carotid arteries. The A2 component of S2 is absent. An S4 gallop is present at the apex."

## Eighth: Common Errors, Pitfalls & Tricks

### Frequent Mistakes Students Make

- **Incorrect Patient Positioning:** Failing to examine the patient in multiple positions (supine, left lateral decubitus, seated leaning forward). A clinically significant aortic regurgitation murmur can be completely missed if the patient is not examined seated, leaning forward, in held end-expiration. An S3 gallop and the murmur of mitral stenosis require the left lateral decubitus position. [1,3]
- **Inadequate Exposure:** Auscultating through clothing produces artifact and attenuates heart sounds. The stethoscope must always be placed on bare skin. [1,3]
- **Incorrect Stethoscope Use:** Using the bell with firm pressure, which stretches the skin and converts it into a diaphragm, filtering out the low-frequency sounds it is intended to detect. Using the diaphragm with light pressure, which fails to dampen skin vibrations. [1,3]
- **Palpating Too Deeply or Too Superficially:** Palpating too deeply can miss a faint thrill. Palpating too superficially can miss a displaced or dyskinetic apex beat. [1,3]
- **Skipping Steps in the Sequence:** A common error is to move directly from inspection to auscultation without palpating. Palpation is essential for detecting thrills, parasternal heaves, and for characterizing the apex beat, all of which guide the subsequent auscultation. [1,3]
- **Misidentification of Systole and Diastole:** When the heart rate is rapid, it is difficult to distinguish systole from diastole. The clinician must always simultaneously palpate the carotid artery while auscultating: the carotid upstroke immediately follows S1 and marks the onset of systole. [1,3]

### Anatomical Variants Mimicking Pathology

- **Split S1:** A normally narrow split S1 (M1-T1) can be heard in healthy individuals and is a normal variant, not a sign of pathology.
- **Physiological S3:** An S3 is a normal finding in children, adolescents, and adults under 35-40 years of age, and during pregnancy. It must be differentiated from a pathological S3 by the clinical context.
- **Innocent Flow Murmurs (Still's Murmur):** A low-to-medium pitched, vibratory, midsystolic murmur heard at the left lower sternal border or apex in children and young adults, without any other abnormalities. It is due to normal flow across the aortic or pulmonary valve and is entirely benign.
- **Venous Hum:** A continuous, low-pitched murmur heard over the supraclavicular fossa, obliterated by light pressure on the internal jugular vein or by turning the head. It is a normal finding, particularly in children and young adults, caused by turbulent flow in the jugular venous system.

### Difficult Patient Populations

- **Obese Patients:** The cardiac examination is challenging due to thick chest walls. The apex beat may not be palpable. Heart sounds may be distant. The JVP may be difficult to visualize. The clinician must rely more heavily on auscultatory findings and diagnostic imaging.
- **Patients with Emphysema:** The hyperinflated lungs interpose between the heart and the chest wall, making the apex beat impalpable and heart sounds distant. The heart sounds may be heard best in the epigastrium. The JVP may be obscured by the overlying sternocleidomastoid muscle.
- **Confused or Uncooperative Patients:** The examination must be adapted. The JVP and dynamic auscultation maneuvers requiring patient cooperation (Valsalva, deep inspiration) may not be possible. The clinician must focus on the information that can be reliably obtained (blood pressure, pulse character, heart sounds, edema).

### Tricks of the Trade

- Use tangential lighting to visualize the JVP pulsations.
- If the apex beat is not palpable supine, roll the patient into the left lateral decubitus position.
- To time events in the cardiac cycle, always auscultate while palpating the carotid artery. The sound that precedes the carotid upstroke is S1.
- The diagnosis of a murmur often relies less on its grade and more on its response to simple bedside maneuvers (standing vs. squatting, handgrip).
- A normal resting JVP in a patient with dyspnea and edema should prompt the assessment of the hepatojugular reflux.

## Ninth: Key Guidelines & Evidence Base

### Authoritative Society Guidelines

- The American Heart Association (AHA) and the American College of Cardiology (ACC) periodically publish comprehensive clinical practice guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease, heart failure, and hypertension. These guidelines explicitly define the role and value of specific physical examination findings in diagnosis and risk stratification. The 2020 ACC/AHA Guideline for the Management of Patients With Valvular Heart Disease describes the classic physical examination findings for each valve lesion and their correlation with severity. [2]
- The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure defines the cardinal physical signs of congestion (elevated JVP, hepatojugular reflux, S3 gallop, crackles, edema) and hypoperfusion, which form the basis of the clinical bedside hemodynamic profile. [4]

### Seminal Evidence

The "Rational Clinical Examination" series published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) has systematically reviewed the evidence base for physical diagnosis maneuvers. [6]

- **Jugular Venous Distension and Hepatojugular Reflux:** A systematic review found that an elevated resting JVP and a positive abdominojugular test have very high positive likelihood ratios for elevated pulmonary capillary wedge pressure in patients with known or suspected heart failure. [6]
- **S3 Gallop:** The presence of an audible S3 in an adult has a high positive likelihood ratio for reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and elevated filling pressures. The interobserver agreement for detecting an S3 is moderate. [6]
- **Pulsus Paradoxus:** A pulsus paradoxus >12 mmHg has very high sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of cardiac tamponade in patients with a pericardial effusion. [6]
- **Clinical Detection of Aortic Stenosis:** The finding of a delayed carotid upstroke (pulsus parvus et tardus), a late-peaking systolic murmur, and a soft or absent A2 is the clinical triad of severe aortic stenosis. [6]

### The Role of Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS)

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has transformed the cardiovascular assessment. For detecting a pericardial effusion, POCUS has a sensitivity and specificity approaching 100% and has replaced the physical examination as the definitive bedside test. For assessing left ventricular function, POCUS is more sensitive and specific than detecting an S3 gallop or a displaced apex beat. For detecting ascites, POCUS has a sensitivity exceeding 95%, surpassing the physical examination signs of shifting dullness and fluid thrill. POCUS is now the Tier 1 test for these diagnoses, and the physical examination serves as a screening tool to guide the appropriate use of POCUS. [3]
