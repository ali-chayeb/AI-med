## Part I: RAAS Inhibitors

### 1.1 ACE Inhibitors (Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors)

- **Drug Names:** Lisinopril, Ramipril, Enalapril, Captopril, Perindopril, Quinapril, Trandolapril, Fosinopril. The "-pril" suffix identifies this class. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs competitively inhibit the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), a dipeptidyl carboxypeptidase found on the surface of endothelial cells, primarily in the pulmonary circulation. ACE normally performs two functions: (1) it cleaves the decapeptide angiotensin I to the octapeptide angiotensin II, the most potent endogenous vasoconstrictor; and (2) it degrades bradykinin, a potent vasodilator peptide, into inactive fragments. By inhibiting ACE, these drugs reduce circulating and tissue levels of angiotensin II, leading to decreased arteriolar vasoconstriction and reduced aldosterone secretion from the adrenal zona glomerulosa. Decreased aldosterone promotes renal sodium and water excretion and potassium retention. Simultaneously, the accumulation of bradykinin enhances vasodilation by increasing the production of nitric oxide and prostacyclin from endothelial cells, though this mechanism is also responsible for the characteristic dry cough. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Hypertension: First-line agent in most patients, including those with diabetes mellitus and proteinuric chronic kidney disease. [3]
  - Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF, LVEF ≤40%): Class I recommendation by the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline. Reduces mortality, heart failure hospitalizations, and improves symptoms. [4]
  - Post-Acute Myocardial Infarction: Especially in patients with anterior MI, LVEF <40%, or clinical heart failure. Reduces mortality and the development of heart failure. [4,5]
  - Chronic Kidney Disease with Proteinuria: Slows progression of diabetic and non-diabetic nephropathy by reducing intraglomerular pressure (efferent arteriolar dilation). [3]
- **Contraindications:** Pregnancy (teratogenic, causes fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, skull hypoplasia, and neonatal death – FDA Category X), history of angioedema with any ACE inhibitor, bilateral renal artery stenosis (risk of acute renal failure due to loss of efferent arteriolar constriction), and severe hyperkalemia. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Lisinopril: 5-40 mg orally once daily. Start at 2.5-5 mg for heart failure.
  - Ramipril: 2.5-10 mg orally once daily (or divided twice daily).
  - Enalapril: 5-20 mg orally once or twice daily. IV enalaprilat available.
  - Captopril: 6.25-50 mg orally three times daily. Short half-life; first ACE inhibitor developed. [1,2]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Dry, non-productive cough: Occurs in 5-20% of patients, more common in women and those of East Asian ancestry. Mechanism is bradykinin and substance P accumulation, which sensitize airway C-fibers. Resolves within 1-4 weeks of discontinuation. Switch to ARB. [1,2]
  - Angioedema: Potentially life-threatening swelling of the tongue, lips, pharynx, or larynx. Occurs in 0.1-0.7% of patients. Bradykinin-mediated. Can occur at any time during therapy, even after years of use. Absolute contraindication to all future ACE inhibitor use. [1,2]
  - Hyperkalemia: Due to decreased aldosterone secretion. Risk is increased in patients with renal insufficiency, diabetes, older age, and concomitant use of potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements. [1,2]
  - Acute Kidney Injury: In volume-depleted patients or those with bilateral renal artery stenosis, the GFR becomes dependent on angiotensin II-mediated efferent arteriolar constriction. ACE inhibition dilates the efferent arteriole, reducing glomerular filtration pressure and causing a precipitous drop in GFR. [1,2]
  - Hypotension: Particularly the first dose, in hyponatremic or volume-depleted patients. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for hypertension and heart failure, unless contraindications develop.

### 1.2 Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs)

- **Drug Names:** Losartan, Valsartan, Candesartan, Irbesartan, Olmesartan, Telmisartan, Azilsartan. The "-sartan" suffix identifies this class. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are non-peptide competitive antagonists at the angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor. By blocking the AT1 receptor, they prevent angiotensin II from exerting its vasoconstrictive, aldosterone-secreting, renal sodium-retaining, and cellular growth-promoting (hypertrophy, fibrosis) effects. Unlike ACE inhibitors, ARBs do not inhibit bradykinin degradation, because ACE activity is unaffected. Consequently, the dry cough and angioedema are significantly less common. The clinical outcomes and hemodynamic effects are essentially identical to ACE inhibitors. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Identical to ACE inhibitors, and are the preferred alternative for patients who experience ACE inhibitor-induced cough. Hypertension, HFrEF, post-MI with LV dysfunction, and proteinuric CKD. [3,4]
- **Contraindications:** Pregnancy (teratogenic; same mechanism as ACE inhibitors, interfering with fetal renal development), bilateral renal artery stenosis, severe hyperkalemia. Angioedema is a relative contraindication; cross-reactivity is rare but has been reported. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Losartan: 25-100 mg orally once daily (or divided twice daily for larger doses).
  - Valsartan: 80-320 mg orally once daily.
  - Candesartan: 8-32 mg orally once daily.
  - Irbesartan: 150-300 mg orally once daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury (in high-risk patients), hypotension. The incidence of cough is comparable to placebo. Angioedema is rare (much less than ACE inhibitors). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for chronic conditions.

### 1.3 Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor (ARNI)

- **Drug Name:** Sacubitril/Valsartan. This is a fixed-dose combination of a neprilysin inhibitor (sacubitril) and an ARB (valsartan) in a 1:1 molar ratio. [2,4]
- **Mechanism of Action:** This class represents a dual mechanism. Sacubitrilat (the active metabolite of the prodrug sacubitril) inhibits neprilysin, a neutral endopeptidase that degrades biologically active natriuretic peptides, including atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP), as well as bradykinin, adrenomedullin, and other vasoactive peptides. By inhibiting neprilysin, levels of these beneficial peptides increase, promoting natriuresis, diuresis, vasodilation, and anti-fibrotic/anti-remodeling effects. However, neprilysin also degrades angiotensin II. To prevent the counterproductive accumulation of angiotensin II, the ARB valsartan is co-formulated to block AT1 receptors. The net effect is simultaneous augmentation of the protective natriuretic peptide system and inhibition of the harmful RAAS. [2,4]
- **Indications:** According to the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines, it is a Class I (strong) recommendation for ambulatory patients with chronic symptomatic HFrEF (NYHA Class II-IV, LVEF ≤40%) to replace an ACE inhibitor or ARB, based on the PARADIGM-HF trial which showed a 20% reduction in cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization compared to enalapril. It is not approved for hypertension alone. [4]
- **Contraindications:** History of angioedema with any ACE inhibitor or ARB, concomitant use with an ACE inhibitor (must allow a 36-hour washout period to avoid doubling bradykinin accumulation and precipitating angioedema), concomitant use with aliskiren in patients with diabetes mellitus, pregnancy (teratogenic), and severe hepatic impairment. [1,2,4]
- **Dosage:** Starting dose is 24/26 mg or 49/51 mg orally twice daily (depending on prior ACEi/ARB dose and blood pressure), titrated to a target maintenance dose of 97/103 mg twice daily as tolerated. [2,4]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hypotension (more common than with enalapril), hyperkalemia, angioedema (0.2-0.5%), and renal impairment. BNP levels will be artificially elevated during therapy (neprilysin degrades BNP, so BNP becomes a less reliable marker of heart failure; NT-proBNP, which is not a neprilysin substrate, remains valid for monitoring). [2,4]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for HFrEF.

### 1.4 Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs)

- **Drug Names:** Spironolactone, Eplerenone. Finerenone is a newer selective non-steroidal MRA used specifically for CKD in diabetes; its role in heart failure is being investigated. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are competitive antagonists of the mineralocorticoid receptor, the intracellular receptor for aldosterone, located in the distal tubule and collecting duct of the kidney, as well as in the heart, vasculature, and brain. By blocking aldosterone binding, they reduce sodium reabsorption (via ENaC and Na-K-ATPase) and potassium and hydrogen ion secretion in the kidney, producing a mild diuresis. Crucially, in heart failure and post-MI, they also block the deleterious effects of aldosterone on the myocardium and vasculature: myocardial fibrosis, cardiac remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and baroreceptor dysfunction. The survival benefit in heart failure is independent of their diuretic effect. [1,2]
  - Spironolactone: Non-selective; also antagonizes androgen and progesterone receptors, accounting for its hormonal side effects.
  - Eplerenone: Selective for the mineralocorticoid receptor, with minimal androgenic or progestogenic activity. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - HFrEF (LVEF ≤35%, NYHA II-IV): Class I recommendation in the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline. Eplerenone or spironolactone reduces mortality and heart failure hospitalizations (RALES and EMPHASIS-HF trials). [4]
  - Post-MI with LVEF ≤40% and clinical heart failure or diabetes mellitus (EPHESUS trial). [5]
  - Resistant Hypertension: As a fourth-line agent. [3]
- **Contraindications:** Severe hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5 mmol/L), severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m²), concomitant use of potassium supplements or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors with eplerenone. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Spironolactone: 12.5-25 mg orally once daily, titrated up to 50 mg daily.
  - Eplerenone: 25-50 mg orally once daily. [1,2]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Hyperkalemia: The most serious and common adverse effect. Risk is increased in renal impairment, advanced age, and concomitant ACEi/ARB use. Potassium levels must be monitored regularly (e.g., at baseline, 3-7 days after initiation or dose change, and periodically thereafter). [1,2]
  - Spironolactone: Gynecomastia and breast tenderness (up to 10-30% of men, dose-dependent), impotence, menstrual irregularities. Mechanism is androgen receptor antagonism and enhanced peripheral conversion of testosterone to estradiol.
  - Eplerenone: These hormonal side effects are avoided due to receptor selectivity. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for chronic heart failure.

### 1.5 Direct Renin Inhibitor

- **Drug Name:** Aliskiren. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Directly inhibits renin, the enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting step of the RAAS cascade: the cleavage of angiotensinogen to angiotensin I. By blocking this initial step, it reduces the entire downstream cascade (angiotensin I, angiotensin II, and aldosterone). This avoids the reactive rise in plasma renin activity seen with ACE inhibitors and ARBs. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Hypertension. It is not a first-line agent and is used less frequently due to lack of outcome data and safety concerns. [3]
- **Contraindications:** Concomitant use with ACE inhibitors or ARBs in patients with diabetes mellitus or moderate-to-severe renal impairment (due to increased risk of hyperkalemia, hypotension, and renal failure, as shown in the ALTITUDE trial). Pregnancy (teratogenic). [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 150-300 mg orally once daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Diarrhea (dose-dependent), hyperkalemia, hypotension, angioedema. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic therapy for hypertension.

## Part II: Beta-Adrenergic Receptor Blockers

### 2.1 Cardioselective Beta-1 Blockers

- **Drug Names:** Metoprolol (succinate extended-release, tartrate immediate-release), Bisoprolol, Atenolol, Nebivolol, Esmolol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are competitive antagonists at the beta-1 adrenergic receptor, the predominant beta-receptor subtype in the heart. Beta-1 receptor blockade results in negative chronotropy (decreased heart rate via SA node inhibition), negative inotropy (decreased myocardial contractility), and negative dromotropy (slowed conduction through the AV node, prolonging the PR interval). These effects reduce myocardial oxygen demand, which is the basis for their anti-anginal efficacy. In heart failure, chronic beta-blockade protects the myocardium from the toxic effects of sustained sympathetic overactivation: it reverses pathological cardiac remodeling (reduces hypertrophy, fibrosis, and chamber dilation), upregulates down-regulated beta-receptors, reduces myocyte apoptosis, and suppresses arrhythmias. At therapeutic doses, beta-1 selective agents spare beta-2 receptors, minimizing bronchoconstriction and peripheral vasoconstriction. Nebivolol is unique among beta-blockers in also stimulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), causing NO-mediated vasodilation, which offsets the initial increase in peripheral vascular resistance. Esmolol is an ultra-short-acting IV beta-blocker (half-life ~9 minutes) used in acute settings. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF): Class I recommendation. Only metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol, and carvedilol have demonstrated mortality benefit in randomized controlled trials (MERIT-HF, CIBIS-II, COPERNICUS). [4]
  - Post-Acute Myocardial Infarction: Reduces mortality and recurrent MI. [5]
  - Stable Ischemic Heart Disease / Chronic Stable Angina: First-line anti-anginal therapy. [5]
  - Hypertension: Second- or third-line agent, especially in patients with concomitant CAD or HF. [3]
  - Rate Control in Atrial Fibrillation/Flutter: Class I for acute and chronic rate control. [4,6]
  - Supraventricular Tachycardias: To terminate or prevent AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) and AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT). [6]
  - Symptomatic Thyrotoxicosis: Blocks peripheral adrenergic manifestations. [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Severe bradycardia, high-grade AV block (without a pacemaker), acute decompensated heart failure with cardiogenic shock, severe reactive airway disease (relative contraindication, but cardioselective agents are preferred in COPD/asthma if beta-blockers are essential), and severe peripheral arterial disease with critical limb ischemia. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Metoprolol Succinate (extended-release): 12.5-200 mg orally once daily. Start 12.5-25 mg in HF.
  - Metoprolol Tartrate (immediate-release): 25-100 mg orally twice daily.
  - Bisoprolol: 2.5-10 mg orally once daily. Start 1.25 mg in HF.
  - Atenolol: 25-100 mg orally once daily.
  - Esmolol: IV bolus (500 mcg/kg over 1 minute), then 50-300 mcg/kg/min infusion.
  - Nebivolol: 5-40 mg orally once daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, fatigue, cold extremities (peripheral vasoconstriction from unopposed alpha-1 activity), nightmares/sleep disturbances (lipophilic agents like metoprolol and propranolol cross the blood-brain barrier more readily), erectile dysfunction, and exacerbation of bronchospasm (especially with non-selective agents). Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound tachycardia, hypertension, and acute coronary events due to beta-receptor upregulation during chronic blockade; therefore, doses must be tapered gradually over 1-2 weeks. Beta-blockers can mask the warning symptoms of hypoglycemia (palpitations, tremor, anxiety), though diaphoresis (sweating) is unaffected (mediated by muscarinic receptors). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for HFrEF and CAD, unless contraindications develop.

### 2.2 Non-Selective Beta Blockers

- **Drug Names:** Propranolol, Nadolol, Timolol, Pindolol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Block both beta-1 and beta-2 adrenergic receptors. In addition to the cardiac effects described above, beta-2 blockade causes bronchial smooth muscle constriction (contraindicated in asthmatics) and peripheral vasoconstriction. Propranolol is also a class I antiarrhythmic (sodium channel blocker) at high doses. Pindolol has intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA), acting as a partial agonist, which means it produces less resting bradycardia. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Hypertension, stable angina, essential tremor (propranolol), migraine prophylaxis (propranolol, timolol), thyrotoxicosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (symptomatic relief), portal hypertension (propranolol and nadolol for primary and secondary prevention of esophageal variceal hemorrhage). [1,2,5]
- **Contraindications:** Asthma and severe COPD (absolute for non-selective agents), severe bradycardia, high-grade AV block, acute decompensated heart failure. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Propranolol: 10-80 mg orally 2-4 times daily (immediate-release); 60-160 mg orally once daily (extended-release).
  - Nadolol: 20-160 mg orally once daily.
  - Timolol: 5-15 mg orally twice daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Same as cardioselective beta-blockers, plus a higher incidence of bronchospasm, worsening of peripheral vascular disease, and masking of hypoglycemic symptoms. [1,2]

### 2.3 Non-Selective Beta-Blocker with Alpha-1 Blockade

- **Drug Names:** Carvedilol, Labetalol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs block both beta-1 and beta-2 receptors, and additionally block alpha-1 adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle. Alpha-1 blockade causes peripheral vasodilation, reducing systemic vascular resistance and afterload. This combined beta- and alpha-blockade profile makes them particularly useful in heart failure (carvedilol) and hypertensive emergencies (labetalol IV), as the vasodilation offsets the initial negative inotropic effect and helps lower blood pressure more effectively. Carvedilol also has antioxidant and anti-proliferative properties. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Carvedilol: HFrEF (Class I, proven mortality benefit), post-MI with LV dysfunction, hypertension. [4,5]
  - Labetalol: Hypertensive emergencies (IV), chronic hypertension (oral), and hypertension in pregnancy (pre-eclampsia/eclampsia). [3,6]
- **Contraindications:** Asthma (relative – may still be used with caution in HF), severe bradycardia, high-grade AV block, acute decompensated heart failure, severe hepatic impairment. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Carvedilol: 3.125-25 mg orally twice daily (immediate-release); 10-80 mg orally once daily (extended-release). Start 3.125 mg twice daily for HF.
  - Labetalol: 100-400 mg orally twice daily; 20-80 mg IV bolus every 10 minutes (up to 300 mg total) for hypertensive emergency. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Same as other beta-blockers plus orthostatic hypotension (more pronounced due to alpha-blockade), dizziness, and headache. Carvedilol is less likely to cause cold extremities due to vasodilation. [1,2]

## Part III: Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

### 3.1 Dihydropyridines (DHPs)

- **Drug Names:** Amlodipine, Nifedipine (extended-release), Felodipine, Nicardipine, Isradipine, Clevidipine, Nisoldipine. The "-dipine" suffix identifies this class. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs bind to and block L-type voltage-gated calcium channels (Cav1.2) in the plasma membrane of vascular smooth muscle cells. By preventing the influx of extracellular calcium during depolarization, they inhibit calcium-dependent activation of myosin light-chain kinase, leading to smooth muscle relaxation, arteriolar vasodilation, and a significant reduction in systemic vascular resistance. Dihydropyridines are highly vascular-selective; at therapeutic doses, they have minimal direct effects on cardiac myocytes or the SA/AV nodes, and therefore do not reduce contractility or heart rate significantly. The reflex tachycardia from vasodilation is usually blunted by the long half-life of second-generation agents like amlodipine. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Hypertension: Amlodipine is one of the most commonly prescribed antihypertensives worldwide. First-line agent. [3]
  - Stable Angina: Reduces afterload and dilates coronary arteries. [5]
  - Prinzmetal's Variant Angina: The drug of choice; prevents coronary vasospasm. [5]
  - Hypertensive Emergencies: Clevidipine (ultra-short-acting IV) and nicardipine IV. [6]
- **Contraindications:** Severe hypotension, cardiogenic shock. Short-acting nifedipine (immediate-release capsules) is contraindicated in acute coronary syndromes and hypertension due to the risk of reflex sympathetic activation, tachycardia, and increased mortality; only extended-release formulations are used chronically. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Amlodipine: 2.5-10 mg orally once daily.
  - Nifedipine Extended-Release: 30-90 mg orally once daily.
  - Felodipine: 2.5-10 mg orally once daily.
  - Clevidipine: IV infusion starting at 1-2 mg/hour, titrated to BP. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Peripheral edema (up to 10-30% with amlodipine). Mechanism: DHP-induced precapillary arteriolar dilation increases capillary hydrostatic pressure without a proportional increase in venous capacitance, promoting fluid filtration into the interstitium. This edema is typically not responsive to diuretics. Headache, flushing, and dizziness are related to vasodilation. Gingival hyperplasia can occur with chronic use (especially nifedipine). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for hypertension.

### 3.2 Non-Dihydropyridines (Non-DHPs)

- **Drug Names:** Verapamil (phenylalkylamine class), Diltiazem (benzothiazepine class). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Unlike DHPs, these drugs block L-type calcium channels in both vascular smooth muscle AND cardiac myocytes, including the SA and AV nodes. The cardiac effects include negative chronotropy (decreased sinus rate), negative dromotropy (slowed AV nodal conduction, prolonged PR interval), and negative inotropy (decreased contractility). The vascular effects cause modest arteriolar vasodilation. Verapamil has the most potent cardiac depressant effects; diltiazem is intermediate between verapamil and DHPs, sometimes called a "non-DHP with some vasodilatory properties." Both suppress calcium-dependent automaticity in the AV node, making them effective for controlling ventricular rate in atrial tachyarrhythmias and for terminating AV nodal re-entry. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Rate Control in Atrial Fibrillation/Flutter: Class I recommendation. [4,6]
  - Termination of AV Nodal Re-entrant Tachycardia (AVNRT): IV verapamil or diltiazem.
  - Hypertension: Particularly in patients who also require rate control.
  - Stable Angina: Reduces myocardial oxygen demand (negative chronotropy and inotropy) and dilates coronary arteries. [5]
- **Contraindications:** Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (negative inotropy can worsen HF), high-grade AV block (without a pacemaker), sick sinus syndrome, severe bradycardia, Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome with atrial fibrillation (can paradoxically accelerate ventricular rate by blocking the AV node and promoting antegrade conduction down the accessory pathway, leading to ventricular fibrillation), and concomitant IV beta-blocker administration (risk of asystole or complete heart block). [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Verapamil Immediate-Release: 40-120 mg orally 3 times daily; Extended-Release: 180-480 mg orally once daily. IV: 2.5-5 mg bolus over 2 minutes, may repeat 5-10 mg.
  - Diltiazem Immediate-Release: 30-90 mg orally 4 times daily; Extended-Release: 180-360 mg orally once daily. IV: 0.25 mg/kg bolus over 2 minutes, then 5-15 mg/hour infusion. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Bradycardia, AV block (especially in combination with beta-blockers or digoxin), constipation (verapamil is the most constipating drug in common use, affecting up to 30% of patients), hypotension, worsening of heart failure, and gingival hyperplasia (less common than DHPs). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic for rate control or hypertension.

## Part IV: Diuretics

### 4.1 Loop Diuretics

- **Drug Names:** Furosemide, Bumetanide, Torsemide, Ethacrynic acid. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These are the most potent class of diuretics. They act on the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, where they competitively inhibit the luminal Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2). This blocks the reabsorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride, abolishing the countercurrent multiplier mechanism. The medullary interstitium loses its hypertonicity, impairing both urine concentration and dilution. The result is a massive excretion of isotonic urine, with fractional sodium excretion reaching 20-25%. Unlike thiazides, loop diuretics remain effective even when the glomerular filtration rate is severely reduced (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m²). Furosemide also has an acute, non-diuretic venodilator effect (preload reduction) when given intravenously, likely mediated by prostaglandins, which provides rapid relief in acute pulmonary edema before the diuresis begins. Ethacrynic acid is a non-sulfonamide loop diuretic, safe for use in patients with sulfa allergy. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Acute Decompensated Heart Failure: Volume overload, pulmonary edema. IV furosemide is the mainstay. [4,6]
  - Chronic Heart Failure with Volume Overload: Oral furosemide or torsemide for symptomatic congestion. [4]
  - Edema from Nephrotic Syndrome, Cirrhosis, and Renal Failure.
  - Hypertension: Reserved for patients with CKD or resistant hypertension, often in combination with other agents. [3]
  - Hypercalcemia: Promotes urinary calcium excretion (in combination with aggressive IV normal saline). [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Anuria (no filtrate to act upon), severe hypokalemia or hyponatremia, and hepatic encephalopathy (hypokalemia-induced ammonia production and hypovolemia can worsen encephalopathy). [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Furosemide: Oral: 20-80 mg once or twice daily (max 600 mg/day in severe renal failure). IV: 20-40 mg bolus initially for pulmonary edema, doubled if no response; continuous infusion (5-20 mg/hour) may be more effective in diuretic resistance. Oral bioavailability is ~50% (range 10-100%), meaning IV is approximately twice as potent as oral. [1,2]
  - Bumetanide: Oral: 0.5-4 mg once or twice daily. IV: 0.5-1 mg. Oral bioavailability is ~80-100%.
  - Torsemide: Oral: 10-100 mg once daily. IV: 10-20 mg. Oral bioavailability is ~80-100% and more predictable than furosemide. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis (increased sodium delivery to the distal tubule stimulates K+ and H+ secretion), hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia (volume depletion increases proximal urate reabsorption, and diuretics compete with uric acid for the organic acid secretory transporter), ototoxicity (high plasma concentrations, typically from rapid IV bolus in renal failure, cause reversible sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus; potentiated by aminoglycosides, mechanism is inhibition of the NKCC1 transporter in the stria vascularis), hypocalcemia (loop diuretics inhibit calcium reabsorption in the thick ascending limb), and pre-renal azotemia from excessive volume depletion. Sulfonamide allergy is a relative contraindication; ethacrynic acid is an alternative. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Acute therapy for decompensation; chronic therapy for symptom control in advanced heart failure.

### 4.2 Thiazide and Thiazide-Like Diuretics

- **Drug Names:** Chlorthalidone, Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), Indapamide, Metolazone. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs inhibit the Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) on the luminal membrane of the early distal convoluted tubule. They are less potent than loop diuretics (fractional sodium excretion 5-10%) and lose efficacy when eGFR falls below 30-40 mL/min/1.73m² (except metolazone, which retains some efficacy). Thiazides are paradoxically the preferred diuretic class for hypertension because they have a sustained vasodilatory effect, reducing peripheral vascular resistance over weeks to months. The mechanism of this vasodilation is not fully understood but may involve calcium-activated potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle. Chlorthalidone is more potent and has a longer half-life (24-72 hours) than HCTZ (6-12 hours) and is preferred for hypertension based on superior outcome data (ALLHAT trial). Metolazone is unique: it is a thiazide-like diuretic that retains efficacy in renal failure and acts synergistically with loop diuretics to overcome diuretic resistance by sequential nephron blockade (blocking sodium reabsorption at both the loop of Henle and the distal tubule). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Essential Hypertension: First-line agent (chlorthalidone preferred). [3]
  - Mild Edema from Heart Failure: Often combined with a loop diuretic.
  - Nephrolithiasis due to Idiopathic Hypercalciuria: Thiazides increase distal tubular calcium reabsorption, reducing urinary calcium excretion. [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Severe renal impairment (eGFR <30), anuria, severe hyponatremia, and sulfonamide allergy (caution). [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Chlorthalidone: 12.5-25 mg orally once daily (max 50 mg). Start low to minimize hypokalemia.
  - Hydrochlorothiazide: 12.5-50 mg orally once daily.
  - Metolazone: 2.5-10 mg orally once daily (often given 30-60 minutes before the loop diuretic in diuretic resistance). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hyponatremia (more common and severe than with loop diuretics, especially in elderly women; mechanism involves increased water reabsorption in the collecting duct due to volume depletion and ADH, combined with a reduced ability to dilute urine since the diluting segment is blocked), hypercalcemia (thiazides increase calcium reabsorption in the distal tubule, the opposite of loop diuretics), hyperglycemia (impaired insulin secretion from hypokalemia), hyperlipidemia (small, transient increases in LDL and triglycerides), and hyperuricemia. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic for hypertension.

### 4.3 Potassium-Sparing Diuretics (ENaC Blockers)

- **Drug Names:** Amiloride, Triamterene. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs directly block the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) on the luminal membrane of principal cells in the collecting duct, independently of aldosterone. By reducing sodium entry, they decrease the lumen-negative transepithelial potential, which is the driving force for potassium and hydrogen ion secretion. This results in a mild natriuresis and diuresis, with potassium and hydrogen ion retention. They are weak diuretics when used alone but are effective potassium-sparing agents. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Combination therapy with thiazide or loop diuretics to prevent or treat hypokalemia.
  - Liddle Syndrome: A genetic disorder with constitutively active ENaC channels. Amiloride or triamterene is the specific treatment. [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Severe hyperkalemia, severe renal impairment, concomitant potassium supplementation or MRAs. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Amiloride: 5-10 mg orally once daily.
  - Triamterene: 50-100 mg orally twice daily. Often available in fixed-dose combinations with HCTZ. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hyperkalemia is the primary risk. Triamterene can cause renal calculi (triamterene stones) and is associated with reversible acute kidney injury (possibly from crystal nephropathy). Amiloride has fewer side effects and is generally preferred. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic.

### 4.4 Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors

- **Drug Name:** Acetazolamide. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Inhibits carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, reducing bicarbonate reabsorption and causing a bicarbonate diuresis. This produces a mild, self-limited diuresis that leads to a metabolic acidosis. Its cardiovascular role is limited but specific. [1,2]
- **Indication in Cardiology:** Acetazolamide has been investigated as an add-on therapy to loop diuretics for decongestion in acute decompensated heart failure. The ADVOR trial (2022) demonstrated that IV acetazolamide (500 mg daily) added to standard IV loop diuretic therapy resulted in a higher rate of successful decongestion, primarily by overcoming diuretic resistance through a synergistic mechanism (proximal and distal tubular blockade), and by counteracting the metabolic alkalosis that can blunt loop diuretic efficacy. [4,7]
- **Dosage:** 250-500 mg IV once daily for 3 days, or 250-500 mg orally once or twice daily. [2,7]
- **Important Side Effects:** Metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, paresthesias, and sulfonamide allergy cross-reactivity. [1,2]

### 4.5 Osmotic Diuretics

- **Drug Name:** Mannitol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Mannitol is an osmotically active, non-reabsorbable sugar alcohol filtered by the glomerulus. It increases the osmotic pressure of the tubular fluid, drawing water into the tubule and preventing water reabsorption throughout the nephron, particularly in the proximal tubule and the descending limb of Henle. It expands extracellular fluid volume and reduces blood viscosity. [1,2]
- **Indication in Cardiology:** Not used for chronic heart failure or hypertension. It is used in the setting of cardiopulmonary bypass to reduce the incidence of acute kidney injury (osmotic diuresis and free radical scavenging), and to transiently reduce intracranial pressure in the context of cerebral edema (e.g., after cardiac arrest). [1,2,6]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hyponatremia (transcellular water shift), volume overload and pulmonary edema (initial ECF expansion before diuresis), and headache. [1,2]

## Part V: Vasodilators

### 5.1 Nitrates

- **Drug Names:** Nitroglycerin (glyceryl trinitrate), Isosorbide Dinitrate (ISDN), Isosorbide Mononitrate (ISMN). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are prodrugs that undergo denitration by mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH-2) in vascular smooth muscle cells to release free nitric oxide (NO). NO activates cytosolic guanylyl cyclase, which catalyzes the conversion of GTP to cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP). cGMP activates cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG), which phosphorylates multiple target proteins that ultimately decrease intracellular calcium and dephosphorylate myosin light chains, leading to smooth muscle relaxation. Nitrates are venoselective at low doses (pooling blood in systemic veins, reducing venous return and preload), and cause arteriolar dilation at higher doses (reducing afterload and systemic blood pressure). They also dilate large epicardial coronary arteries, including eccentric stenoses. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Acute Angina Attack: Sublingual nitroglycerin (0.4 mg every 5 minutes, up to 3 doses). First-line for immediate relief. [5]
  - Prophylaxis of Stable Angina: Long-acting oral nitrates (ISMN) or transdermal patches used before planned exertion. [5]
  - Acute Pulmonary Edema / Decompensated Heart Failure: IV nitroglycerin is a powerful preload reducer and is the vasodilator of choice, especially in hypertensive acute heart failure. [4,6]
- **Contraindications:** Severe aortic stenosis (reduced preload can precipitate cardiovascular collapse), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg or >30 mmHg below baseline), right ventricular infarction (preload-dependent), and concomitant use of phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) due to risk of profound, potentially fatal hypotension from synergistic cGMP accumulation. [1,2,4]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Nitroglycerin Sublingual: 0.4 mg every 5 minutes, max 3 doses in 15 minutes.
  - Nitroglycerin IV: Start 5-10 mcg/min, titrate by 5-20 mcg/min every 3-5 minutes to effect.
  - Isosorbide Mononitrate: 30-120 mg orally once daily (eccentric dosing with a nitrate-free interval).
  - Nitroglycerin Patch: 0.1-0.8 mg/hour applied to skin for 12-14 hours daily (remove for 10-12 hours at night to prevent tolerance). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Severe throbbing headache (cerebral vasodilation, tolerance develops with continued use), hypotension, reflex tachycardia (can be prevented by co-administration with beta-blockers), flushing, and methemoglobinemia (rare, with high doses). Tolerance to the hemodynamic and anti-anginal effects develops rapidly with continuous, sustained administration. The mechanism involves depletion of sulfhydryl groups, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Tolerance is prevented by ensuring a daily nitrate-free interval of 10-12 hours (typically overnight). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Long-term for angina prophylaxis, but with mandatory daily drug-free interval. IV nitrates are used acutely for 24-48 hours in decompensated heart failure.

### 5.2 Hydralazine

- **Mechanism of Action:** A direct arteriolar vasodilator. Its exact mechanism is unclear, but it involves the opening of potassium channels and inhibition of IP3-mediated calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum in vascular smooth muscle cells. It reduces systemic vascular resistance and afterload, with no effect on venous capacitance. It stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and the RAAS (baroreflex-mediated), causing reflex tachycardia and sodium retention, which limits its efficacy as monotherapy. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Hypertension (typically as a third-line agent in combination with a beta-blocker and a diuretic, especially in pregnancy – it is the first-line IV agent for severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia in some guidelines).
  - Heart Failure in African Americans: The fixed-dose combination of hydralazine and isosorbide dinitrate (BiDil) demonstrated a significant mortality benefit in self-identified African American patients with HFrEF in the A-HeFT trial. It is a Class I recommendation in this population. [4]
- **Contraindications:** Coronary artery disease without concurrent beta-blockade (reflex tachycardia increases MVO2 and can provoke angina or MI), dissecting aortic aneurysm, and severe tachycardia. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Hydralazine: 10-50 mg orally four times daily (max 300 mg/day). Often given in combination with a beta-blocker and a diuretic.
  - Hydralazine/Isosorbide Dinitrate (BiDil): 37.5/20 mg three times daily, titrated to 75/40 mg three times daily.
  - IV Hydralazine: 5-20 mg bolus every 4-6 hours for hypertensive emergencies in pregnancy. [1,2,4]
- **Important Side Effects:** Reflex tachycardia, sodium and water retention, headache, nausea, and a dose-dependent drug-induced lupus syndrome. The lupus syndrome occurs in 5-10% of patients on chronic doses >200 mg/day, more commonly in slow acetylators (genetic polymorphism of N-acetyltransferase). It presents with arthralgia, myalgia, fever, and a positive antinuclear antibody (ANA). Antihistone antibodies are typical. It resolves upon discontinuation of the drug. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic for hypertension and heart failure (in selected populations).

### 5.3 Minoxidil

- **Mechanism of Action:** A prodrug that is metabolized by hepatic sulfotransferase to its active metabolite, minoxidil sulfate. It opens ATP-sensitive potassium channels (KATP) in vascular smooth muscle, causing hyperpolarization, closure of voltage-gated calcium channels, and profound arteriolar vasodilation. It is the most potent oral arteriolar vasodilator available. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Resistant hypertension or hypertension refractory to maximum doses of standard three-drug regimens (typically including a diuretic, RAAS inhibitor, and beta-blocker). It is a last-resort oral agent. [3]
- **Contraindications:** Pheochromocytoma (can provoke catecholamine release), and without concomitant beta-blocker and loop diuretic (reflex tachycardia and fluid retention are severe). [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 2.5-40 mg orally once or twice daily (max 100 mg/day). Must always be used with a beta-blocker and a loop diuretic. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Profound reflex tachycardia (obligatory concomitant beta-blocker), severe sodium and water retention (obligatory concomitant loop diuretic), pericardial effusion (rare but serious), and hypertrichosis (increased hair growth, which is the basis for its topical use in alopecia). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic, for truly resistant hypertension.

### 5.4 Sodium Nitroprusside

- **Mechanism of Action:** A direct, balanced arteriolar and venous vasodilator. Nitroprusside interacts with oxyhemoglobin, releasing nitric oxide (NO) and cyanide ions. NO activates guanylyl cyclase in both arteriolar and venous smooth muscle, causing vasodilation. It reduces both preload and afterload. Its onset of action is immediate (within seconds), and its duration is very short (1-2 minutes) due to rapid metabolism, making it ideal for continuous IV titration in critical care settings. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Hypertensive emergencies (e.g., acute aortic dissection, acute pulmonary edema with severe hypertension, hypertensive encephalopathy), and acute decompensated heart failure with severe afterload elevation refractory to nitroglycerin. [4,6]
- **Contraindications:** Should not be used for >24-48 hours, in patients with hepatic or renal failure (risk of cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity), and in conditions with high mortality regardless of therapy. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** IV infusion starting at 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min, titrated by 0.5 mcg/kg/min every 3-5 minutes to target blood pressure. Maximum rate is 8-10 mcg/kg/min (toxic cyanide accumulation above 2 mcg/kg/min if prolonged). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hypotension (potent, requires continuous monitoring, often with an arterial line). Cyanide toxicity (metabolic acidosis, increased mixed venous oxygen saturation, altered mental status, seizures, coma) is a life-threatening complication of high doses or prolonged infusion, particularly in renal or hepatic impairment. Thiocyanate toxicity (accumulates in renal failure) causes psychosis, hyperreflexia, and seizures. The infusion must be protected from light to prevent degradation. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Maximum 24-48 hours; it is a bridge to oral therapy.

### 5.5 Nesiritide

- **Mechanism of Action:** A recombinant form of human B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP). It binds to natriuretic peptide receptors (NPR-A) on vascular smooth muscle and in the kidney, increasing intracellular cGMP. This causes balanced venous and arterial vasodilation (reducing preload and afterload), promotes natriuresis and diuresis, and inhibits the RAAS and sympathetic nervous system. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Acute decompensated heart failure with dyspnea at rest. Its use has declined significantly after the ASCEND-HF trial showed no mortality benefit and an increased risk of hypotension and renal dysfunction. It is rarely used in contemporary practice, largely superseded by IV nitroglycerin and loop diuretics. [2,4]
- **Dosage:** IV bolus of 2 mcg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion of 0.01 mcg/kg/min. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Severe hypotension (dose-related), worsening renal function. [1,2]

## Part VI: Antiplatelet Agents

### 6.1 Cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) Inhibitor

- **Drug Name:** Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Aspirin irreversibly acetylates serine-529 of the cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) enzyme in platelets. Because platelets are anucleate and cannot synthesize new proteins, this inhibition permanently blocks the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin H2, the precursor of thromboxane A2 (TXA2). TXA2 is a potent platelet aggregator and vasoconstrictor. The antiplatelet effect lasts for the entire lifespan of the platelet (7-10 days). At low doses (75-162 mg/day), aspirin selectively inhibits platelet TXA2 production while largely sparing endothelial prostacyclin (PGI2) production, which is mediated by COX-2 and requires new enzyme synthesis. At higher doses, endothelial PGI2 synthesis is also inhibited, potentially reducing the antiplatelet benefit. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Acute Coronary Syndrome (STEMI, NSTEMI, Unstable Angina): Immediate loading dose of 162-325 mg chewed, followed by 75-162 mg daily indefinitely. [5]
  - Stable Ischemic Heart Disease: 75-162 mg daily for secondary prevention. [5]
  - Post-CABG and Post-PCI: To maintain graft patency and prevent stent thrombosis. [5]
  - Primary Prevention of ASCVD: 75-100 mg daily may be considered in select adults aged 40-70 years at higher ASCVD risk without increased bleeding risk. [5]
  - Acute Ischemic Stroke: 162-325 mg within 24-48 hours. [1]
  - Rheumatic Fever and Pericarditis: High-dose aspirin (650-1000 mg 3-4 times daily) for anti-inflammatory effect. [4]
- **Contraindications:** Active peptic ulcer disease, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe hepatic disease, history of aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD, Samter's Triad), and children with viral illness (risk of Reye's syndrome). [1,2]
- **Dosage:** Loading dose: 162-325 mg (non-enteric coated, chewed). Maintenance dose: 75-162 mg orally once daily. [5]
- **Important Side Effects:** Gastrointestinal mucosal injury (erosive gastritis, peptic ulcer disease), gastrointestinal bleeding, tinnitus (salicylism, dose-related), hypersensitivity reactions (bronchospasm, rhinitis, rarely anaphylaxis), and hemorrhagic stroke (at very low absolute rates). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for secondary prevention, with constant re-evaluation of risk-benefit ratio.

### 6.2 P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitors

- **Drug Names:** Clopidogrel, Prasugrel, Ticagrelor. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs block the P2Y12 ADP receptor on the platelet surface. ADP released from activated platelets and damaged endothelium binds to P2Y12, which signals through Gi protein to inhibit adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cAMP. This amplifies platelet activation, induces the conformational change of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor (allowing fibrinogen binding and platelet cross-linking), and promotes granule release. By blocking P2Y12, these agents inhibit this amplification pathway, reducing platelet aggregation.
  - Clopidogrel and Prasugrel are thienopyridine prodrugs that require hepatic CYP450 metabolism to generate their active metabolites, which irreversibly inhibit the P2Y12 receptor. Clopidogrel requires two-step oxidation (primarily by CYP2C19); genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C19 result in reduced active metabolite formation and higher rates of stent thrombosis in poor metabolizers. Prasugrel has a more efficient, one-step activation and is less affected by CYP polymorphisms, making it more potent and predictable, but at a cost of increased bleeding risk.
  - Ticagrelor is a cyclopentyltriazolopyrimidine that is not a prodrug; it is a direct-acting, reversible, non-competitive antagonist of the P2Y12 receptor. It does not require metabolic activation, has a faster onset and offset than clopidogrel, and is a more potent platelet inhibitor. Ticagrelor also inhibits the equilibrative nucleoside transporter-1 (ENT1), which increases extracellular adenosine levels, contributing to its additional effects (improved coronary blood flow, but also dyspnea). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Acute Coronary Syndrome (NSTEMI/UA or STEMI): Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor preferred over clopidogrel in guidelines, or prasugrel in those undergoing PCI without contraindications) for at least 12 months. [5]
  - Post-PCI with Stent Implantation: DAPT for a minimum duration depending on stent type and clinical presentation (1-3 months for stable CAD with newer-generation DES, 6-12 months for ACS). [5]
  - Secondary Prevention of Stroke (Clopidogrel) or in selected patients with minor stroke/TIA (DAPT with aspirin and ticagrelor or clopidogrel for 21-30 days). [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Active pathological bleeding (e.g., peptic ulcer, intracranial hemorrhage), severe hepatic impairment. Prasugrel is contraindicated in patients with a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) due to increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage (TRITON-TIMI 38 trial). [1,2,5]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Clopidogrel: Loading dose 300-600 mg orally, then 75 mg orally once daily.
  - Prasugrel: Loading dose 60 mg orally, then 10 mg orally once daily (5 mg once daily for patients <60 kg; consider 5 mg in patients ≥75 years).
  - Ticagrelor: Loading dose 180 mg orally, then 90 mg orally twice daily. [1,2,5]
- **Important Side Effects:** Bleeding (the primary and most serious adverse effect, including major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and fatal bleeding). Clopidogrel: generally well-tolerated; risk of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is very rare. Prasugrel: higher bleeding risk than clopidogrel, increased risk of colonic neoplasms (unclear significance). Ticagrelor: higher rate of non-CABG major bleeding compared to clopidogrel in some studies, but not fatal bleeding; dyspnea (14-21% of patients, typically mild, dose-related, rarely leads to discontinuation; mechanism is increased adenosine levels); ventricular pauses in the first week (usually asymptomatic). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** 12 months for ACS (standard duration), with consideration for shorter duration (3-6 months) in high bleeding risk patients, or extended DAPT beyond 12 months with ticagrelor 60 mg twice daily in high ischemic risk, low bleeding risk patients (PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial). [5]

### 6.3 Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa Inhibitors

- **Drug Names:** Abciximab, Eptifibatide, Tirofiban. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation. They are potent IV inhibitors of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor (integrin αIIbβ3) on the platelet surface. Fibrinogen and von Willebrand factor bind to this receptor to cross-link adjacent platelets. By blocking this receptor, these drugs prevent platelet aggregation regardless of the agonist that activated the platelet. Abciximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody fragment that binds irreversibly to the receptor. Eptifibatide and tirofiban are small-molecule competitive inhibitors with shorter half-lives. [1,2]
- **Indications:** High-risk PCI in ACS, particularly in patients with a large thrombus burden, no reflow, or those who have not been pre-loaded with an oral P2Y12 inhibitor. Their use has declined in the era of potent oral P2Y12 inhibitors and routine radial artery access. [5]
- **Contraindications:** Active bleeding, history of hemorrhagic stroke, recent major surgery or trauma, severe hypertension, thrombocytopenia. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Eptifibatide: IV bolus 180 mcg/kg (double bolus 10 min apart), then 2 mcg/kg/min infusion for 12-24 hours.
  - Tirofiban: IV bolus 25 mcg/kg over 3 minutes, then 0.15 mcg/kg/min infusion. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Major bleeding, thrombocytopenia (especially with abciximab, which can cause profound thrombocytopenia within hours of administration; the mechanism is immune-mediated). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Limited to the periprocedural period (hours to days).

### 6.4 PAR-1 Antagonist

- **Drug Name:** Vorapaxar. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** A competitive antagonist of the protease-activated receptor-1 (PAR-1) on platelets. Thrombin potently activates platelets by cleaving PAR-1. By blocking PAR-1, vorapaxar inhibits thrombin-mediated platelet activation without affecting thrombin's role in fibrin generation (coagulation). This provides an additional, complementary mechanism of platelet inhibition to aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Reduction of atherothrombotic events in patients with a history of MI or peripheral arterial disease (PAD) without a history of stroke, TIA, or intracranial hemorrhage. It is used as an adjunct to aspirin and/or clopidogrel in selected high-risk patients. Its use is limited by significant bleeding risk. [5]
- **Contraindications:** History of stroke, TIA, or intracranial hemorrhage (due to a marked increase in intracranial hemorrhage in these patients in the TRA 2P-TIMI 50 trial), active bleeding, severe hepatic impairment. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 2.08 mg orally once daily (note: this is the dose for the sulfate salt, equivalent to 2.5 mg vorapaxar). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Major bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage. Anemia. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Long-term for secondary prevention in a very select population.

### 6.5 Phosphodiesterase-3 Inhibitor (Antiplatelet)

- **Drug Name:** Cilostazol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** A selective inhibitor of phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE3) in platelets and vascular smooth muscle. By inhibiting PDE3, it increases intracellular cAMP, which inhibits platelet aggregation and causes peripheral arterial vasodilation. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Symptomatic Intermittent Claudication: Improves walking distance in patients with PAD. [5]
  - Secondary Prevention of Stroke (in some Asian populations, it has shown benefit for stroke prevention). [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Heart failure of any severity (PDE3 inhibitors increase mortality in HF, as demonstrated with milrinone in the PROMISE trial). [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 100 mg orally twice daily (50 mg twice daily in patients taking strong CYP3A4 or CYP2C19 inhibitors). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Headache, diarrhea, palpitations, tachycardia, and dizziness. [1,2]

## Part VII: Anticoagulants

### 7.1 Vitamin K Antagonist

- **Drug Name:** Warfarin. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Warfarin is a competitive inhibitor of vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1 (VKORC1) in the liver. This enzyme is responsible for recycling oxidized vitamin K back to its reduced, active form (vitamin K hydroquinone). Reduced vitamin K is an essential cofactor for the enzyme gamma-glutamyl carboxylase, which catalyzes the post-translational gamma-carboxylation of glutamic acid residues on the N-terminal domains of clotting factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX, and X, as well as the anticoagulant proteins C and S. Gamma-carboxylation is required for these proteins to bind calcium and phospholipid surfaces, enabling their biological activity. Warfarin's inhibition of VKORC1 depletes the pool of reduced vitamin K, leading to the synthesis of biologically inactive, decarboxylated clotting factors (PIVKA: proteins induced by vitamin K absence). The anticoagulant effect is delayed until the pre-existing, fully carboxylated factors are cleared from the circulation (prothrombin has a half-life of ~60-72 hours, so the full antithrombotic effect takes 5-7 days). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Stroke Prevention in Atrial Fibrillation: Particularly in valvular AF (mechanical heart valves, moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis). DOACs are now preferred for non-valvular AF. [4,6]
  - Treatment and Secondary Prevention of Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE): DOACs are now preferred, but warfarin remains an option.
  - Mechanical Heart Valves: Warfarin is the only approved oral anticoagulant for mechanical valves. Target INR depends on valve type and position (aortic bileaflet mechanical valve: INR 2.0-3.0; mitral mechanical valve: INR 2.5-3.5). [4]
  - Left Ventricular Thrombus: Typically treated for 3-6 months. [4,5]
- **Contraindications:** Pregnancy (teratogenic; causes warfarin embryopathy, including nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses, and CNS abnormalities; LMWH is used during pregnancy), active bleeding, severe hepatic disease, recent or planned surgery (risk of hemorrhage), and known hypersensitivity. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** Initial dose is typically 5 mg orally once daily, adjusted based on INR monitoring. Lower starting doses (2-2.5 mg) are used in the elderly, malnourished, hepatic impairment, or those on interacting medications. The dose is titrated to a target INR, usually 2.0-3.0 for most indications (range 2.5-3.5 for mechanical mitral valves). [1,2]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Hemorrhage: The most common and serious complication, including major bleeding (intracranial, gastrointestinal, retroperitoneal) and fatal bleeding. Risk is highest during the first few months of therapy, at higher INR values, and in older adults.
  - Warfarin-Induced Skin Necrosis: A rare but devastating complication, occurring 3-10 days after initiation, typically in patients with congenital protein C or protein S deficiency. Mechanism: Warfarin inhibits the synthesis of the anticoagulant proteins C and S (which have shorter half-lives, ~8-10 hours for protein C) before it depletes the procoagulant factors (II, IX, X). This creates a transient, paradoxical hypercoagulable state, causing thrombosis of cutaneous microvasculature and necrosis of fatty tissues (breasts, thighs, buttocks). Treatment: Discontinue warfarin, administer vitamin K and protein C concentrate or fresh frozen plasma, and initiate heparin.
  - Purple Toe Syndrome: A painful, bluish discoloration of the toes due to cholesterol microembolization from atheromatous plaques, occurring 3-8 weeks after starting warfarin.
  - Teratogenicity: Absolute contraindication in pregnancy.
  - Numerous Drug and Food Interactions: Warfarin metabolism (primarily via CYP2C9) is affected by many drugs (e.g., amiodarone, fluconazole, metronidazole, sulfamethoxazole, NSAIDs). Vitamin K intake in the diet (green leafy vegetables) must be consistent. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** 3-6 months for provoked DVT/PE; indefinite for recurrent or unprovoked VTE, or for mechanical heart valves and persistent AF.
- **Monitoring:** INR is monitored regularly (initially daily until stable, then every 1-4 weeks). Time in therapeutic range (TTR) should be >65-70% for optimal benefit. [1,2]

### 7.2 Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) – Factor Xa Inhibitors

- **Drug Names:** Apixaban, Rivaroxaban, Edoxaban. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are direct, reversible, competitive inhibitors of activated Factor X (Factor Xa). Factor Xa sits at the convergence of the intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways, where it is the rate-limiting enzyme for thrombin generation. One molecule of Factor Xa generates approximately 1000 molecules of thrombin. By directly inhibiting Factor Xa, these drugs potently reduce thrombin generation without requiring antithrombin as a cofactor. They inhibit both free Factor Xa in solution and Factor Xa bound within the prothrombinase complex. They do not require metabolic activation (are not prodrugs) and have predictable pharmacokinetics, allowing fixed dosing without routine coagulation monitoring. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Stroke Prevention in Non-Valvular Atrial Fibrillation: Class I recommendation in all major guidelines. All three agents are at least as effective as warfarin and significantly reduce intracranial hemorrhage. [4,6]
  - Treatment of Acute DVT and PE: Rivaroxaban and apixaban can be used as monotherapy from diagnosis; edoxaban requires an initial 5-10 day course of parenteral anticoagulation (LMWH). [6]
  - Long-Term Secondary Prevention of Recurrent VTE.
  - Post-Operative Prophylaxis after Hip or Knee Arthroplasty (rivaroxaban, apixaban). [1,2]
- **Contraindications:** Active pathological bleeding, severe hepatic impairment with coagulopathy, mechanical heart valves (RE-ALIGN trial showed increased thromboembolic and bleeding events with dabigatran compared to warfarin in mechanical valves; this class effect extends to Factor Xa inhibitors), and moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis (often grouped with valvular AF and excluded from DOAC trials). Renal impairment: Dose adjustment or contraindication varies by drug and renal function. [1,2,4]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Apixaban: AF: 5 mg orally twice daily (2.5 mg twice daily if ≥2 of: age ≥80, weight ≤60 kg, creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL). VTE treatment: 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily.
  - Rivaroxaban: AF: 20 mg orally once daily with the evening meal (15 mg daily if CrCl 15-50 mL/min). VTE treatment: 15 mg twice daily with food for 21 days, then 20 mg once daily.
  - Edoxaban: AF: 60 mg orally once daily (30 mg if CrCl 15-50 mL/min, weight ≤60 kg, or on strong P-gp inhibitors). VTE treatment: 60 mg once daily (same dose reductions) after initial 5-10 days of LMWH. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hemorrhage, including major bleeding (the risk of intracranial hemorrhage is 50-60% lower than with warfarin across all DOACs). Gastrointestinal bleeding is higher with rivaroxaban and edoxaban compared to warfarin; apixaban has the most favorable GI bleeding profile. Specific reversal agents: Andexanet alfa (recombinant Factor Xa decoy) for apixaban and rivaroxaban; ciraparantag (under investigation). In the absence of a specific antidote, prothrombin complex concentrates (PCC) are used for life-threatening bleeding. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for AF; 3-6 months or indefinite for VTE based on risk factors.
- **Monitoring:** No routine coagulation monitoring. Check renal function at baseline and at least annually (more frequently in CKD or changing clinical status). Specific anti-Xa chromogenic assays can measure drug levels if clinically indicated (e.g., emergency surgery, bleeding, overdose). [1,2]

### 7.3 Direct Oral Anticoagulant (DOAC) – Direct Thrombin Inhibitor

- **Drug Name:** Dabigatran etexilate. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Dabigatran etexilate is an oral prodrug that is rapidly hydrolyzed by plasma esterases to the active moiety, dabigatran. Dabigatran is a potent, competitive, reversible, direct inhibitor of thrombin (Factor IIa). Unlike heparins, it does not require antithrombin as a cofactor. It binds to both free thrombin in solution and thrombin bound to fibrin (clot-bound thrombin), preventing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin. It also inhibits thrombin-mediated positive feedback activation of Factors V, VIII, and XI, and thrombin-induced platelet aggregation (by blocking PAR-1 and PAR-4). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Stroke Prevention in Non-Valvular Atrial Fibrillation.
  - Treatment and Secondary Prevention of VTE.
  - Post-Operative Prophylaxis after Hip or Knee Arthroplasty. [1,2,4,6]
- **Contraindications:** Mechanical heart valves (absolute contraindication, based on the RE-ALIGN trial), active bleeding, severe renal impairment (CrCl <15 mL/min), and severe hepatic impairment. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - AF: 150 mg orally twice daily (110 mg twice daily for patients ≥80 years, or those on concomitant verapamil; in some countries, 110 mg is the standard dose for patients ≥75-80 years or with increased bleeding risk).
  - VTE: 150 mg twice daily after initial 5-10 days of LMWH.
  - Note: Capsules must be swallowed whole; do not crush or open (increases bioavailability by 75% and risk of overdose/bleeding). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hemorrhage. Dyspepsia (up to 10-15%, due to the tartaric acid core in the capsule; can be mitigated by taking with food). A specific reversal agent exists: Idarucizumab (a humanized monoclonal antibody fragment that binds dabigatran with high affinity). In the absence of idarucizumab, hemodialysis can remove dabigatran (it is ~60% protein-bound and has moderate dialyzability). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for AF; variable for VTE.
- **Monitoring:** Renal function (dabigatran is 80% renally excreted as active drug). Coagulation tests: a normal thrombin time (TT) or dilute thrombin time essentially excludes therapeutic dabigatran levels; a normal aPTT suggests a low level but does not reliably exclude therapeutic concentrations. [1,2]

### 7.4 Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)

- **Mechanism of Action:** UFH is a mixture of sulfated glycosaminoglycan chains of varying lengths (molecular weight 3,000-30,000 Da). It binds to antithrombin III (ATIII) through a unique pentasaccharide sequence, which induces a conformational change in ATIII, converting it from a slow to a rapid inhibitor of clotting factors. The heparin-ATIII complex inactivates thrombin (Factor IIa), Factor Xa, Factor IXa, Factor XIa, and Factor XIIa. To inactivate thrombin, heparin must bind simultaneously to both ATIII and thrombin, which requires a heparin chain of at least 18 saccharide units. UFH contains many chains of sufficient length and thus has equal anti-Factor Xa and anti-thrombin (anti-IIa) activity (ratio 1:1). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Initial Management of Acute Coronary Syndromes (NSTEMI/UA, STEMI): Often used in the catheterization laboratory.
  - Bridging Therapy: For warfarin interruption in patients with mechanical heart valves or high thromboembolic risk.
  - Prophylaxis and Treatment of DVT/PE.
  - Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Hemodialysis.
  - Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT): In this case, non-heparin anticoagulants (argatroban, bivalirudin) must be used. [1,2,5]
- **Contraindications:** Active bleeding, history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), severe thrombocytopenia. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** Therapeutic dosing: IV bolus of 60-80 units/kg (max 5000 units), followed by a continuous infusion of 12-18 units/kg/hour, titrated to a target aPTT of 1.5-2.5 times the control. Prophylactic dosing: 5000 units subcutaneously every 8-12 hours. [1,2]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Hemorrhage: Major bleeding risk ~2-5% with therapeutic dosing.
  - Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT): An immune-mediated, life-threatening complication caused by IgG antibodies against the heparin-PF4 (platelet factor 4) complex. These antibodies activate platelets, causing a paradoxical prothrombotic state. It typically occurs 5-14 days after initiation and is characterized by a >50% drop in platelet count, venous and arterial thrombosis (limb ischemia, stroke, MI, PE), and skin necrosis at injection sites. All heparin must be stopped immediately, and a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux in stable cases) must be started. Warfarin is contraindicated acutely (can cause venous limb gangrene from protein C depletion).
  - Osteoporosis: With prolonged (>1 month) high-dose therapy.
  - Hyperkalemia: Due to inhibition of aldosterone synthesis.
  - Hypersensitivity reactions. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Typically 48-72 hours as a bridge, or until therapeutic oral anticoagulation is established.
- **Monitoring:** aPTT every 6 hours until stable, then daily. Platelet count every 2-3 days. [1,2]

### 7.5 Low Molecular Weight Heparins (LMWH)

- **Drug Names:** Enoxaparin, Dalteparin, Tinzaparin. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** LMWHs are produced by chemical or enzymatic depolymerization of UFH, resulting in shorter polysaccharide chains (mean molecular weight ~4,000-5,000 Da). They bind to antithrombin III through the same pentasaccharide sequence, enhancing Factor Xa inhibition. However, because most LMWH chains are too short (<18 saccharides) to bridge ATIII to thrombin, they preferentially inhibit Factor Xa (anti-Xa:anti-IIa ratio is 2:1 to 4:1). This selective Factor Xa inhibition is equally effective as an anticoagulant. Advantages over UFH include: more predictable pharmacokinetics (less binding to plasma proteins and endothelial cells, allowing weight-based dosing without routine monitoring), a longer half-life (allowing once or twice-daily subcutaneous injection), and a lower risk of HIT and osteoporosis. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Initial Management of NSTEMI/UA and STEMI (in select protocols).
  - Bridging Therapy for warfarin (preferred agent over UFH).
  - Treatment of Acute DVT and PE (often as a bridge to oral anticoagulation).
  - Prophylaxis in high-risk hospitalized medical and surgical patients. [1,2,5,6]
- **Contraindications:** Active bleeding, history of HIT (relative; fondaparinux is preferred), severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min; UFH is preferred or enoxaparin dose must be reduced and monitored with anti-Xa levels). [1,2]

### Dosage Examples (Enoxaparin):

  - Therapeutic: 1 mg/kg subcutaneously every 12 hours (or 1.5 mg/kg once daily). For STEMI with PCI, a 30 mg IV bolus is given.
  - Prophylactic: 40 mg subcutaneously once daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hemorrhage, HIT (less common than UFH), and osteoporosis (lower risk than UFH). Spinal/epidural hematoma risk (in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia or spinal puncture). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Typically 5-7 days as a bridge to warfarin or until therapeutic DOAC is achieved.
- **Monitoring:** Routine monitoring is not required. In renal impairment, pregnancy, or obesity, anti-Factor Xa levels can be measured (target 0.5-1.0 IU/mL for twice-daily dosing). Platelet counts are monitored periodically. [1,2]

### 7.6 Fondaparinux

- **Mechanism of Action:** A synthetic pentasaccharide that is the exact sequence of heparin that binds to antithrombin III. By binding to ATIII, it markedly enhances its inactivation of Factor Xa. However, because it is only a pentasaccharide, it is too short to bridge ATIII to thrombin, and therefore has no anti-thrombin activity (pure anti-Xa agent). [1,2]

### Indications:

  - NSTEMI/UA: In patients managed with a conservative (non-invasive) strategy.
  - STEMI: Particularly with streptokinase or no reperfusion, or when HIT is a concern.
  - Treatment and Prophylaxis of VTE. [5,6]
- **Contraindications:** Active bleeding, severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min; accumulates and increases bleeding risk), bacterial endocarditis. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Therapeutic (VTE, ACS): 2.5-7.5 mg subcutaneously once daily (weight-based). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hemorrhage. HIT is extremely rare (no PF4 binding), making it safe in HIT. Cannot be used in PCI as monotherapy (risk of catheter-related thrombosis) unless an additional anticoagulant with anti-IIa activity (e.g., UFH, bivalirudin) is administered. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** 5-9 days.
- **Monitoring:** No routine monitoring. Anti-Xa levels can be measured in special populations. [1,2]

### 7.7 Direct Thrombin Inhibitors (Parenteral)

- **Drug Names:** Bivalirudin, Argatroban. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These are direct, bivalent (bivalirudin) or univalent (argatroban) inhibitors of thrombin that do not require antithrombin as a cofactor. They bind to both the active site and exosite I of thrombin, preventing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Bivalirudin: PCI in ACS, particularly in patients with HIT or at high risk of bleeding. It has a short half-life (25 minutes).
  - Argatroban: Treatment and prevention of thrombosis in patients with HIT (with or without thrombosis). Metabolized in the liver, it is the agent of choice for HIT in renal failure. [1,2,5]
- **Contraindications:** Active bleeding. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Bivalirudin: IV bolus of 0.75 mg/kg, then 1.75 mg/kg/hour infusion during PCI.
  - Argatroban: IV infusion starting at 2 mcg/kg/min, titrated to aPTT 1.5-3.0 times baseline (max 10 mcg/kg/min). Dose reduction in hepatic impairment. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hemorrhage. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Procedural (bivalirudin) or until transition to oral anticoagulation (argatroban).
- **Monitoring:** aPTT for argatroban; ACT (activated clotting time) for bivalirudin during PCI. [1,2]

## Part VIII: Fibrinolytics

- **Drug Names:** Alteplase (recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, tPA), Tenecteplase (TNK-tPA), Reteplase (rPA). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are recombinant forms of human tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). They bind to fibrin within a thrombus and convert the entrapped plasminogen to the active enzyme plasmin. Plasmin then proteolytically degrades the fibrin mesh, dissolving the clot and restoring blood flow. Fibrin specificity varies: alteplase and tenecteplase are highly fibrin-specific (they activate plasminogen primarily at the site of the thrombus); streptokinase (rarely used now) is non-fibrin-specific and causes systemic fibrinolysis. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Acute ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI): Class I when timely primary PCI (within 120 minutes of first medical contact) is not possible. The goal door-to-needle time is ≤30 minutes. Tenecteplase is the preferred agent due to its ease of administration (single IV bolus). [5]
  - Acute Ischemic Stroke: Alteplase within 3-4.5 hours of symptom onset.
  - Acute Massive or High-Risk Pulmonary Embolism: With hemodynamic compromise (systolic BP <90 mmHg). [6]
  - Prosthetic Mechanical Heart Valve Thrombosis: In critically ill patients who are not surgical candidates. [4]
- **Absolute Contraindications:** Any prior intracranial hemorrhage, known structural cerebral vascular lesion, known intracranial malignancy, ischemic stroke within 3 months, suspected aortic dissection, active bleeding or bleeding diathesis, recent significant head/facial trauma, and uncontrolled severe hypertension (>185/110 mmHg). [1,2,5]

### Dosage:

  - Tenecteplase: Single IV bolus over 5 seconds, weight-based (30-50 mg). [5]
  - Alteplase for STEMI: 15 mg IV bolus, then 0.75 mg/kg over 30 minutes (max 50 mg), then 0.5 mg/kg over 60 minutes (max 35 mg). Total max 100 mg. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Intracranial hemorrhage (the most feared, 0.5-1.0% risk), major systemic bleeding, reperfusion arrhythmias (usually benign, AIVR), and allergic reactions (especially with streptokinase). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Single administration.

## Part IX: Antiarrhythmic Agents (Vaughan-Williams Classification)

### 9.1 Class I: Sodium Channel Blockers

- **Mechanism:** These drugs bind to and block voltage-gated fast sodium channels (Nav1.5) in cardiac myocytes, reducing the maximal upstroke velocity (dV/dtmax) of phase 0 of the action potential. This slows conduction velocity and can suppress ectopic automaticity. [1,2]

### 9.1.1 Class IA

- **Drug Names:** Procainamide, Quinidine, Disopyramide. [1,2]
- **Mechanism:** Intermediate sodium channel blocking kinetics. They slow conduction and prolong repolarization (prolong the action potential duration and QT interval) by also blocking potassium channels (IKr). Procainamide is the most commonly used IV agent. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Procainamide: Acute pharmacologic cardioversion of atrial fibrillation (particularly in pre-excited AF/WPW), and ventricular arrhythmias. Quinidine: Historical use for AF; now rarely used (in combination with verapamil or a beta-blocker). [6]
- **Important Side Effects:** QT prolongation and torsades de pointes. Procainamide: Drug-induced lupus (long-term use), hypotension. Disopyramide: Negative inotrope (contraindicated in heart failure). [1,2]

### 9.1.2 Class IB

- **Drug Names:** Lidocaine, Mexiletine. [1,2]
- **Mechanism:** Fast sodium channel kinetics. They preferentially bind to sodium channels in the inactivated state, and rapidly dissociate at normal resting potentials. This means they selectively suppress depolarized, ischemic, or diseased tissue without significantly affecting normal tissue. They shorten, rather than prolong, repolarization. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Lidocaine (IV): Ventricular arrhythmias, particularly in the setting of acute myocardial ischemia (second-line to amiodarone). Mexiletine (oral): Chronic ventricular arrhythmias, particularly in congenital long QT syndrome type 3 (LQT3). [6]
- **Important Side Effects:** Neurological toxicity (paresthesias, dizziness, confusion, seizures) at toxic levels. [1,2]

### 9.1.3 Class IC

- **Drug Names:** Flecainide, Propafenone. [1,2]
- **Mechanism:** Slow sodium channel kinetics. They are the most potent sodium channel blockers, causing profound slowing of conduction with little effect on repolarization. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Pharmacologic cardioversion of atrial fibrillation in patients without structural heart disease ("pill-in-the-pocket" approach). Maintenance of sinus rhythm in paroxysmal AF. [6]
- **Contraindications:** Coronary artery disease, structural heart disease (LVH, HFrEF, prior MI). The CAST trial demonstrated increased mortality from proarrhythmia when flecainide and encainide were used to suppress PVCs post-MI. Propafenone also has beta-blocking activity. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Atrial flutter with 1:1 AV conduction (due to slowing of the atrial rate and possible AV nodal facilitation), proarrhythmia, and conduction disturbances. [1,2]

### 9.2 Class II: Beta-Blockers

- See the comprehensive section above. [1,2]

### 9.3 Class III: Potassium Channel Blockers

- **Drug Names:** Amiodarone, Dronedarone, Sotalol, Dofetilide, Ibutilide. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** The primary mechanism is blockade of the rapid delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr, encoded by the hERG gene), which prolongs phase 3 repolarization, increasing the action potential duration and effective refractory period. This prolongs the QT interval, which can be both therapeutic (suppressing re-entry) and dangerous (predisposing to torsades de pointes).
  - Amiodarone: A unique, multi-channel blocker with Class I, II (non-competitive beta-blockade), III (IKr, IKs blockade), and IV (L-type calcium channel blockade) effects. It prolongs repolarization in all cardiac tissues, making it the most potent and versatile antiarrhythmic agent available.
  - Sotalol: A racemic mixture. The D-isomer is a Class III potassium channel blocker; the L-isomer is a non-selective beta-blocker (Class II). It is a combined Class II/III agent. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Amiodarone: Atrial fibrillation (rhythm control, rate control), ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation (including cardiac arrest with shock-refractory VF/pulseless VT), and prevention of recurrent VT/VF. [4,6]
  - Dronedarone: Maintenance of sinus rhythm in paroxysmal or persistent AF after cardioversion, in patients without decompensated HF. It is less effective but safer than amiodarone. [4,6]
  - Sotalol: Maintenance of sinus rhythm in AF and treatment of ventricular arrhythmias.
  - Dofetilide: Pharmacologic cardioversion and maintenance of sinus rhythm in AF/Aflutter.
  - Ibutilide: Acute pharmacologic cardioversion of atrial fibrillation and flutter (IV). [1,2,6]

### Contraindications:

  - Amiodarone: Iodine allergy, severe sinus node dysfunction (without pacemaker), high-grade AV block, and thyroid dysfunction (relative).
  - Dronedarone: NYHA Class IV heart failure or recent decompensation (ANDROMEDA trial), permanent AF, and concomitant use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or QT-prolonging drugs.
  - Sotalol/Dofetilide/Ibutilide: Prolonged QT at baseline, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, severe renal impairment (for sotalol and dofetilide, which are renally cleared). [1,2]

### Dosage Examples:

  - Amiodarone: Oral loading: 400-600 mg/day in divided doses for 2-4 weeks, then taper to maintenance 100-200 mg/day. IV: 150 mg bolus over 10 minutes, then 1 mg/min for 6 hours, then 0.5 mg/min.
  - Sotalol: 80-120 mg orally twice daily. Dose-adjust for renal function (CrCl).
  - Dofetilide: Dosed based on CrCl, initiated in-hospital with continuous ECG monitoring. [1,2]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Amiodarone: This drug has a very long half-life (25-110 days) and significant multi-organ toxicity. Pulmonary fibrosis (1-5%, potentially fatal, dose-dependent, cumulative dose >400 g is a major risk factor). Thyroid dysfunction (both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism; due to its high iodine content, 37% iodine by weight). Corneal microdeposits (>90% of patients, rarely visually significant). Hepatotoxicity (elevated transaminases to cirrhosis). Skin discoloration (slate-gray, photosensitive). Peripheral neuropathy. Optic neuritis. It interacts with many drugs, most notably warfarin (inhibits CYP2C9, increases INR by 30-50%) and digoxin (increases digoxin levels).
  - Dronedarone: Less toxic than amiodarone. GI upset. Rare hepatotoxicity. Contraindicated in symptomatic HF.
  - Sotalol and Dofetilide: QT prolongation and risk of torsades de pointes (dose-related; risk is highest with initiation in the hospital). Both require CrCl-based dosing and monitoring. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic for maintenance of sinus rhythm; acute for cardioversion.

### 9.4 Class IV: Non-Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers

- See the comprehensive section above. Verapamil and Diltiazem are Class IV antiarrhythmics. [1,2]

### 9.5 Other Antiarrhythmics

### Adenosine:

  - **Mechanism of Action:** An endogenous nucleoside that binds to A1 purinergic receptors on the SA and AV nodes. This activates the G protein-gated inward rectifier potassium channel (IKado), causing potassium efflux, hyperpolarization of the cell membrane, and transient suppression of SA node automaticity and AV nodal conduction. It has an ultra-short half-life (<10 seconds) due to rapid cellular uptake and metabolism by adenosine deaminase. [1,2]
  - **Indications:** Acute termination of re-entrant supraventricular tachycardias involving the AV node (AVNRT, AVRT). It is the diagnostic and therapeutic agent of choice for stable, regular, narrow-complex tachycardia. [6]
  - **Contraindications:** High-grade AV block, sick sinus syndrome (without pacemaker), active bronchospasm (can cause bronchoconstriction), and WPW syndrome with atrial fibrillation (may precipitate ventricular fibrillation by promoting antegrade accessory pathway conduction).
  - **Dosage:** Rapid IV bolus: 6 mg over 1-2 seconds, followed by a 20 mL saline flush. If no response after 1-2 minutes, give 12 mg bolus, may repeat once. [1,2,6]
  - **Important Side Effects:** Transient asystole (often the goal, but alarming), facial flushing, chest discomfort, dyspnea, and a sense of impending doom. These are due to adenosine receptor activation on nociceptive afferents and are very short-lived. Caffeine and theophylline are competitive antagonists and can block its effect. Dipyridamole blocks adenosine reuptake and potentiates its effect. [1,2]

### Digoxin:

  - (See detailed section in Part XI for its inotropic effects). As an antiarrhythmic, it enhances vagal tone, slowing AV nodal conduction and ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation. It is a second-line agent for chronic rate control, particularly in patients with hypotension or HFrEF. [1,2,4]

### Magnesium Sulfate (IV):

  - **Mechanism:** Magnesium is a cofactor for the Na-K-ATPase pump and stabilizes the membrane potential. It is the drug of choice for torsades de pointes, regardless of serum magnesium levels. [1,2]
  - **Indications:** Torsades de pointes, cardiac arrest with hypomagnesemia or prolonged QT. [6]
  - **Dosage:** 2 g IV bolus over 1-2 minutes for cardiac arrest; 1-2 g over 5-60 minutes for torsades with a pulse, followed by an infusion. [1,2,6]

## Part X: Lipid-Lowering Agents

### 10.1 HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors (Statins)

- **Drug Names:** Atorvastatin, Rosuvastatin, Simvastatin, Pravastatin, Pitavastatin. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are competitive inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in the hepatic cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. By inhibiting this enzyme, they reduce intracellular cholesterol synthesis in the liver. This leads to an upregulation of LDL receptors on the hepatocyte surface, which increases the clearance of LDL cholesterol (and its precursor, VLDL) from the circulation. As a result, they produce a substantial reduction in LDL cholesterol (20-55% depending on the specific statin and dose), a modest reduction in triglycerides, and a small increase in HDL cholesterol. Critically, statins also have pleiotropic effects that contribute to their cardiovascular benefit, independent of LDL lowering: improvement of endothelial function, reduction of vascular inflammation (decreased CRP), stabilization of atherosclerotic plaque (reducing the lipid core and thickening the fibrous cap), and antithrombotic effects. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Secondary Prevention of Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD): High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 mg or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg) is indicated for all patients with clinical ASCVD (coronary artery disease, stroke/TIA, peripheral arterial disease), regardless of baseline LDL. [7]
  - Primary Prevention: In adults aged 40-75 years with LDL-C ≥70 mg/dL and a 10-year ASCVD risk ≥7.5%, a moderate-intensity statin is recommended.
  - Acute Coronary Syndrome: High-intensity statin should be initiated as early as possible, regardless of baseline LDL.
  - Familial Hypercholesterolemia: High-intensity statin is the cornerstone of therapy. [7]
- **Contraindications:** Active liver disease or unexplained persistent elevations of hepatic transaminases (>3x upper limit of normal), pregnancy and lactation (cholesterol is essential for fetal development), and concomitant use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors with simvastatin or high-dose atorvastatin. [1,2]

### Dosage Examples (High-Intensity, reduces LDL ≥50%):

  - Atorvastatin: 40-80 mg orally once daily.
  - Rosuvastatin: 20-40 mg orally once daily.
  - **Dosage Examples (Moderate-Intensity, reduces LDL 30-49%):**
  - Atorvastatin: 10-20 mg once daily.
  - Rosuvastatin: 5-10 mg once daily.
  - Simvastatin: 20-40 mg once daily (80 mg is no longer recommended due to myopathy risk).
  - Pravastatin: 40-80 mg once daily. [7]

### Important Side Effects:

  - Myopathy and Rhabdomyolysis: The most clinically significant adverse effect. Spectrum ranges from myalgia (muscle pain without CK elevation, 2-11% in observational studies, but lower in randomized trials) to myositis (muscle pain with CK elevation) to rhabdomyolysis (CK >10x upper limit of normal, myoglobinuria, acute kidney injury; very rare, <0.1%). Risk factors for myopathy include: high doses, older age, female sex, small body frame, renal impairment, hypothyroidism, and important drug interactions (simvastatin and atorvastatin are metabolized by CYP3A4; strong inhibitors like clarithromycin, azole antifungals, and protease inhibitors significantly increase levels; pravastatin and rosuvastatin are not significantly metabolized by CYP enzymes and have fewer interactions).
  - Hepatotoxicity: Asymptomatic transaminase elevation (1-3%), usually transient and benign. Routine monitoring of liver enzymes is no longer recommended by the FDA for all statin users (baseline measurement before initiation is recommended, and repeated only if clinically indicated).
  - Increased Risk of New-Onset Diabetes Mellitus: A small, dose-dependent risk (about 1 excess case per 1000 patient-years), primarily in patients with pre-existing metabolic risk factors. The absolute cardiovascular benefit far outweighs this risk.
  - Cognitive Effects: Reports of memory loss and confusion are rare and reversible upon discontinuation, but no causal link has been established in large trials. [1,2,7]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for secondary prevention and FH; indefinite for primary prevention with continued risk.

### 10.2 Cholesterol Absorption Inhibitor

- **Drug Name:** Ezetimibe. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Ezetimibe inhibits the Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1) protein located on the brush border of jejunal enterocytes. NPC1L1 is responsible for the absorption of dietary and biliary cholesterol from the intestinal lumen. By blocking this transporter, ezetimibe reduces cholesterol delivery to the liver, which upregulates hepatic LDL receptors and increases clearance of LDL from the blood. It reduces LDL cholesterol by an additional 15-25% when added to a statin. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Adjunct to statin therapy in patients with ASCVD or FH who require additional LDL-lowering to reach target.
  - Statin-intolerant patients.
  - In combination with a statin for secondary prevention (IMPROVE-IT trial showed incremental clinical benefit with ezetimibe added to simvastatin in post-ACS patients). [7]
- **Contraindications:** Active liver disease or unexplained persistent transaminase elevations (when used with a statin). It is generally very well tolerated as monotherapy. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 10 mg orally once daily, with or without food. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Rarely causes myopathy or transaminase elevation; usually very well-tolerated. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong for secondary prevention.

### 10.3 PCSK9 Inhibitors

- **Drug Names:** Alirocumab, Evolocumab (monoclonal antibodies), Inclisiran (small interfering RNA). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) is a protease produced by the liver that binds to LDL receptors on the hepatocyte surface, targeting them for lysosomal degradation instead of recycling them to the cell membrane. Alirocumab and evolocumab are fully humanized monoclonal antibodies that bind directly to circulating PCSK9, preventing it from interacting with the LDL receptor. This increases the number of available LDL receptors, profoundly lowering LDL cholesterol by 50-65% on top of a statin. Inclisiran is a synthetic small interfering RNA (siRNA) that is taken up by hepatocytes and silences the translation of PCSK9 mRNA, thereby reducing PCSK9 synthesis. It requires only twice-yearly subcutaneous injections. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Adults with clinical ASCVD or Familial Hypercholesterolemia who require additional lowering of LDL cholesterol despite maximally tolerated statin therapy and ezetimibe.
  - Statin-intolerant patients with very high ASCVD risk. [7]
- **Contraindications:** History of serious hypersensitivity reaction. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Alirocumab: 75-150 mg subcutaneously every 2 weeks, or 300 mg every 4 weeks.
  - Evolocumab: 140 mg subcutaneously every 2 weeks, or 420 mg once monthly.
  - Inclisiran: 284 mg subcutaneously on Day 1, Day 90, and then every 6 months. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Injection site reactions (pruritus, erythema, swelling). Nasopharyngitis. In very long-term safety analysis, no significant increase in adverse events, including neurocognitive disorders or new-onset diabetes, has been confirmed, though these were theoretical concerns due to the very low LDL-C levels achieved (<25 mg/dL). [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Lifelong.

### 10.4 Bempedoic Acid

- **Mechanism of Action:** Bempedoic acid is a prodrug that is activated by very long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase-1 (ACSVL1), an enzyme present in the liver but not in skeletal muscle. The active metabolite inhibits ATP-citrate lyase, an enzyme upstream of HMG-CoA reductase in the cholesterol synthesis pathway. This reduces hepatic cholesterol synthesis, upregulating LDL receptors. Because it is not activated in muscle, it has a much lower risk of myopathy than statins. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Adjunct to maximally tolerated statin therapy in adults with ASCVD or heterozygous FH who require additional LDL-C lowering. [7]
- **Dosage:** 180 mg orally once daily. Available in fixed-dose combination with ezetimibe. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Mild increase in uric acid levels (can precipitate gout). Slight increase in risk of tendon rupture. [1,2]

### 10.5 Fibrates

- **Drug Names:** Fenofibrate, Gemfibrozil. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Fibrates are agonists of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α), a nuclear receptor that regulates lipid metabolism. Activation of PPAR-α in the liver increases the expression of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which hydrolyzes triglycerides in VLDL and chylomicrons. They also increase fatty acid beta-oxidation and reduce apolipoprotein C-III (an inhibitor of LPL). The net effect is a significant reduction in triglycerides (30-50%) and an increase in HDL (5-15%). Their effect on LDL is variable (may increase LDL in patients with very high triglycerides). [1,2]
- **Indications:** Severe hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides >500 mg/dL) to reduce the risk of pancreatitis. They may be used in combination with statins for moderate hypertriglyceridemia, but large outcome trials have not demonstrated a consistent cardiovascular mortality benefit when added to a statin (except in the subgroup with high triglycerides and low HDL in ACCORD and PROMINENT). [7]
- **Contraindications:** Severe hepatic or renal impairment, gallbladder disease. Gemfibrozil is absolutely contraindicated with simvastatin and should be avoided with other statins due to a high risk of rhabdomyolysis (gemfibrozil inhibits statin glucuronidation, leading to a 2-5 fold increase in statin plasma levels). Fenofibrate is safer in combination with statins. [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Fenofibrate: 145-160 mg orally once daily.
  - Gemfibrozil: 600 mg orally twice daily, before meals. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Myopathy (especially with gemfibrozil + statin), cholelithiasis (increased biliary cholesterol excretion), and transaminase elevation. [1,2]

### 10.6 Omega-3 Fatty Acids

- **Drug Name:** Icosapent ethyl (highly purified eicosapentaenoic acid, EPA). [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Icosapent ethyl is a prescription formulation of EPA. While the exact mechanisms are not fully elucidated, EPA reduces hepatic triglyceride synthesis and VLDL secretion, and enhances the clearance of circulating triglycerides. It also has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and membrane-stabilizing effects, including the inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This is distinct from mixed omega-3 products containing both EPA and DHA, which have not shown a cardiovascular benefit. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Based on the REDUCE-IT trial, icosapent ethyl (4 g/day) is indicated for cardiovascular risk reduction in patients with established ASCVD or diabetes plus ≥2 risk factors, who have elevated triglycerides (150-499 mg/dL) despite statin therapy. [7]
- **Dosage:** 2 g orally twice daily (total 4 g/day). [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Atrial fibrillation/flutter (increased risk in REDUCE-IT, ~3-5% absolute increase), bleeding (mildly increased risk). [1,2]

### 10.7 Bile Acid Sequestrants

- **Drug Names:** Cholestyramine, Colesevelam, Colestipol. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These are large, non-absorbable, positively charged resins that bind negatively charged bile acids in the intestinal lumen, preventing their enterohepatic recirculation. This forces the liver to divert cholesterol to the synthesis of new bile acids, depleting the hepatic cholesterol pool and upregulating LDL receptors. They lower LDL by 15-25%. They can increase triglycerides slightly. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Adjunct to statins when additional LDL-lowering is needed, or in patients who cannot tolerate statins. Colesevelam is also approved for improving glycemic control in type 2 diabetes. [7]
- **Contraindications:** Complete biliary obstruction, severe hypertriglyceridemia (>500 mg/dL). [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Colesevelam: 3.75 g orally once daily or 1.875 g twice daily with meals.
  - Cholestyramine: 4 g packets 1-6 times daily. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Gastrointestinal complaints (bloating, constipation, flatulence) are very common and limit adherence. They can interfere with the absorption of many other drugs (warfarin, digoxin, thyroid hormone, thiazides, statins); other drugs should be taken 1 hour before or 4 hours after the resin. [1,2]

## Part XI: Positive Inotropic Agents

### 11.1 Cardiac Glycosides

- **Drug Name:** Digoxin. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** Digoxin binds to and inhibits the alpha-subunit of the sarcolemmal Na-K-ATPase pump in cardiac myocytes. This inhibition increases intracellular sodium concentration. The elevated intracellular sodium reduces the activity of the Na-Ca exchanger (NCX), which normally extrudes one calcium ion in exchange for three sodium ions (secondary active transport). With less sodium gradient driving calcium extrusion, intracellular calcium increases. The excess calcium is sequestered in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, leading to a larger calcium release during subsequent action potentials (increased calcium transient), which enhances the force of contraction (positive inotropy). This is a unique inotropic mechanism that does not increase heart rate or myocardial oxygen consumption significantly. Additionally, at therapeutic concentrations, digoxin enhances parasympathetic (vagal) outflow, which slows the sinus rate (negative chronotropy) and reduces AV nodal conduction velocity (negative dromotropy). This vagotonic effect is useful for controlling ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation. At toxic levels, digoxin also increases sympathetic activity and can cause calcium overload, leading to delayed afterdepolarizations and arrhythmias. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Rate Control in Atrial Fibrillation: Particularly useful as a second-line agent in patients with concomitant heart failure and hypotension, or as an adjunct to beta-blockers or non-DHPs when rate control is inadequate. It is less effective at controlling the ventricular rate during exercise (when vagal tone is withdrawn and sympathetic activity dominates). [4,6]
  - Symptomatic Chronic HFrEF: In patients with LVEF ≤40% who remain symptomatic (NYHA II-IV) despite optimal doses of an ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, and MRA, digoxin can be added to reduce the risk of heart failure hospitalization. It does not reduce overall mortality (DIG trial). [4]
- **Contraindications:** Ventricular fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction (relative, increased arrhythmic risk), restrictive cardiomyopathy, constrictive pericarditis, and known hypersensitivity. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** The typical maintenance dose is 0.125-0.25 mg orally once daily, adjusted based on renal function, age, and serum levels. Loading doses (digitalization) are rarely used now. The therapeutic serum digoxin concentration is 0.5-0.9 ng/mL (measured 6-8 hours after the last dose, or just before the next dose). Levels >1.2 ng/mL are associated with increased mortality. [1,2,4]
- **Important Side Effects:** Digoxin has a very narrow therapeutic index.
  - Cardiac Arrhythmias: The hallmark of digoxin toxicity is "paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with 2:1 AV block" (PAT with block). It can cause virtually any arrhythmia, including frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), bidirectional ventricular tachycardia (a specific but rare sign), ventricular fibrillation, and severe bradycardia or high-grade AV block.
  - Neurological: Xanthopsia (yellow-green visual halos), scotomas, blurred vision, confusion, delirium.
  - Gastrointestinal: Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea (often the earliest signs of toxicity).
  - Factors Predisposing to Toxicity: Hypokalemia (digoxin competes with potassium for the Na-K-ATPase binding site; low potassium enhances digoxin binding and toxicity), hypercalcemia, hypomagnesemia, renal impairment (digoxin is 70-80% renally excreted), advanced age, and interacting drugs that increase digoxin levels (amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine, macrolides, cyclosporine). [1,2]

### Treatment of Toxicity:

  - Discontinue digoxin and any contributing medications.
  - Correct electrolyte abnormalities (especially hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia).
  - For life-threatening toxicity (ventricular arrhythmias, hemodynamic instability, severe hyperkalemia >5.0 mmol/L due to Na-K-ATPase poisoning of skeletal muscle), administer digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Digoxin Immune Fab, Digibind, DigiFab). These bind free digoxin and rapidly reverse toxicity. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Chronic for rate control and symptom relief in HFrEF.

### 11.2 Beta-Adrenergic Agonists

- **Drug Names:** Dobutamine, Dopamine, Isoproterenol. [1,2]

### Mechanism of Action:

  - Dobutamine: A synthetic catecholamine that is a strong agonist at beta-1 adrenergic receptors (increasing myocardial contractility and heart rate) and a weak agonist at beta-2 and alpha-1 receptors. The net effect at therapeutic doses is a significant increase in cardiac output (positive inotropy) with relatively balanced peripheral vascular effects (mild vasodilation from beta-2 effects offsetting alpha-1 vasoconstriction). It is primarily an inotrope, not a vasopressor.
  - Dopamine: An endogenous catecholamine precursor to norepinephrine. Its effects are dose-dependent. At low doses (1-3 mcg/kg/min), it stimulates dopamine D1 receptors in the renal and splanchnic vasculature, causing vasodilation and increased renal blood flow (the "renal dose" is controversial and not recommended for renal protection). At intermediate doses (3-10 mcg/kg/min), it stimulates beta-1 receptors, increasing heart rate and contractility. At high doses (>10 mcg/kg/min), alpha-1 adrenergic stimulation predominates, causing vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure.
  - Isoproterenol: A pure, non-selective beta-adrenergic agonist (beta-1 and beta-2). It increases heart rate (positive chronotrope) and contractility (positive inotrope), while causing peripheral vasodilation (beta-2) and a fall in diastolic blood pressure. It is used for severe bradycardia or torsades de pointes while pacing is arranged. [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Dobutamine: Acute decompensated systolic heart failure with low cardiac output and adequate blood pressure (cardiogenic shock that is not profoundly hypotensive). Dobutamine stress echocardiography.
  - Dopamine: Historically used for cardiogenic shock; norepinephrine is now preferred due to a lower rate of arrhythmias and better outcomes.
  - Isoproterenol: Symptomatic bradycardia, torsades de pointes (to increase heart rate and shorten the QT interval). [1,2,4,6]
- **Contraindications:** Severe ventricular arrhythmias, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, severe hypotension (for dobutamine and isoproterenol). [1,2]

### Dosage:

  - Dobutamine: IV infusion 2-20 mcg/kg/min.
  - Dopamine: IV infusion 2-20 mcg/kg/min (typical "inotropic" dose is 5-10 mcg/kg/min).
  - Isoproterenol: IV infusion 0.5-10 mcg/min. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Tachycardia, arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopy), myocardial ischemia (increased MVO2), hypotension (isoproterenol). Dobutamine can cause hypotension due to beta-2 vasodilation if the patient is volume-depleted. Tolerance (tachyphylaxis) develops with prolonged dobutamine infusion. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Short-term, as a bridge to recovery, transplant, or a mechanical device.

### 11.3 Phosphodiesterase-3 Inhibitors (Inodilators)

- **Drug Names:** Milrinone, Enoximone. [1,2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** These drugs are selective inhibitors of phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE3), the enzyme that degrades cAMP in cardiac myocytes and vascular smooth muscle. By inhibiting PDE3, they increase intracellular cAMP. In cardiac myocytes, increased cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates L-type calcium channels (increasing calcium influx) and phospholamban (increasing SR calcium reuptake), leading to a significant increase in contractility (positive inotropy) and accelerated relaxation (positive lusitropy). In vascular smooth muscle, increased cAMP causes vasodilation (reducing systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance, afterload). They are therefore "inodilators": they increase contractility while simultaneously unloading the ventricle. Unlike beta-agonists, their mechanism is independent of the beta-receptor, and they remain effective in the presence of beta-blockade. [1,2]
- **Indications:** Acute decompensated heart failure with low cardiac output and adequate blood pressure, particularly in patients on chronic beta-blocker therapy or those who have developed tachyphylaxis to dobutamine. Milrinone is also used in the management of right ventricular failure and pulmonary hypertension post-cardiac surgery. [4,6]
- **Contraindications:** Severe aortic or pulmonic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, acute myocardial infarction, and severe hypotension. Long-term oral PDE3 inhibitors (like milrinone) have been associated with increased mortality in chronic heart failure (PROMISE trial) and are not used chronically. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** Milrinone: IV bolus of 50 mcg/kg over 10 minutes (optional and often omitted in hypotensive patients), followed by a continuous infusion of 0.125-0.5 mcg/kg/min. Renally excreted; dose reduction in renal impairment. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Ventricular and atrial arrhythmias, hypotension, and headache. Milrinone can cause thrombocytopenia. [1,2]
- **Duration of Treatment:** Short-term IV infusion for 24-72 hours.

### 11.4 Calcium Sensitizer

- **Drug Name:** Levosimendan. [2]
- **Mechanism of Action:** A unique inodilator that enhances cardiac contractility by sensitizing troponin C to calcium, increasing the force of contraction without increasing intracellular calcium levels (thus avoiding arrhythmias and increasing MVO2 minimally). It also opens ATP-sensitive potassium channels (KATP) in vascular smooth muscle, causing arterial and venous vasodilation. [2]
- **Indications:** Acute decompensated heart failure in patients with low cardiac output (approved in many countries, not in the US). It has a long-acting active metabolite with a half-life of 80 hours, providing sustained hemodynamic benefit. [2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Hypotension, headache, atrial fibrillation. [2]

## Part XII: Vasopressors and Inotropes in Shock

### 12.1 Catecholamines

- **Norepinephrine:** A potent alpha-1 agonist with moderate beta-1 activity and minimal beta-2 activity. It is the first-line vasopressor for most forms of shock, including septic shock and cardiogenic shock. It increases mean arterial pressure by increasing systemic vascular resistance, with a modest increase in contractility. [1,2,4]
- **Epinephrine:** A potent agonist at alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 receptors. At low doses, beta effects predominate (increased heart rate and contractility, vasodilation). At high doses, alpha-mediated vasoconstriction is dominant. It is the drug of choice in anaphylactic shock and cardiac arrest. Used as a second-line agent in septic and cardiogenic shock. [1,2,6]
- **Phenylephrine:** A pure alpha-1 agonist. It causes vasoconstriction and increases blood pressure without any inotropic or chronotropic effect (reflex bradycardia may occur). It is used in hypotension with adequate cardiac output (e.g., anesthesia-induced vasodilation, neurogenic shock) and in patients with atrial fibrillation and rapid ventricular response where tachycardia is undesirable. [1,2]
- **Vasopressin:** Acts on V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle to cause vasoconstriction. It is used as an adjunct to norepinephrine in septic shock to reduce the required norepinephrine dose and improve outcomes. [1,2,4]
- **Angiotensin II:** Synthetic human angiotensin II. It is a potent direct vasoconstrictor, acting via AT1 receptors. Approved as an additional vasopressor in catecholamine-refractory vasodilatory shock. [2]

## Part XIII: Pulmonary Hypertension-Specific Therapy

### 13.1 Endothelin Receptor Antagonists (ERAs)

- **Drugs:** Bosentan, Ambrisentan, Macitentan. Block endothelin-1 receptors (ETA and ETB), reducing pulmonary vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling. Used in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) WHO Group 1. [2,4]
- **Key Side Effects:** Hepatotoxicity (bosentan requires monthly LFTs), teratogenicity (requires monthly pregnancy testing and two forms of contraception), edema.

### 13.2 Phosphodiesterase-5 Inhibitors

- **Drugs:** Sildenafil, Tadalafil. Inhibit PDE5 in the pulmonary vasculature, increasing cGMP, causing pulmonary vasodilation. Used in PAH. Contraindicated with nitrates. [2,4]

### 13.3 Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase Stimulators

- **Drug:** Riociguat. Stimulates soluble guanylyl cyclase directly, increasing cGMP independent of NO. Used in PAH and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). [2]

### 13.4 Prostacyclin Analogues and Receptor Agonists

- **Drugs:** Epoprostenol (continuous IV), Treprostinil (IV, SC, inhaled, oral), Iloprost (inhaled), Selexipag (oral, selective IP receptor agonist). These are the most potent pulmonary vasodilators and are used in advanced PAH. [2,4]

## Part XIV: Other Cardiovascular Drugs

### 14.1 Ivabradine

- **Mechanism of Action:** Ivabradine selectively blocks the hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels responsible for the "funny current" (If) in the SA node. The If is the primary current driving spontaneous phase 4 diastolic depolarization, which determines heart rate. By inhibiting If, ivabradine reduces the slope of phase 4 depolarization, thereby slowing the sinus rate. Critically, it has no effect on myocardial contractility, AV conduction, or blood pressure (no negative inotropic or vasodilatory effects). The heart rate reduction is "pure." [1,2]

### Indications:

  - Stable angina in patients with a normal sinus rhythm who are in sinus rhythm with a heart rate ≥70 bpm, who are on a beta-blocker (or cannot tolerate one).
  - Chronic HFrEF (LVEF ≤35%) in patients with sinus rhythm and a heart rate ≥70 bpm despite maximally tolerated beta-blocker (or who cannot tolerate a beta-blocker), based on the SHIFT trial showing a reduction in heart failure hospitalizations. [4,5]
- **Contraindications:** Sick sinus syndrome, sinoatrial block, high-grade AV block, pacemaker dependence, severe hepatic impairment, resting heart rate <60 bpm, and concomitant use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. [1,2]
- **Dosage:** 2.5-7.5 mg orally twice daily, titrated to a goal heart rate of 50-60 bpm. [1,2]
- **Important Side Effects:** Bradycardia, atrial fibrillation (increased risk), visual disturbances (phosphenes, transiently enhanced brightness in a part of the visual field, due to inhibition of HCN channels in the retina, which are similar to the cardiac If channel). [1,2]

### 14.2 Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) Inhibitors

- **Drug Names:** Dapagliflozin, Empagliflozin. [2,4]
- **Mechanism of Action in Heart Failure:** Originally developed as antihyperglycemic agents for type 2 diabetes (inhibiting renal glucose reabsorption), SGLT2 inhibitors were serendipitously found to have profound cardiovascular benefits. The exact mechanism in heart failure is not fully understood and is likely multifactorial: they promote an osmotic diuresis (reducing preload), improve cardiac energetics (shifting metabolism from glucose to ketone bodies, which are a more efficient myocardial fuel), reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and inhibit the cardiac Na-H exchanger (reducing intracellular sodium and calcium), which may reduce arrhythmias and improve contractility. They have a Class I recommendation in the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline for HFrEF, regardless of diabetes status, and a strong recommendation for HFpEF. [4]
- **Indications:** Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), both in patients with and without type 2 diabetes. [4]

## ARBs (Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers):

  - Drug names: Losartan, Valsartan, Candesartan, Irbesartan, Olmesartan, Telmisartan, Azilsartan.
  - Mechanism of action: Selectively block the angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor, preventing the vasoconstrictive and aldosterone-secreting effects of angiotensin II. Unlike ACE inhibitors, they do not inhibit bradykinin degradation. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Identical to ACE inhibitors, primarily used when patients are intolerant of ACE inhibitor-induced cough. Hypertension, heart failure, post-MI, proteinuric CKD. [3,4]
  - Important adverse effects: Hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, hypotension. Angioedema is significantly rarer than with ACE inhibitors but can still occur. Teratogenic; contraindicated in pregnancy. [1,2]

## ARNI (Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor):

  - Drug name: Sacubitril/Valsartan.
  - Mechanism of action: Sacubitril inhibits neprilysin, which degrades natriuretic peptides (BNP, ANP), bradykinin, and adrenomedullin. Increased natriuretic peptides promote natriuresis, diuresis, and vasodilation. Valsartan blocks the AT1 receptor, counteracting the concurrent increase in angiotensin II. [2,4]
  - Key indication: Class I recommendation for ambulatory patients with chronic symptomatic HFrEF (LVEF ≤40%) as a replacement for an ACE inhibitor or ARB, to further reduce morbidity and mortality. [4]
  - Important adverse effects: Hypotension, hyperkalemia, angioedema. Must not be co-administered with an ACE inhibitor; a 36-hour washout period is required when switching to prevent angioedema from dual bradykinin pathway inhibition. [2,4]

### Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs):

  - Drug names: Spironolactone, Eplerenone.
  - Mechanism of action: Block the mineralocorticoid receptor in the distal nephron and cardiovascular tissues, inhibiting the effects of aldosterone on sodium and water retention, potassium excretion, and myocardial fibrosis. [1,2]
  - Key indications: HFrEF (LVEF ≤35%, NYHA Class II-IV), post-MI with LVEF ≤40% and heart failure or diabetes, resistant hypertension. [3,4]
  - Important adverse effects: Hyperkalemia (risk increased with renal impairment, advanced age, and concomitant ACEi/ARB). Spironolactone causes gynecomastia and impotence due to androgen receptor antagonism; eplerenone is selective and lacks these effects. [1,2]

## Loop Diuretics:

  - Drug names: Furosemide, Bumetanide, Torsemide, Ethacrynic acid.
  - Mechanism of action: Inhibit the luminal Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, preventing the generation of a hypertonic medullary interstitium and causing massive excretion of sodium, chloride, potassium, and water. They are the most potent class of diuretics and remain effective in advanced renal failure. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Decompensated heart failure with volume overload, acute pulmonary edema, chronic edema from heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, and hypercalcemia. [4,6]
  - Important adverse effects: Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, ototoxicity (reversible, usually with rapid IV bolus or in renal failure), and pre-renal azotemia from excessive volume depletion. [1,2]

## Thiazide and Thiazide-Like Diuretics:

  - Drug names: Chlorthalidone, Hydrochlorothiazide, Indapamide, Metolazone.
  - Mechanism of action: Inhibit the Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule. They are less potent than loop diuretics but are superior agents for hypertension due to sustained reduction in peripheral vascular resistance. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Essential hypertension (first-line agent), mild edema from heart failure (often in combination with a loop diuretic when sequential nephron blockade is needed). [3,6]
  - Important adverse effects: Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hyponatremia (more common than with loop diuretics, especially in elderly women), hypercalcemia (thiazides increase calcium reabsorption), hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hyperuricemia. [1,2]

## Potassium-Sparing Diuretics:

  - Drug names: Amiloride, Triamterene.
  - Mechanism of action: Directly block the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) on the luminal membrane of the principal cells in the collecting duct, reducing sodium reabsorption and decreasing the electrical gradient for potassium and hydrogen ion secretion. They are weak diuretics when used alone. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Used in combination with thiazide or loop diuretics to counteract hypokalemia. Also used in Liddle syndrome. [2,3]
  - Important adverse effects: Hyperkalemia, especially when used with ACEi/ARBs or in renal failure. [1,2]

## Nitrates:

  - Drug names: Nitroglycerin (sublingual, IV, topical), Isosorbide Dinitrate, Isosorbide Mononitrate.
  - Mechanism of action: Metabolized in vascular smooth muscle to release nitric oxide (NO), which activates guanylyl cyclase, increases cGMP, and causes relaxation. At low doses, venodilation predominates (reducing preload); at higher doses, arterial dilation occurs (reducing afterload). They also dilate epicardial coronary arteries. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Acute angina attack (sublingual nitroglycerin), prophylaxis of stable angina (long-acting oral nitrates), and acute pulmonary edema (IV nitroglycerin). [5]
  - Important adverse effects: Severe headache (cerebral vasodilation), hypotension, reflex tachycardia, flushing. Tolerance develops rapidly with continuous dosing, requiring a nitrate-free interval of 10-12 hours daily. Contraindicated with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil) due to risk of profound hypotension. [1,2]

### Hydralazine:

  - Mechanism of action: Direct arteriolar vasodilator, causing a reduction in systemic vascular resistance. The exact mechanism is unclear but involves inhibition of IP3-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Hypertension (particularly pregnancy-induced), and heart failure in African Americans when combined with isosorbide dinitrate (based on the A-HeFT trial). [4]
  - Important adverse effects: Reflex tachycardia (blocked by co-administration of beta-blockers), headache, sodium and water retention (requiring concomitant diuretic), and a lupus-like syndrome (dose-dependent, more common in slow acetylators; resolves upon discontinuation). [1,2]

### 6. Antiplatelet Agents

### Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid):

  - Mechanism of action: Irreversibly acetylates serine-529 of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) in platelets, inhibiting the synthesis of thromboxane A2 (TXA2), a potent platelet aggregator and vasoconstrictor. The effect lasts for the lifespan of the platelet (7-10 days). [1,2]
  - Key indications: Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), stable ischemic heart disease, secondary prevention of stroke and MI, post-coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). [5]
  - Important adverse effects: Gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding, hypersensitivity reactions (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease), and Reye's syndrome in children. [1,2]

### P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitors:

  - Drug names: Clopidogrel, Prasugrel, Ticagrelor.
  - Mechanism of action: Block the P2Y12 ADP receptor on platelets, inhibiting ADP-mediated amplification of platelet activation and the conformational change of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor. Clopidogrel and prasugrel are irreversible inhibitors (prodrugs requiring hepatic CYP activation); ticagrelor is a direct, reversible antagonist. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Acute coronary syndrome (dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin), post-PCI with stent placement. [5]
  - Important adverse effects: Bleeding. Clopidogrel: efficacy is reduced in CYP2C19 poor metabolizers. Prasugrel: contraindicated in patients with history of stroke/TIA. Ticagrelor: associated with dyspnea (due to adenosine reuptake inhibition) and requires twice-daily dosing. [1,2,5]

## Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa Inhibitors:

  - Drug names: Eptifibatide, Tirofiban, Abciximab.
  - Mechanism of action: Block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation—the cross-linking of platelets by fibrinogen to the activated GP IIb/IIIa receptor. Administered intravenously. [1,2]
  - Key Indication: High-risk PCI or ACS patients, primarily in the catheterization laboratory. [5]
  - Important adverse effects: Major bleeding, thrombocytopenia. [1,2]

## Vitamin K Antagonist:

  - Drug name: Warfarin.
  - Mechanism of action: Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), depleting reduced vitamin K, which is a cofactor for the gamma-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X, and proteins C and S. This results in the synthesis of non-functional clotting factors. Therapeutic effect is monitored by the international normalized ratio (INR). [1,2]
  - Key indications: Stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation (especially valvular AF with mechanical valves or moderate-severe mitral stenosis), deep vein thrombosis (DVT)/pulmonary embolism (PE) treatment, and left ventricular thrombus. [4,5]
  - Important adverse effects: Bleeding (major, including intracranial hemorrhage), warfarin-induced skin necrosis, and numerous drug and food interactions. Teratogenic. [1,2]

## Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs):

  - Drug names: Apixaban, Rivaroxaban, Edoxaban (Factor Xa inhibitors), Dabigatran (Direct Thrombin inhibitor).
  - Mechanism of action: Directly and selectively inhibit their respective targets in the coagulation cascade, preventing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin and thrombus formation. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Stroke prevention in non-valvular atrial fibrillation, treatment and secondary prevention of DVT/PE. [4,5]
  - Important adverse effects: Bleeding (lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage compared to warfarin). Contraindicated in mechanical heart valves and in severe renal impairment. Specific reversal agents exist (e.g., idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet alfa for apixaban and rivaroxaban). [1,2,4]

## Heparins:

  - Drug names: Unfractionated Heparin (UFH), Enoxaparin (Low Molecular Weight Heparin, LMWH).
  - Mechanism of action: Bind to antithrombin III, accelerating its inhibition of activated clotting factors, primarily thrombin (Factor IIa) and Factor Xa. UFH inhibits both equally; LMWH preferentially inhibits Factor Xa. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Initial management of ACS, bridging therapy for warfarin, prophylaxis and treatment of DVT/PE. [5,6]
  - Important adverse effects: Bleeding, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), and osteoporosis with long-term use. UFH requires monitoring with aPTT; LMWH typically does not. [1,2]

### 8. Antiarrhythmic Agents

## Class I (Sodium Channel Blockers):

  - Drug names: Flecainide, Propafenone (Class IC), Lidocaine (Class IB).
  - Mechanism of action: Block fast sodium channels, reducing the rate of phase 0 depolarization and slowing conduction velocity. Class IC drugs markedly slow conduction and are used for rhythm control in atrial fibrillation in patients without structural heart disease. Lidocaine is used for ventricular arrhythmias. [1,2]
  - Key Indication: Pharmacologic cardioversion of atrial fibrillation (flecainide, propafenone), suppression of ventricular ectopy/tachycardia. [4,6]
  - Important adverse effects: Proarrhythmia (contraindicated in structural heart disease and coronary artery disease for Class IC), neurological side effects (lidocaine). [1,2]

## Class III (Potassium Channel Blockers):

  - Drug names: Amiodarone, Dronedarone, Sotalol, Dofetilide.
  - Mechanism of action: Prolong the action potential duration and refractory period by blocking potassium channels (IKr, IKs), which delays phase 3 repolarization. Amiodarone is a multi-channel blocker with Class I, II, III, and IV effects. [1,2]
  - Key Indications: Amiodarone is used for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. Dronedarone is used for atrial fibrillation in non-permanent AF without heart failure. [4,6]
  - Important adverse effects: Amiodarone: pulmonary fibrosis, thyroid dysfunction (hyper/hypo), corneal microdeposits, hepatotoxicity, skin discoloration. Dronedarone is contraindicated in severe/recently decompensated heart failure. Sotalol and dofetilide can cause QT prolongation and torsades de pointes. [1,2]

### Adenosine:

  - Mechanism of action: Binds to A1 receptors on the SA and AV nodes, activating potassium channels (IKado), which hyperpolarizes the cell membrane and transiently slows AV nodal conduction and sinus rate. [1,2]
  - Key Indication: Diagnosis and termination of re-entrant supraventricular tachycardias involving the AV node (e.g., AVNRT, AVRT). [6]
  - Important adverse effects: Transient asystole, flushing, chest discomfort, dyspnea (all due to its extremely short half-life, <10 seconds). [1,2]

### Digoxin:

  - Mechanism of action: Inhibits the Na-K-ATPase pump, increasing intracellular sodium, which reduces calcium extrusion via the Na-Ca exchanger, resulting in increased intracellular calcium and positive inotropy. It also enhances vagal (parasympathetic) tone, slowing AV nodal conduction. [1,2]
  - Key Indications: Rate control in permanent atrial fibrillation (particularly with a sedentary lifestyle or concomitant hypotension), and symptomatic HFrEF to reduce hospitalizations (when first-line agents are maximized). [4]
  - Important adverse effects: Narrow therapeutic index (0.5-0.9 ng/mL). Toxicity manifests as cardiac arrhythmias (classically, paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with 2:1 block), anorexia, nausea, visual disturbances (xanthopsia), and confusion. Hypokalemia potentiates toxicity. [1,2]

## HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors (Statins):

  - Drug names: Atorvastatin, Rosuvastatin, Simvastatin, Pravastatin.
  - Mechanism of action: Competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase in the liver, the rate-limiting step in cholesterol synthesis. This upregulates LDL receptors, increasing clearance of LDL cholesterol from the blood. They also have pleiotropic effects including plaque stabilization. [1,2]
  - Key indications: Primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), familial hypercholesterolemia, and acute coronary syndrome. [5,7]
  - Important adverse effects: Myopathy/myalgia (risk increased with high doses and drug interactions), hepatotoxicity (transaminase elevation), and a small increased risk of new-onset diabetes. [1,2]

### Ezetimibe:

  - Mechanism of action: Inhibits the Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1) protein, blocking absorption of dietary and biliary cholesterol in the small intestine. [1,2]
  - Key Indication: Adjunct to statin therapy when additional LDL-lowering is required, or in statin-intolerant patients. [7]
  - Important adverse effects: Well-tolerated; rarely causes myopathy or transaminase elevation. [1,2]

## PCSK9 Inhibitors:

  - Drug names: Alirocumab, Evolocumab, Inclisiran (siRNA).
  - Mechanism of action: Monoclonal antibodies (alirocumab, evolocumab) that bind to proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), preventing its binding to LDL receptors and subsequent receptor degradation. Inclisiran is a small interfering RNA that inhibits PCSK9 synthesis. [1,2]
  - Key Indication: Familial hypercholesterolemia or ASCVD requiring further LDL reduction despite maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe. [7]
  - Important adverse effects: Injection site reactions. [1,2]
