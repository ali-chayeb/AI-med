## First: Definition, Quick Epidemiological Snapshot, and Importance

### Definition

Chest pain is a broad term encompassing any discomfort, pressure, tightness, burning, or pain localized to the anterior thorax between the diaphragm and the clavicles. It is one of the most common and potentially serious symptoms encountered in clinical medicine. The term is deliberately non-specific because the underlying etiologies range from immediately life-threatening conditions (acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism) to benign musculoskeletal disorders. The challenge in evaluating chest pain lies in discriminating the minority of patients with a dangerous underlying cause from the majority with a self-limited or non-cardiac condition. [1,2]

### Quick Epidemiological Snapshot

Chest pain is the second most common presenting complaint in emergency departments, accounting for approximately 6-8% of all visits (over 8 million visits annually in the United States). Globally, the lifetime prevalence of non-cardiac chest pain in the general population is estimated to be 13-33%, while angina pectoris has a prevalence of 3-5% in adults over 40. The incidence of acute myocardial infarction in patients presenting to the emergency department with chest pain is approximately 5-15%. The remaining 85-95% of patients have non-cardiac causes or non-ischemic cardiac causes. [1,2]

The importance of chest pain as a symptom cannot be overstated. It is the cardinal symptom of coronary artery disease, the leading cause of death worldwide. A missed diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome carries a 30-day mortality of 1.8% for patients inappropriately discharged from the emergency department, approximately double the mortality of those who are admitted. Furthermore, chest pain is a major driver of healthcare utilization, and the fear of litigation for missed myocardial infarction contributes to high rates of hospital admission for low-risk patients. [1,2]

### System Affiliation and Related Symptoms

Chest pain primarily implicates the cardiovascular system, but it can arise from virtually any structure in the thorax or upper abdomen. The systems and their associated symptoms are: [1,2]

- **Cardiovascular:** Palpitations, dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, syncope, presyncope, hemoptysis, diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, and a sense of impending doom.
- **Respiratory:** Cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, dyspnea, pleuritic pain (worsened by inspiration), wheezing.
- **Gastrointestinal (Upper GI):** Heartburn, regurgitation, dysphagia, odynophagia, water brash, epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting.
- **Musculoskeletal:** Pain reproducible with palpation, specific movements, or changes in posture. Localized tenderness.
- **Psychiatric:** Panic attacks with a sense of choking, paresthesias, depersonalization, derealization, intense fear, and palpitations.
- **Dermatological:** Visible vesicular rash in a unilateral dermatomal distribution (herpes zoster).

## Second: Pathogenesis - Pathophysiology

The mechanism of chest pain depends entirely on the specific etiology. However, the fundamental pathophysiological process underlying the most critical diagnosis—myocardial ischemia—is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. [1,3]

**Cardiac Ischemic Pain (Angina Pectoris):**

The heart is an obligate aerobic organ with a very high resting oxygen extraction ratio (70-80%). When myocardial oxygen demand exceeds the capacity of the coronary circulation to supply oxygen, the myocardium shifts to anaerobic metabolism. The accumulation of metabolites—lactate, adenosine, bradykinin, serotonin, and potassium ions—stimulates chemosensitive and mechanosensitive nociceptors in the ventricular myocardium. These nociceptive signals are transmitted via cardiac sympathetic afferent fibers that travel through the cervical and upper thoracic sympathetic ganglia and enter the spinal cord at the T1-T4/T5 dorsal horn. Convergence of these visceral afferent fibers with somatic afferent fibers from the chest wall, neck, and arms in the same spinal segments accounts for the phenomenon of referred pain. The pain is typically dull, aching, or pressure-like ("visceral" quality), located substantially in the retrosternal area, and radiates to the left arm (ulnar aspect), right arm, neck, jaw, back, or epigastrium. [1,3]

**Aortic Dissection Pain:**

A tear in the aortic intima allows high-pressure blood to enter the aortic media, creating a false lumen. The propagating hematoma strips the medial layers, causing intense, severe, "tearing" or "ripping" pain. The pain is maximal at onset, unlike the crescendo nature of ischemic pain. As the dissection propagates, the pain migrates along the path of the aorta (e.g., from the anterior chest to the interscapular back as a Type A dissection extends into the descending aorta). [1,4]

**Pericarditic Pain:**

Inflammation of the pericardium stimulates nociceptive afferents on the pericardial surface. The pain is sharp, pleuritic (worsened by inspiration), and positional (worse when lying flat, relieved by sitting up and leaning forward). The pericardium is innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3-C5), which can refer pain to the trapezius ridge. [1,4]

**Pulmonary Embolism Pain:**

The pain of pulmonary embolism is pleuritic, caused by infarction of the distal pulmonary parenchyma supplied by an occluded pulmonary artery branch, which produces local inflammation extending to the visceral pleura. A massive central embolus can cause substernal angina-like pain from acute right ventricular distension and strain. [1,2]

**Gastroesophageal Reflux Pain:**

Acid reflux into the esophagus causes chemical irritation and stimulation of esophageal chemoreceptors. The esophagus shares embryological origin and sensory innervation with the heart, and the pain can be indistinguishable from cardiac pain. It is often burning, related to meals and recumbency, and relieved by antacids. [1,2]

**Musculoskeletal Pain:**

Direct irritation or injury to the muscles, ribs, costochondral junctions, or intercostal nerves produces somatic pain. Somatic pain is well-localized, sharp, and reproducible with palpation or movement, unlike visceral pain, which is poorly localized and deep. [1,2]

## Third: Diagnostic Framework - From Symptom to Diagnosis

### Introduction to the Approach

Chest pain is the paradigm of a symptom that must be approached with a dual-processing diagnostic strategy. The first and most urgent task is to identify or exclude the immediately life-threatening causes: acute coronary syndrome (ACS), aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, esophageal rupture, and cardiac tamponade. This is a Pattern Recognition task using the history, ECG, and targeted physical examination. The second, slower task is to differentiate among the remaining common, less dangerous causes using an Analytical/Hypothetico-Deductive approach. [1,2]

### Step 1: Immediate Assessment and "Red Flag" Triage

Within the first moments of encountering a patient with chest pain, the clinician must assess for hemodynamic stability (level of consciousness, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation) and look for clinical features that immediately point to a life-threatening cause. [1,2]

### Step 2: The Full List of Differential Diagnoses

The differential diagnosis is organized into two critical sub-lists: Dangerous (must not miss) and Common (often benign, but can mimic dangerous causes).

### Dangerous (Life-Threatening) Differential Diagnoses

### Acute Coronary Syndrome (Unstable Angina, NSTEMI, STEMI):

  - **Typical Presentation:** Retrosternal chest pressure, heaviness, or squeezing (not sharp or stabbing) brought on by exertion or at rest, lasting >20 minutes. May radiate to the left arm, right arm, both arms, neck, jaw, or epigastrium. Associated with dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, and a sense of impending doom. In elderly, female, and diabetic patients, the presentation may be "atypical" with dyspnea as the sole symptom, or epigastric discomfort, fatigue, or syncope.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Can you describe the quality of the pain in your own words?" (Ischemic pain is visceral: pressure, tightness, heaviness, squeezing, aching. It is not sharp, stabbing, or pleuritic.)
    - "What were you doing when the pain started?" (ACS pain occurs at rest, or with minimal exertion; stable angina is predictably exertional.)
    - "Has this pain occurred before, and if so, what has changed?" (Crescendo angina: increasing frequency, duration, or severity of previously stable angina.)
    - "Does the pain radiate anywhere?" (Radiation to both arms, the left arm, the neck, or the jaw increases the likelihood of ACS.)
    - "Do you have associated shortness of breath, sweating, or nausea?" (The presence of diaphoresis significantly increases the likelihood of MI.)
    - "Do you have a history of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, or a family history of early heart disease?" (Assess cardiovascular risk factors.)

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Inspection:** Diaphoresis, pallor, anxious appearance, Levine sign (clenched fist held over the sternum).
    - **Palpation:** Pulse may be irregular (arrhythmia). The skin is cool and clammy.
    - **Auscultation:** An S4 gallop (reduced ventricular compliance from ischemia). A new holosystolic murmur (papillary muscle dysfunction from inferior MI causing acute mitral regurgitation, or ventricular septal rupture). Lung crackles (pulmonary edema from pump failure). [1,2]

### Acute Aortic Dissection:

  - **Typical Presentation:** Sudden, severe, "tearing" or "ripping" pain, maximal at onset, often radiating to the back between the scapulae. The pain may migrate as the dissection propagates. It is more common in patients with hypertension, Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, bicuspid aortic valve, or aortitis.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Was the pain maximal at the very beginning, or did it build up gradually?" (Pain maximal at onset is a key discriminator from ACS pain, which builds over minutes.)
    - "Can you describe the quality of the pain?" (A tearing or ripping quality, though not always present, is highly suggestive.)
    - "Does the pain move anywhere?" (Migratory pain following the path of the aorta is highly specific.)
    - "Do you have high blood pressure, or are you known to have an aneurysm or a bicuspid aortic valve?"

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Palpation:** Pulse deficit or blood pressure asymmetry (>20 mmHg difference between the right and left arms, or a new pulse deficit in a femoral or carotid artery).
    - **Auscultation:** A new diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation (dissection extending into the aortic root).
    - **Neurological:** Focal neurological deficit (dissection extending into the carotid artery causing stroke). [1,2,4]

### Pulmonary Embolism (PE):

  - **Typical Presentation:** Acute onset of pleuritic chest pain (sharp, worse with breathing), dyspnea, and tachypnea. With a massive PE, there may be substernal angina-like pain, syncope, and signs of acute right heart failure (elevated JVP, parasternal heave).

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Is the pain worse when you take a deep breath?" (Pleuritic quality is typical for a small-to-medium PE causing pulmonary infarction.)
    - "Did you have sudden shortness of breath?" (Dyspnea is the most sensitive symptom.)
    - "Have you had any recent surgery, prolonged travel, or a period of immobility?" (Assess for risk factors for venous thromboembolism.)
    - "Have you ever had a blood clot in your leg or lung before?"
    - "Have you coughed up any blood?" (Hemoptysis occurs with pulmonary infarction.)

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Inspection:** Tachypnea, accessory muscle use.
    - **Palpation:** Tachycardia.
    - **Auscultation:** Loud P2 (acute pulmonary hypertension), a right ventricular S3 gallop. Lung exam may be normal or reveal a localized pleural rub and crackles over the infarcted area.
    - **Extremities:** Unilateral leg swelling, warmth, or tenderness (signs of a deep vein thrombosis, the source of the embolus). [1,2]

### Tension Pneumothorax:

  - **Typical Presentation:** Acute onset of unilateral, pleuritic chest pain and severe dyspnea, progressing to tracheal deviation (away from the affected side), distended neck veins, and hypotension. Often occurs in tall, thin young males (spontaneous), or after trauma or mechanical ventilation.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Was the onset of pain and shortness of breath very sudden?"
    - "Do you have a history of lung disease (COPD, asthma) or recent chest trauma?"

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Inspection:** Tracheal deviation, unilateral absent chest wall motion.
    - **Palpation:** Trachea deviated away from the affected side. No tactile fremitus.
    - **Percussion:** Hyper-resonant on the affected side.
    - **Auscultation:** Absent breath sounds on the affected side. [1,2]

### Esophageal Rupture (Boerhaave Syndrome):

  - **Typical Presentation:** Severe, sudden retrosternal or epigastric pain, often after forceful vomiting or retching. Subcutaneous emphysema (air in the tissues of the neck and chest wall) is pathognomonic.
  - **Key Questions to Ask:** "Did the pain start immediately after a forceful episode of vomiting or retching?"

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Palpation:** Subcutaneous crepitus (air) in the suprasternal notch and neck.
    - **Auscultation:** A mediastinal crunch (Hamman's sign: a crunching, rasping sound synchronous with the heartbeat, caused by air in the mediastinum). [1,2]

### Common (Often Benign, or Non-Lifethreatening) Differential Diagnoses

### Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) / Esophageal Spasm:

  - **Typical Presentation:** Burning retrosternal pain (heartburn), often after meals, when lying flat, or during bending. It may be relieved by antacids. Esophageal spasm can cause intense, squeezing pain indistinguishable from ACS.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Is the pain burning in quality?" (Heartburn.)
    - "Is the pain related to meals or to lying down?" (Reflux is exacerbated by recumbency and food.)
    - "Have you tried antacids, and did they help?" (A positive response to antacids supports the diagnosis, but does not rule out cardiac pain.)
    - "Do you have a sensation of acid or food coming up into your throat?" (Regurgitation is a specific GERD symptom.)
  - **Key Signs on Physical Examination:** Typically none. The physical exam is normal. [1,2]

### Musculoskeletal Chest Pain (Costochondritis, Chest Wall Pain):

  - **Typical Presentation:** Sharp, localized, well-demarcated pain that is reproducibly exacerbated by specific movements (twisting, bending, lifting) and by direct palpation over the affected costochondral junction or muscle.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Can you point to the exact spot where it hurts with one finger?" (Localized, somatic pain can often be precisely localized.)
    - "Is the pain worse with movement, twisting, or deep breathing?" (Suggestive of a musculoskeletal origin.)
    - "Have you done any recent physical activity or heavy lifting that could have strained a muscle?"

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Palpation:** The single most important examination maneuver. Pain is precisely and completely reproduced by firm palpation over the affected costochondral junction, intercostal muscle, or rib. This is the "reproducibility sign." [1,2]

### Pericarditis:

  - **Typical Presentation:** Sharp, pleuritic, retrosternal or left precordial pain, worse with lying flat and inspiration, and significantly relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. The pain may radiate to the trapezius ridge.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Is the pain worse when you take a deep breath and when you lie flat?" (The two most discriminating features of pericarditic pain.)
    - "Does leaning forward relieve the pain?" (A pathognomonic feature.)

### Key Signs on Physical Examination:

    - **Auscultation:** A pericardial friction rub, a scratchy, superficial, systolic-diastolic sound heard best at the left lower sternal border with the diaphragm, with the patient leaning forward in held expiration. It is pathognomonic but may be fleeting. [1,2,4]

### Panic Attack / Anxiety:

  - **Typical Presentation:** Acute onset of chest tightness or palpitations, accompanied by a sense of choking, paresthesias (tingling around the mouth and fingertips), depersonalization, derealization, and an intense fear of dying or losing control. Often occurs in a young patient with no cardiac risk factors.

### Key Questions to Ask:

    - "Did you feel a sudden rush of intense fear or a sense of impending doom?"
    - "Did you have tingling around your mouth or in your fingers?"
    - "Have you ever had an episode like this before?"
  - **Key Signs on Physical Examination:** Tachycardia, tachypnea. The examination is otherwise normal. [1,2]

### Herpes Zoster (Shingles):

  - **Typical Presentation:** Burning, tingling, or sharp pain in a unilateral dermatomal band on the chest wall, preceding the appearance of the characteristic vesicular rash by 1-3 days. The pain may be mistaken for cardiac pain before the rash appears.
  - **Key Questions to Ask:** "Do you have any tingling or burning sensation on your skin before the pain started?"
  - **Key Signs on Physical Examination:** A unilateral, dermatomal, maculopapular or vesicular rash that does not cross the midline. [1,2]

### Step 3: History - The Critical First Moments

The history is the single most powerful diagnostic tool. In the evaluation of chest pain, five key descriptors of the pain have the highest discriminatory value for acute myocardial ischemia: [1,2,5]

- **Quality:** Visceral descriptors (pressure, tightness, heaviness, squeezing, aching) increase the likelihood of ischemia. Sharp, stabbing, pleuritic, or burning descriptors make ischemia less likely but do not rule it out.
- **Location:** Retrosternal or left precordial location is most typical. Radiation to the left arm (ulnar aspect), right arm, both arms, neck, or jaw significantly increases the likelihood of ACS. Epigastric pain, particularly in older or diabetic patients, can be an anginal equivalent.
- **Provocation and Palliation:** Ischemic pain is provoked by exertion or emotional stress and is relieved by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin. Pain that is provoked by inspiration, palpation, movement, or eating is more likely non-ischemic.
- **Timing and Duration:** Pain from stable angina lasts 2-10 minutes and is predictably exertional. Pain from ACS is more prolonged (>20 minutes), occurs at rest, or represents a significant change in a previously stable anginal pattern. Pain that is maximal at onset ("tearing") strongly suggests aortic dissection.
- **Associated Symptoms:** Diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, vomiting, and a sense of impending doom are strong independent predictors of acute myocardial infarction. The presence of three or more of these symptoms with typical chest pain makes ACS highly probable.

### Step 4: Physical Examination - Targeted and Sequential

The physical examination of a patient with chest pain is directed at identifying signs of the specific life-threatening causes and assessing hemodynamic stability. [1,2]

### Vital Signs and General Appearance:

  - **Tachycardia or Bradycardia:** Arrhythmia.
  - **Hypotension:** Cardiogenic shock (massive MI, tamponade), tension pneumothorax, or massive PE.
  - **Hypertension:** Aortic dissection, hypertensive emergency.
  - **Tachypnea and Hypoxia:** PE, tension pneumothorax, acute pulmonary edema.
  - **Diaphoresis, Pallor, Distress:** ACS.

### Vascular Examination:

  - **Blood Pressure in Both Arms:** A difference of >15-20 mmHg systolic pressure between the arms is a specific sign of aortic dissection (subclavian artery involvement) or severe subclavian stenosis.
  - **Pulse Assessment:** Assess all peripheral pulses. A pulse deficit (asymmetric or absent pulse) strongly suggests aortic dissection. Pulsus paradoxus suggests cardiac tamponade.

### Neck Examination:

  - **Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP):** Elevated JVP with clear lung fields suggests right ventricular infarction, cardiac tamponade, or massive PE. Elevated JVP with crackles suggests biventricular failure from a large MI. Kussmaul's sign (a rise in JVP with inspiration) suggests constrictive pericarditis or right ventricular infarction.

### Chest Wall Examination:

  - **Palpation:** Systematic palpation of the sternum, costochondral junctions, and intercostal muscles. If the patient's pain is exactly and completely reproduced by palpation, and there are no other high-risk features, a musculoskeletal cause is highly probable (positive likelihood ratio for non-cardiac pain ~2.5-3.0). [6]

### Cardiac Auscultation:

  - **A New Murmur:** A new, loud, holosystolic murmur at the apex or left lower sternal border in the setting of an acute MI raises the alarm for a mechanical complication: acute mitral regurgitation (from papillary muscle rupture) or ventricular septal rupture. Both are surgical emergencies.
  - **S3 or S4 Gallop:** An S3 indicates acute pump failure with elevated filling pressures. An S4 indicates a stiff, ischemic ventricle.
  - **Pericardial Friction Rub:** Acute pericarditis.
  - **Muffled Heart Sounds:** Cardiac tamponade.

### Lung Auscultation:

  - **Crackles:** Cardiogenic pulmonary edema from pump failure.
  - **Unilateral Absent Breath Sounds:** Pneumothorax (with hyper-resonance) or large pleural effusion (with dullness).
  - **Pleural Rub:** Pulmonary embolism with infarction, or pleurisy.

### Extremity Examination:

  - **Unilateral Leg Swelling:** A key finding pointing toward DVT and PE.

### Step 5: Investigations and Their Expected Findings

The investigations are ordered based on the leading differential after the initial history and physical exam.

### 12-Lead Electrocardiogram (ECG):

  - **Why Order It:** It is the single most important test for suspected ACS and should be obtained within 10 minutes of presentation.

### Expected Findings and What They Diagnose:

    - **STEMI:** ST-segment elevation ≥1 mm in two or more contiguous limb leads, or ≥2 mm in two contiguous precordial leads (V2-V3). New left bundle branch block.
    - **NSTEMI/Unstable Angina:** ST-segment depression ≥0.5 mm, T-wave inversion (new and deep), or transient ST-segment elevation. A completely normal ECG does not exclude unstable angina or NSTEMI.
    - **Pericarditis:** Diffuse, concave-upward ST-segment elevation in most leads (except aVR and V1, which show reciprocal ST depression), with PR-segment depression.
    - **Pulmonary Embolism (Massive):** S1Q3T3 pattern (deep S in lead I, Q wave and T inversion in lead III), right axis deviation, right bundle branch block. These signs are specific but insensitive.
  - **Sensitivity and Specificity:** The initial ECG is diagnostic in only 30-50% of acute MIs. Serial ECGs increase the sensitivity to >95%. [1,2]

### High-Sensitivity Cardiac Troponin (hs-cTn):

  - **Why Order It:** To detect myocardial necrosis. This is the gold standard biomarker for the diagnosis of acute MI.
  - **Expected Findings:** A rise and/or fall in hs-cTn with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit is required for the diagnosis of MI. In stable angina, troponin is normal.
  - **Sensitivity and Specificity:** Using the 0-hour/1-hour or 0-hour/2-hour rapid rule-out protocols, high-sensitivity troponin assays have a negative predictive value for acute MI of >99% in low-risk patients. A dynamic change (a "delta") in troponin over 1-2 hours distinguishes acute injury (dynamic rise/fall) from chronic injury (stable elevation). [1,2]

### Chest Radiograph (CXR):

  - **Why Order It:** To evaluate for alternative life-threatening causes.

### Expected Findings and What They Diagnose:

    - **Pneumothorax:** Visceral pleural line with absent lung markings peripherally.
    - **Pneumomediastinum/Aortic Dissection:** Widened mediastinum (>8 cm on AP view). An abnormal mediastinal contour is present in 60-90% of aortic dissections, but a normal CXR does NOT exclude dissection.
    - **Heart Failure:** Cardiomegaly, pulmonary vascular cephalization, Kerley B lines, alveolar edema, pleural effusions.
    - **Pneumonia:** Lobar consolidation.
  - **Sensitivity and Specificity:** For aortic dissection, CXR is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule the disease out. [1,2]

### D-Dimer:

  - **Why Order It:** To evaluate for pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection when pre-test probability is low-to-intermediate.
  - **Expected Findings:** A negative D-dimer (below the age-adjusted threshold) has a very high negative predictive value for PE and effectively rules it out in low-risk patients. A positive D-dimer is non-specific and requires further imaging.
  - **Sensitivity and Specificity:** Sensitivity for PE is >95%, but specificity is very low (30-50%). [1,2]

### Definitive Imaging (for specific dangerous diagnoses):

  - **Coronary Angiography:** The gold standard for diagnosing obstructive coronary artery disease in high-risk ACS.
  - **CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA):** The gold standard for diagnosing PE. High sensitivity (94-99%) and specificity (97-98%).
  - **CT Aortography (CTA):** The gold standard for diagnosing aortic dissection. Sensitivity and specificity approach 100%. Performed in patients with high clinical suspicion.
  - **Echocardiography:** The gold standard for detecting a pericardial effusion, right ventricular strain (suggestive of PE), and regional wall motion abnormalities (suggestive of acute MI). It is also the test of choice for diagnosing mechanical complications of MI (acute MR, VSR). [1,2]

### Step 6: Diagnostic Algorithms and Special Approaches

The evaluation of acute chest pain follows a structured, algorithmic approach based on the initial ECG and troponin results. The 2025 ACC/AHA guidelines emphasize the use of clinical decision pathways. [2]

### The "Rule-Out" Pathway for ACS:

- **Initial Triage:** 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes.
- **If STEMI is present:** Activate the cardiac catheterization laboratory for primary PCI.
- **If Non-ST Elevation ACS is suspected:** Use a validated risk score (HEART Score, TIMI Risk Score) and a high-sensitivity troponin assay.
- **HEART Score:** A score of 0-3 is low risk (1.6% risk of MACE at 6 weeks), and these patients may be candidates for early discharge or observation without immediate invasive investigation. A score of 4-6 is moderate risk, and a score of 7-10 is high risk, warranting admission and inpatient coronary angiography. The HEART Score includes five variables: History, ECG, Age, Risk factors, and Troponin. [2]
- **The 0h/1h or 0h/2h hs-cTn Algorithm:** This is the core of the rapid rule-out protocol. If hs-cTn is very low at presentation and there is no significant change on repeat testing after 1 or 2 hours, the patient can be "ruled out" for acute MI with a very high negative predictive value. [2]

## Fourth: Red Flags

The following symptoms, signs, and clinical patterns are absolute "red flags" that demand immediate action and must never be missed or dismissed. [1,2]

### Symptoms That Indicate a Life-Threatening Condition

- **Severe, tearing/ripping chest pain, maximal at onset, radiating to the back.** (Acute Aortic Dissection until proven otherwise.)
- **Chest pain with syncope.** (Massive PE, aortic dissection, critical aortic stenosis, or ventricular arrhythmia.)
- **Chest pain with acute, severe dyspnea, hemoptysis, and unilateral leg swelling.** (Massive PE.)
- **Sudden onset of pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea in a tall, thin young male, or after trauma.** (Tension pneumothorax.)
- **Severe retrosternal pain after forceful vomiting.** (Esophageal rupture.)
- **The "anginal equivalent" triad in elderly or diabetic patients:** Sudden onset of dyspnea, epigastric pain, and diaphoresis with minimal or no chest pain. This is a myocardial infarction until proven otherwise.

### Signs That Indicate a Life-Threatening Condition

- **A difference in systolic blood pressure of >20 mmHg between the two arms, or a new pulse deficit.** (Aortic dissection.)
- **Tracheal deviation, distended neck veins, and absent breath sounds on one side.** (Tension pneumothorax – requires immediate needle decompression, not imaging.)
- **A new, loud holosystolic murmur with acute pulmonary edema and shock following an MI.** (Papillary muscle rupture or ventricular septal rupture – surgical emergencies.)
- **Beck's Triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, elevated JVP) and pulsus paradoxus >12 mmHg.** (Cardiac tamponade.)
- **Subcutaneous emphysema (air in the tissues of the neck and chest wall) on palpation.** (Esophageal rupture or tension pneumothorax.)

### "Don't Miss" Matters

- **Pericarditis with Fever and Recent Cardiac Surgery or Procedure:** Raise suspicion for post-cardiotomy syndrome or purulent pericarditis.
- **Recurrent Pleuritic Chest Pain with Fever and a History of Pericarditis:** Consider Dressler syndrome (post-MI syndrome) or a pericardial effusion with impending tamponade.
- **Chest Pain in a Patient with Known Cancer:** Always consider malignant pericardial effusion and pulmonary embolism.
- **A single normal troponin or a non-diagnostic ECG never rules out an acute coronary syndrome.** The diagnosis requires a combination of history, serial ECGs, and serial troponin assays. Discharging a patient with "chest pain of unknown origin" without an objective rule-out pathway is a leading cause of malpractice claims. [1,2]

## Fifth: Guidelines for Approaching This Symptom

The evaluation of acute chest pain is standardized by major societal guidelines.

- **The 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes (ACS):** Provide comprehensive algorithms for the evaluation of acute chest pain, incorporating high-sensitivity troponin assays and risk stratification scores. They recommend immediate invasive strategy (<2 hours) for very high-risk NSTEMI patients and an early invasive strategy (<24 hours) for high-risk patients. [7]
- **The 2025 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ASPC/NLA/PCNA Guideline for the Management of Patients With Chronic Coronary Disease:** This guideline emphasizes the pre-test probability of obstructive CAD as the foundation for deciding on diagnostic testing for patients with stable chest pain. In patients with a low pre-test probability, the guideline recommends against routine stress testing or coronary CTA, instead prioritizing risk factor modification and a shared decision-making process. For patients with an intermediate-high pre-test probability, coronary CTA or stress imaging is recommended. Invasive coronary angiography is reserved for those with a high pre-test probability and refractory symptoms, or for those with high-risk features on non-invasive testing. [2]

The key principles from these guidelines are:

- **Risk Stratify First:** The first diagnostic decision is not "Is this cardiac?" but "Is this a high-risk acute coronary syndrome?" This is determined by the ECG, hemodynamic stability, and the presence of high-risk features in the history.
- **Use Evidence-Based Pathways:** Clinicians must use a validated clinical decision pathway (e.g., the HEART Score with a high-sensitivity troponin assay) to guide triage decisions.
- **Coronary CTA is a First-Line Test for Stable Pain:** For patients with stable chest pain and an intermediate pre-test probability of obstructive CAD, coronary CT angiography is a first-line diagnostic test and has excellent negative predictive value.
- **A Normal Test is Not Always a Benign Test:** A patient with persistent, typical angina and a normal stress test or coronary CTA requires further investigation for non-obstructive coronary causes (coronary microvascular dysfunction or vasospastic angina). [2,7]
