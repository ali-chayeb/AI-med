## Typical Disease Story / Model Case History

A 58-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes, and a 40-pack-year smoking history presents to the emergency department at 3:00 AM. He describes being woken from sleep by severe, crushing, retrosternal chest pressure, which he rates 10/10 in intensity. The pain radiates to his left shoulder and jaw. It is not relieved by rest or antacids. He is diaphoretic, nauseated, and vomits once. On examination, he is pale, clammy, and in visible distress, clutching his chest. His heart rate is 104 bpm, blood pressure is 150/95 mmHg, respiratory rate is 22/min, and oxygen saturation is 94% on room air. Heart sounds are normal with no murmurs or rubs, but an S4 gallop is auscultated. The lungs are clear. The 12-lead ECG shows 4 mm ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL. This is an inferior STEMI.[1]

### Pathogenesis

The vast majority of STEMIs are caused by the rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque in a coronary artery. A thin-cap fibroatheroma, rich in a lipid-laden necrotic core and infiltrated by inflammatory macrophages, fissures. The exposed subendothelial collagen and tissue factor trigger platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation, and activate the coagulation cascade, forming an occlusive, fibrin-rich thrombus. This results in complete, sustained cessation of antegrade coronary blood flow, leading to transmural myocardial ischemia, and if not rapidly reperfused, transmural myocardial necrosis. The pattern of ST elevation on the ECG corresponds to the injured myocardial territory.[1]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls (The "Must-Know" Traps)

- **Missing the Posterior STEMI:** A patient with unexplained hypotension, bradycardia, and chest pain with only ST depression in V1-V3 is having a posterior MI. The error is failing to obtain posterior leads (V7-V9), which will reveal ST elevation. Treat as a STEMI.[1]
- **Calling a STEMI in Left Bundle Branch Block (LBBB) Without Using Sgarbossa Criteria:** Applying Sgarbossa-Smith criteria is mandatory. Concordant ST elevation ≥1 mm in a lead with a positive QRS complex is the most specific criterion. Discordant ST elevation (in a lead with a negative QRS) >5 mm (or >25% of the depth of the preceding S-wave, the modified Smith criterion) is the most sensitive. Failure to use these rules leads to unnecessary and harmful fibrinolysis or catheterization lab activation.[1]
- **Misdiagnosing Pericarditis:** The ST elevation in pericarditis is diffuse, concave-upwards ("saddle-shaped"), and not confined to a coronary artery territory. PR segment depression in multiple leads is a key clue. The error is giving a thrombolytic to a patient with pericarditis, causing hemorrhagic cardiac tamponade.[1]
- **Ignoring the De Winter T-wave Pattern:** A 1–3 mm upsloping ST depression at the J-point in V1-V6, followed by tall, peaked, symmetric T-waves in the precordial leads. This is an anterior STEMI equivalent, representing a proximal LAD occlusion. The standard ECG read may be "non-diagnostic," but this pattern is a STEMI emergency.[2]

### Scores

- **HEART Score:** For risk stratification of undifferentiated chest pain in the emergency department. Age, History, ECG findings, risk factors, and initial Troponin. A score of 0–3 is low risk (1.7% 30-day MACE). A score of 7–10 is high risk (50–65% 30-day MACE).[3]
- **Killip Classification:** A bedside clinical score for the severity of heart failure in STEMI (Killip I–IV), which is a powerful predictor of mortality.

### Correct Detection (The Systematic Approach)

- **The 10-Second, 12-Lead ECG within 10 Minutes of Arrival:** This is the single most important step. The computer interpretation is unreliable; the physician must systematically read it.
- **Look for STEMI Equivalents:** Hyperacute T-waves (tall, broad-based, symmetric), the de Winter pattern, posterior MI with dominant R-wave in V1, and Wellens' syndrome (biphasic or deeply inverted T-waves in V2-V3 with recent chest pain) indicating critical proximal LAD stenosis.[2]
- **Serial Troponin I/T:** The key diagnostic strategy is the 0h/1h or 0h/3h high-sensitivity troponin protocol. A rise and/or fall of troponin >99th percentile upper reference limit is mandatory for the diagnosis of myocardial infarction.[1]

### Emergency Management

According to the 2023 ESC guidelines, the goal is immediate reperfusion.[1]

- **Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI):** The gold standard. Goal: First medical contact (FMC) to balloon inflation time of <90 minutes. A drug-eluting stent is implanted.
- **Fibrinolysis:** If PCI is not available within 120 minutes and symptoms are <12 hours. A fibrin-specific agent (tenecteplase, alteplase) is given. Transfer to a PCI-capable center immediately after.

### Antiplatelet Therapy:

  - Aspirin 150–300 mg chewed (irreversible COX-1 inhibitor, blocks thromboxane A2 production).
  - P2Y12 inhibitor: Ticagrelor 180 mg loading dose (reversible P2Y12 antagonist) or Prasugrel 60 mg loading dose (irreversible P2Y12 antagonist). Clopidogrel is third-line.
- **Anticoagulation:** Unfractionated heparin IV bolus (70–100 U/kg) and infusion, enoxaparin IV, or bivalirudin.
- **Symptomatic Relief:** Sublingual nitroglycerin (if no hypotension, RV infarction, or recent PDE5 inhibitor use), morphine for refractory pain (use with caution, associated with delayed gastric emptying and slower P2Y12 absorption).

## Typical Disease Story

A 72-year-old woman with diabetes and chronic kidney disease presents with a 2-day history of intermittent, burning chest discomfort that she describes as "indigestion." The pain occurs at rest and with minimal exertion. It is less intense than her prior MI 5 years ago but feels "similar." The ECG shows 1.5 mm of horizontal ST depression in V4-V6 with T-wave inversion in leads I and aVL. The initial high-sensitivity troponin T is 150 ng/L (normal <14 ng/L). This is a high-risk NSTEMI.[1]

### Pathogenesis

The same process of plaque disruption as in STEMI, but the thrombus is non-occlusive, flow-limiting, and platelet-rich (white clot). This causes subendocardial ischemia (ST depression) rather than transmural ischemia (ST elevation). Distal microembolization of platelet aggregates and cholesterol crystals into the microcirculation causes microscopic foci of necrosis, which are detected by the high-sensitivity troponin assay.[1]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls

- **False Reassurance from a Normal ECG:** A completely normal ECG does not rule out NSTEMI. Approximately 5–10% of NSTEMIs have a normal initial ECG. The troponin is the definitive test.
- **Misdiagnosing Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy:** An elderly woman after severe emotional stress (death of a spouse) presents with chest pain, ST elevation or deep T-wave inversion, and a mildly elevated troponin. The coronary angiogram is normal, but the left ventriculogram shows apical ballooning with hyperdynamic basal contraction. The error is mislabeling this as an NSTEMI and treating it with long-term DAPT when the condition is usually self-limited and managed with supportive care.[4]
- **Type 2 MI vs. Acute Myocardial Injury:** An elevated troponin in a septic, tachycardic patient with no chest pain is not an NSTEMI. It is a Type 2 MI (supply-demand mismatch) or non-ischemic myocardial injury. The error is subjecting the patient to the bleeding risk of DAPT and an invasive coronary angiogram when the treatment is treating the underlying sepsis.[1]

### Scores

- **GRACE Score (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events):** The definitive risk score for NSTEMI. It uses 8 clinical variables (Age, Heart rate, Systolic BP, Creatinine, Killip class, Cardiac arrest at admission, Elevated cardiac biomarkers, ST segment deviation) to predict in-hospital and 6-month mortality. A GRACE score >140 is high-risk and mandates an early invasive strategy (<24 hours).[1]
- **CRUSADE Score:** Assesses the bleeding risk in NSTEMI patients to guide antithrombotic therapy choices.

### Correct Detection

The standard diagnostic pathway is:

- **12-lead ECG:** Look for ST depression ≥0.5 mm, T-wave inversion, or transient ST elevation. A normal ECG does not rule out NSTEMI.
- **High-Sensitivity Cardiac Troponin (hs-cTn):** The 0h/1h ESC algorithm is the fastest and most accurate. A baseline and 1-hour sample are drawn. The algorithm uses absolute assay-specific thresholds. The diagnosis requires a significant rise and/or fall of hs-cTn above the 99th percentile.[1]

### Emergency Management

- **Antiplatelet Therapy:** Aspirin 150–300 mg chewed. A P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor or prasugrel) is generally given once the coronary anatomy is known and the decision for PCI (not CABG) is made. Routine upstream P2Y12 inhibitor loading before angiography is no longer recommended for most patients.[1]
- **Anticoagulation:** Fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC once daily (safest bleeding profile), or enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC twice daily, or UFH IV.[1]

### Invasive Strategy Selection:

  - **Very High-Risk:** Refractory angina, hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, life-threatening arrhythmias. **Immediate invasive angiography (<2 hours).**
  - **High-Risk:** GRACE score >140, dynamic ST/T-wave changes, confirmed diagnosis. **Early invasive angiography (<24 hours).**(1)

Causes: plaque disruption missed on angiography, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina), spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD, common in young women), and microvascular disease. ### 3. Acute Pulmonary Edema / Acute Decompensated Heart Failure (ADHF)

## Typical Disease Story

A 68-year-old man with known ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 30%) and non-compliance with his furosemide and salt restriction presents in severe respiratory distress. He is sitting bolt upright in bed, unable to speak in full sentences, and is diaphoretic. His respiratory rate is 36/min, heart rate is 130 bpm, blood pressure is 220/130 mmHg, and SpO₂ is 78% on room air. On auscultation, there are coarse, bubbling, diffuse crackles throughout both lung fields. He has an elevated JVP with positive hepatojugular reflux, and significant bilateral pitting edema of the lower extremities up to the knees. He is coughing up frothy, pink-tinged sputum.[5]

### Pathogenesis

This is acute left ventricular systolic and/or diastolic failure causing a sudden, profound increase in left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), which is transmitted retrogradely to the left atrium and pulmonary veins. When the pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds the plasma oncotic pressure (approximately 25 mmHg), fluid transudes into the pulmonary interstitium, and then floods the alveoli. The resulting ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, intrapulmonary shunt, and stiffening of the lungs from interstitial edema cause catastrophic hypoxemia and the sensation of drowning. The pink, frothy sputum is a protein-rich transudate mixed with air and a small amount of blood from ruptured engorged bronchial veins.[5]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls

- **Misdiagnosing an Acute COPD Exacerbation:** This is the single most common and dangerous error. Both present with severe dyspnea and wheezing ("cardiac asthma"). The key discriminators: a history of heart failure, an S3 gallop (100% specific for HF), elevated JVP, a cold, clammy periphery, and bilateral crackles (not just expiratory wheezing) are all specific for heart failure. A bedside lung ultrasound showing B-lines in an anterior lung zone in a patient with a high pre-test probability is diagnostic of pulmonary edema. Giving a beta-agonist (salbutamol) to a heart failure patient will cause tachycardia and worsen ischemia and hemodynamics. A brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level <100 pg/mL effectively rules out acute HF.[5]
- **Missing a Silent Myocardial Infarction as the Trigger:** An elderly diabetic patient presenting with acute pulmonary edema without chest pain. The ischemic myocardium is the cause of the acute pump failure, and the ST-segment elevation may be subtle or absent on the initial ECG. A high-sensitivity troponin and a focused echocardiogram are mandatory to rule out an ischemic trigger.[1]
- **Aggressive Fluid Boluses for "Hypotension" in Heart Failure:** A patient with heart failure, low blood pressure (80/50 mmHg), and cold extremities is in cardiogenic shock, not septic shock from hypovolemia. Giving a bolus of saline will further elevate the LVEDP, worsen pulmonary edema, and precipitate respiratory arrest. The physical exam reveals the truth: an elevated JVP and an S3 gallop are signs of a failing, volume-overloaded pump. The correct action is an inotrope (dobutamine) or vasopressor (norepinephrine), not a fluid bolus.[5]

### Correct Detection (The Bedside Approach)

- **History:** Orthopnea (the number of pillows) and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND) are highly specific symptoms for left ventricular failure.
- **Physical Examination:** The critical triad is **elevated JVP, an S3 gallop, and bilateral crackles.** The S3 is a low-frequency, early diastolic sound, best heard with the bell of the stethoscope at the apex with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position. Its presence in a patient with dyspnea has a positive likelihood ratio of >10 for acute heart failure.[5]
- **Bedside Lung Ultrasound:** An anterior zone B-line pattern (≥3 B-lines in a single rib interspace in two lung zones bilaterally) has a sensitivity >90% for acute pulmonary edema. It is performed in 2 minutes and is more sensitive than a chest X-ray.
- **ECG:** Rule out an ischemic trigger (STEMI, NSTEMI, new arrhythmia).
- **BNP or NT-proBNP:** A BNP <100 pg/mL effectively rules out acute heart failure (high negative predictive value).

### Emergency Management

- **Position the Patient:** Sitting fully upright with legs dangling over the side of the bed. This reduces venous return (preload) immediately.
- **Non-Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV) or CPAP:** This is the single most effective intervention to reduce mortality and avoid intubation. It provides positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), which forces fluid out of the alveoli (alveolar recruitment) and reduces preload and afterload. Start immediately at a CPAP of 5–10 cm H₂O and titrate FiO₂ to SpO₂ >92%.[5]

### Vasodilators (Reduce Afterload and Preload):

  - **Sublingual or IV Nitroglycerin:** A 0.4 mg sublingual dose every 5 minutes, or an IV infusion starting at 10–20 mcg/min and rapidly titrated up. Nitroglycerin is a potent venodilator at low doses and a systemic vasodilator at higher doses. The goal is a rapid reduction in blood pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure. It is the first-line pharmacotherapy for hypertensive acute pulmonary edema. Contraindicated in severe aortic stenosis, RV infarction, and recent PDE5 inhibitor use.[5]

### Loop Diuretics (Preload Reduction):

  - **Furosemide 40–80 mg IV bolus** (or double the patient's usual oral dose if chronically on a loop diuretic). The initial effect within minutes is venodilation (prostaglandin-mediated), which reduces preload. The diuretic effect (natriuresis) occurs 30–60 minutes later. The dose is doubled every 30 minutes if no response. A furosemide infusion may be necessary.[5]
- **Inotropes:** For the patient with cardiogenic shock (cold, clammy periphery, low SBP, elevated JVP, S3). Dobutamine (a β1 and β2 agonist) increases cardiac contractility and causes peripheral vasodilation. Milrinone (a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor) is an alternative but can cause significant hypotension.

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (LVEF <45% in the last month of pregnancy or first 5 months postpartum) is a dilated cardiomyopathy triggered by the hormonal milieu and abnormal prolactin cleavage (a 16-kDa prolactin fragment that is toxic to the microvasculature). The treatment is standard heart failure therapy, with ACE-I/ARB replaced by hydralazine and nitrates during pregnancy. |

### 4. Cardiac Tamponade

## Typical Disease Story

A 42-year-old woman with stage IV lung adenocarcinoma presents to the emergency department with progressive, severe dyspnea and dizziness. She cannot lie flat. Her heart rate is 130 bpm, blood pressure is 85/50 mmHg, and her respiratory rate is 30/min. On examination, her JVP is distended to the angle of the jaw and rises further on inspiration (Kussmaul's sign). Her heart sounds are muffled and distant. A pulsus paradoxus of 20 mmHg is measured (a drop in systolic BP >10 mmHg on inspiration). An urgent bedside echocardiogram shows a large circumferential pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of the right ventricle.[6]

### Pathogenesis

Fluid (blood, serous fluid, or pus) accumulates in the pericardial sac. Because the pericardium is relatively inelastic, the accumulation of fluid compresses the cardiac chambers, primarily the thin-walled right atrium and right ventricle. This restricts diastolic filling, causing a reduction in stroke volume and cardiac output. The equalization of diastolic pressures across all four cardiac chambers is the pathognomonic hemodynamic finding. Pulsus paradoxus is the clinical manifestation: during inspiration, the negative intrathoracic pressure increases venous return to the right ventricle, but the constraining pericardial fluid prevents the right ventricle from expanding. The interventricular septum bows into the left ventricle, drastically reducing its filling and stroke volume, causing a fall in systolic blood pressure. In tamponade, the heart is so preload-dependent that anything that reduces venous return (e.g., the positive pressure of mechanical ventilation) causes immediate cardiovascular collapse.[6]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls

- **Misdiagnosing Cardiac Tamponade as a "PEA" Cardiac Arrest:** The patient with tamponade may have a blood pressure and an organized ECG rhythm, but the output is so low that the periphery is pulseless. This is a classic cause of a PEA arrest. The error is following the ACLS algorithm (chest compressions, epinephrine) without performing a bedside cardiac ultrasound, which will instantly reveal the effusion and RV collapse. The only correct treatment is immediate pericardiocentesis.[6]
- **Missing the Diagnosis in a Normotensive Patient:** A patient with a known malignancy, a mildly elevated JVP, and unexplained tachycardia and dyspnea. Early tamponade is a clinical diagnosis, not an echocardiographic one. The echo may show a moderate effusion without diastolic chamber collapse, but the clinical signs of impending tamponade are present. Do not wait for hypotension to develop.
- **Misinterpreting Pulsus Paradoxus:** It is not unique to tamponade. Severe COPD, asthma, and massive pulmonary embolism also cause a significant pulsus paradoxus. In tamponade, the JVP is elevated and Kussmaul's sign is present. In severe asthma/COPD, the JVP is often normal or low, and the Kussmaul sign is absent.[6]

### Correct Detection (Beck's Triad and the Bedside Echo)

- **Beck's Triad (acute surgical tamponade):** Distended neck veins (elevated JVP), muffled heart sounds, and hypotension. This triad is present in only a minority of chronic medical tamponade patients.
- **Pulsus Paradoxus Measurement:** Using a manual sphygmomanometer cuff on the arm. Inflate the cuff above the systolic pressure, then slowly deflate (2–3 mmHg per heartbeat). The first Korotkoff sounds are heard only during expiration (the higher-pressure phase of the respiratory cycle). The point at which sounds become continuous throughout the respiratory cycle is the expiratory systolic pressure. The difference between the expiratory systolic pressure and the pressure at which sounds are first heard is the pulsus paradoxus. A value >10 mmHg is significant and indicates tamponade physiology.[6]
- **POCUS (Point-of-Care Ultrasound):** This is the modern Beck's triad. A subcostal 4-chamber view showing a large pericardial effusion, diastolic collapse of the right ventricle, and a plethoric, non-collapsible inferior vena cava is the definitive bedside diagnosis.[6]

### Emergency Management

- **IV Fluids:** This is a temporizing measure. Give a 500–1000 mL bolus of IV crystalloid to increase the right ventricular filling pressure and overcome the pericardial constraint. This is only a bridge to the definitive procedure.
- **Avoid Positive Pressure Ventilation if Possible:** Intubation and positive pressure ventilation will drastically reduce venous return in a preload-dependent heart and can precipitate immediate PEA arrest. Pericardiocentesis should be performed before intubation if at all possible. If intubation is mandatory, have the pericardiocentesis kit open and the operator ready to puncture at the moment of sedation.[6]
- **Pericardiocentesis:** The definitive life-saving procedure. Under continuous ultrasound or echocardiographic guidance, a long spinal needle is inserted at the subxiphoid angle and directed toward the left shoulder. Aspiration of as little as 50–100 mL of fluid can produce a dramatic increase in cardiac output and blood pressure. A pigtail catheter is left in place for continuous drainage.
- **Open Surgical Pericardial Window:** For recurrent malignant effusions, traumatic hemopericardium, or loculated effusions not amenable to percutaneous drainage.

### Atypical Presentations of Cardiac Tamponade

| Atypical Presentation | Key Feature | Why It Happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-Pressure Tamponade** | A severely hypovolemic trauma patient with a penetrating chest wound has a normal JVP and no distended neck veins but has a large hemopericardium on EFAST and is in shock. | Hypovolemia depletes the venous system, so the neck veins are flat. The EFAST ultrasound is mandatory and reveals the diagnosis. |

The treatment is immediate synchronized cardioversion to restore sinus rhythm and the atrial kick, followed by pericardiocentesis. |

### 5. Acute Aortic Dissection (Stanford Type A)

## Typical Disease Story

A 65-year-old man with a history of poorly controlled hypertension and a known ascending aortic aneurysm (4.8 cm on a prior echo) presents with the sudden, instantaneous onset of the worst chest pain of his life. He describes it as a "tearing" or "ripping" sensation that began in his anterior chest and radiated directly to his interscapular back. His blood pressure is 210/120 mmHg in the right arm, but the left arm blood pressure is 90/50 mmHg. A new, early diastolic, decrescendo murmur of aortic regurgitation is auscultated at the left sternal border. He has a pulse deficit in the left femoral artery. The ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy but no ST elevation.[7]

### Pathogenesis

A primary tear occurs in the intima of the ascending aorta (usually within 10 cm of the aortic valve). The combination of chronic hypertension and a weakened, cystic medial degeneration of the aortic wall causes the intima to rip. The high-pressure systemic arterial blood is driven through this intimal tear, creating a false lumen within the media that propagates antegrade or retrograde along the length of the aorta. The retrograde propagation into the aortic root can involve the aortic valve commissures, causing acute aortic regurgitation. The dissection flap can shear off the ostia of the coronary arteries (causing MI), the carotid arteries (causing stroke), the subclavian arteries (causing pulse deficits and pseudohypotension), the renal arteries (causing AKI), the mesenteric arteries (causing bowel ischemia), and the spinal arteries (causing paraplegia). Rupture into the pericardium causes cardiac tamponade and death.[7]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls (These Kill the Patient)

- **Failing to Check Bilateral Arm Blood Pressures:** This is the single most basic, most neglected, and most unforgivable error. A difference of >20 mmHg in systolic pressure between the arms is a classic sign of subclavian artery involvement and strongly suggests aortic dissection. It takes 30 seconds. Failure to do it leads to a missed diagnosis.
- **Calling It an "Anterior STEMI" and Giving Thrombolytics:** The dissection flap has sheared off the right coronary ostium. The ECG shows an inferior STEMI. The patient receives a thrombolytic, which lyses the protective clot in the false lumen, causing immediate aortic rupture into the pericardium and death. This is a well-documented, catastrophic error. The rule is: always consider the chest X-ray and the patient's overall clinical picture (the tearing pain, pulse deficit, wide mediastinum) before giving a lytic for chest pain.[7]
- **Treating the Hypertension Without Imaging First:** A patient with classic tearing chest pain and a BP of 200/110 mmHg is not a hypertensive urgency. This is an acute aortic syndrome. The hypertension is secondary to the massive catecholamine surge and pain, and it is the only thing keeping the false lumen from fully propagating. Reducing the blood pressure without a CT scan first delays the definitive diagnosis and may miss a proximal dissection that requires immediate surgery.
- **Missing the Diagnosis in a Syncopal Patient with No Chest Pain:** A patient with a proximal Type A dissection can present with a syncopal episode from acute cardiac tamponade or carotid artery occlusion. The patient wakes up and has no pain. The clinician attributes the syncope to a simple vasovagal episode and discharges the patient, who dies hours later from aortic rupture.

### Scores

- **Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS):** A validated clinical decision tool. It uses high-risk conditions (Marfan syndrome, family history of aortic disease, known aortic valve disease, known thoracic aortic aneurysm), high-risk pain features (abrupt onset, severe intensity, tearing or ripping quality), and high-risk physical examination findings (pulse deficit, systolic BP differential >20 mmHg, focal neurological deficit, new aortic insufficiency murmur, hypotension/shock). A score of 0 is low risk. A score of 1 is intermediate risk, and D-dimer testing is recommended. A score of ≥2 is high risk, and the patient proceeds directly to urgent CT aortography.[7]

### Correct Detection

- **Bilateral Arm Blood Pressures and Full Vascular Examination:** Palpate and auscultate all pulses. Look for a pulse deficit. Measure the BP in both arms and document it.
- **Chest X-Ray:** The classic finding is a widened mediastinum (>8 cm at the level of the aortic knob). An abnormal aortic contour, displacement of intimal calcification >1 cm from the outer aortic border, or a left pleural effusion (hemothorax) are also signs. A normal chest X-ray does not rule out dissection.
- **D-dimer:** If the ADD-RS is low or intermediate, a D-dimer can be used. A negative D-dimer (<500 ng/mL) in a patient with an ADD-RS ≤1 effectively rules out aortic dissection with a very high negative predictive value.
- **Definitive Diagnosis: CT Aortography with IV Contrast.** This is the gold standard in the emergency department. It visualizes the intimal flap, the true and false lumens, the entry tear, and the involvement of branch vessels. It must be an ECG-gated, triple-rule-out protocol if pulmonary embolism and ACS are also being considered.[7]

### Emergency Management

The management of acute Type A dissection is time-critical, life-saving surgery. Medical therapy is a bridge to the operating room.

- **Immediate Blood Pressure and Heart Rate Control ("Anti-Impulse Therapy"):** The goal is to reduce the shear stress (dP/dt) on the aortic wall. The target heart rate is <60 bpm, and the target systolic BP is 100–120 mmHg (the lowest blood pressure that perfuses the brain and kidneys).[7]
  - **Esmolol (IV Beta-Blocker, First-Line):** A loading dose of 500 mcg/kg over 1 minute, followed by an infusion of 50–200 mcg/kg/min. Esmolol is a cardioselective beta-1 blocker with an ultra-short half-life, ideal for critical titration. It reduces the force of left ventricular contraction.
  - **Metoprolol or Labetalol IV:** Alternatives if esmolol is not available. Labetalol has both alpha and beta-blocking properties.
  - **After Beta-Blockade is Achieved (HR <60):** Add a vasodilator to control the systolic BP. **Nicardipine or Nitroprusside IV infusion.** Never start a vasodilator before a beta-blocker, because the reflex tachycardia and increased inotropy from the pure afterload reduction will worsen the dissection.
- **Analgesia:** IV Morphine reduces pain and sympathetic drive.
- **Definitive Treatment:Emergency cardiothoracic surgery.** The ascending aorta is resected and replaced with a Dacron graft. The aortic valve is repaired or replaced. The coronary arteries are re-implanted. Without surgery, the mortality of Type A dissection exceeds 1–2% per hour in the first 48 hours, reaching 50–60% in-hospital mortality. With surgery, the mortality is 15–30%.[7]

### Atypical Presentations of Aortic Dissection

| Atypical Presentation | Key Feature | Why It Happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Syncope as the Sole Presenting Symptom** | A 70-year-old man has a syncopal episode in a grocery store. He wakes up feeling fine and has no chest pain. His ECG is normal. He has a known ascending aortic aneurysm. | The dissection has ruptured into the pericardial sac, causing a sudden, self-limited tamponade that caused the syncope. The patient recovered because the pericardial blood clotted and temporarily sealed the rupture. A chest X-ray is obtained, and a widened mediastinum is seen. The CT scan reveals the dissection and contained rupture. This patient is a walking time bomb and must go directly to surgery. |

### 6. Acute Severe Mitral Regurgitation (Papillary Muscle Rupture)

## Typical Disease Story

A 72-year-old woman is on day 3 of a large anterolateral STEMI that was not revascularized due to late presentation. She suddenly develops profound dyspnea, severe pulmonary edema, and hypotension. On auscultation, there is a new, harsh, grade 4/6 pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. However, the murmur is soft, and the cardiac apex is hyperdynamic and displaced. An echocardiogram reveals a flail posterior mitral valve leaflet with a ruptured papillary muscle head and torrential, eccentric mitral regurgitation.[8]

### Pathogenesis

The posteromedial papillary muscle is supplied solely by the posterior descending coronary artery (PDA). In an acute inferior or posterior STEMI, the PDA territory is infarcted. The papillary muscle, an extension of the subendocardial myocardium, necroses and ruptures 2–7 days post-infarction, during the period of maximal macrophage infiltration and collagenase activity. This causes a mechanical catastrophe: the mitral valve leaflet loses its tethering anchor and prolapses into the left atrium during systole, causing acute, torrential, eccentric mitral regurgitation into a normal-sized, non-compliant left atrium. The left atrial pressure increases to very high levels (a giant V-wave), causing flash pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock.[8]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls

- **Failing to Hear the Murmur (The Silent Killer):** The murmur of acute severe MR is often soft or completely absent because the regurgitant orifice is so large that the pressure in the left atrium and left ventricle equalize rapidly, reducing the turbulence that creates the audible murmur. A patient with an acute MI, sudden pulmonary edema, and a soft systolic murmur is having a papillary muscle rupture until proven otherwise. The error is assuming the pulmonary edema is from a large, uncomplicated MI.
- **Calling It a Ventricular Septal Rupture (VSR):** Both present with a new murmur, hypotension, and pulmonary edema on day 3–7 post-MI. The key clinical discriminator is that a VSR has a loud, harsh holosystolic murmur best heard at the left lower sternal border with a parasternal thrill, and the echocardiogram shows a left-to-right shunt with color flow across the interventricular septum. The treatment for VSR is surgical, and the use of an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is beneficial (reduces afterload and the shunt fraction). The error is failing to differentiate them, as the surgical approach is entirely different.[8]
- **Misdiagnosing Acute Severe MR as Sepsis-Associated ARDS:** A patient with flash pulmonary edema and hypotension is intubated for "severe pneumonia." The correct diagnosis is mechanical mitral regurgitation, and the treatment is urgent surgery. The echocardiogram is the diagnostic test.

### Correct Detection

- **Auscultation:** A pansystolic murmur, best heard at the apex, radiating to the axilla and back. The murmur may be decrescendo (rather than the classic holosystolic plateau) because the late systolic pressure gradient equalizes. The S2 is widely split.
- **Transthoracic Echocardiogram (TTE):** The definitive bedside test. The hallmark is a **flail mitral valve leaflet** with the ruptured papillary muscle head attached to the prolapsing leaflet, and a torrential, eccentric, posteriorly or anteriorly directed regurgitant jet. The left atrium is normal-sized (unlike chronic MR). The pulmonary vein Doppler shows systolic flow reversal (a highly specific sign of severe MR).[8]

### Emergency Management

- **Afterload Reduction (Medical Stabilization):** The goal is to reduce the resistance to forward flow, thereby reducing the regurgitant fraction and increasing forward stroke volume.
  - **Nitroprusside IV infusion:** A balanced arteriolar and venous vasodilator. It is the most effective pharmacological agent for acute severe MR. Titrate to a systolic BP of 100–120 mmHg.
  - **Avoid Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP) in Pure Acute MR:** The IABP reduces afterload, but it is not as effective as in VSR, and its main role is as a bridge to surgery. It is not a substitute for urgent surgical consultation.
- **Intubation and Mechanical Ventilation:** For flash pulmonary edema and respiratory failure.
- **Definitive Treatment:** Emergency mitral valve surgery (repair if possible, replacement if not). Without surgery, the mortality is >75% within 24 hours. The timing is immediate.[8]

### 7. Hypertensive Emergency (Hypertensive Crisis with Acute End-Organ Damage)

## Typical Disease Story

A 50-year-old, non-compliant, African American man with a history of chronic hypertension and chronic kidney disease presents with a severe, pounding occipital headache, blurred vision, and confusion. His BP is 280/170 mmHg in both arms. Fundoscopic examination reveals bilateral papilledema, flame-shaped hemorrhages, and cotton-wool spots. He has acute-on-chronic kidney injury (Cr 3.5 mg/dL from a baseline of 2.0). The CT brain shows posterior white matter hypodensities consistent with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES). This is hypertensive encephalopathy.[9]

### Pathogenesis

A sudden, critical, and sustained elevation in systemic blood pressure overwhelms the autoregulatory capacity of the cerebral and renal arterioles. Normally, cerebral blood flow is maintained constant between a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of ~60–150 mmHg. In chronic hypertension, this curve is shifted rightward as a protective adaptation. However, when the MAP exceeds the upper limit of autoregulation (typically >180 mmHg), the arterioles undergo forced vasodilation and become mechanically stretched. This disruption of the blood-brain barrier causes hyperperfusion, leakage of protein-rich plasma into the cerebral interstitium (vasogenic edema), and the clinical syndrome of hypertensive encephalopathy. The same process of fibrinoid necrosis of the afferent renal arterioles causes acute kidney injury, and in the retinal arterioles, it causes hypertensive retinopathy (flame hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, papilledema).[9]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls

- **Overly Rapid Reduction of Blood Pressure:** This is the classic and most harmful mistake. The goal in hypertensive emergency is a controlled, partial reduction of MAP by 20–25% in the first hour, not normalization. Lowering the diastolic BP below 100 mmHg too rapidly can shift the autoregulatory curve below the perfusion threshold of the brain and kidneys, causing an iatrogenic ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, or acute kidney injury.
- **Using Oral Nifedipine Capsules:** The practice of giving an immediate-release nifedipine capsule sublingually or orally to rapidly lower BP is absolutely contraindicated. The unpredictable, profound, and rapid drop in blood pressure causes a reflex sympathetic surge, which can precipitate a myocardial infarction, stroke, or death. This is a dangerous, unscientific practice that has been condemned by the AHA/ACC and the Joint National Committee (JNC).
- **Treating the Blood Pressure Number, Not the Patient:** The term "hypertensive urgency" (severely elevated BP without acute end-organ damage) is a myth. These patients do not need acute blood pressure reduction in the emergency department. They need initiation or adjustment of oral antihypertensives and close outpatient follow-up. Admitting them and giving IV antihypertensives causes harm.

### Correct Detection

The diagnosis is clinical, based on the blood pressure measurement and the evidence of acute, progressive end-organ damage. The specific presentations are:

- **Hypertensive Encephalopathy:** Severe headache, confusion, visual disturbances, seizures, and coma. Fundoscopy shows papilledema. CT/MRI brain shows PRES (posterior leukoencephalopathy syndrome).
- **Acute Hypertensive Cardiogenic Pulmonary Edema:** As described in Section 3. Treat with IV nitroglycerin as the first-line agent.
- **Acute Hypertensive Nephropathy:** Rapid rise in creatinine, proteinuria, and RBC casts.
- **Hypertensive Retinopathy:** Flame hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, and bilateral papilledema.
- **Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA):** The fibrinoid necrosis in the arterioles shears red blood cells, causing schistocytes on the blood smear and a falling hemoglobin.
- **Eclampsia in Pregnancy:** Severe hypertension, proteinuria, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The definitive treatment is delivery of the fetus. IV magnesium sulfate is given for seizure prophylaxis and treatment.

### Emergency Management

The AHA/ACC 2017 guidelines recommend a controlled, monitored reduction of MAP by 20–25% in the first hour, followed by a gradual reduction to 160/100 mmHg over the next 2–6 hours. Excessive, rapid reduction causes organ ischemia. A continuous IV infusion of a short-acting, titratable agent is mandatory, in an ICU setting with an arterial line.[9]

- **Clevidipine:** An ultra-short-acting, IV, dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker. Onset in 2–3 minutes, offset in 5–15 minutes. The ideal agent for precise, titratable control.
- **Nicardipine:** An IV dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker with a slightly longer half-life. Onset in 5–10 minutes. The most widely used agent.
- **Labetalol:** An IV combined alpha and non-selective beta blocker. Ideal for aortic dissection and the hyperadrenergic state. Do not use in acute pulmonary edema.
- **Nitroprusside:** A balanced arterial and venous vasodilator. Onset in seconds, offset in 1–2 minutes. The most potent agent. Toxic metabolite (cyanide and thiocyanate) accumulation limits its use to <24–48 hours and requires monitoring of thiocyanate levels and pH.
- **Fenoldopam:** A selective dopamine-1 receptor agonist. It causes renal and systemic arteriolar vasodilation, making it a favorable agent in patients with acute kidney injury. However, it is rarely used due to its high cost and availability.
- **Esmolol or Metoprolol IV:** As adjuncts to reduce the reflex tachycardia and increased shear stress that occur with pure vasodilators.

## Extended Guide to Cardiovascular Emergencies and Cardiac Drug Management

### Part Three: Additional Critical Cardiac Emergencies

**8. Ventricular Fibrillation (VF) and Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT) Cardiac Arrest**

**Typical Disease Story:**

A 62-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 25%) is on the telemetry ward. The monitor alarm sounds. The rhythm strip shows a chaotic, disorganized waveform with no discernible QRS complexes. The amplitude is coarse, then rapidly degrades to fine VF. The patient is unresponsive, apneic, and has no palpable pulse. This is a witnessed, shockable cardiac arrest.

**Pathogenesis:**

The initiating mechanism is most commonly an acute ischemic event (myocardial infarction) or a scar-based re-entrant circuit around a healed infarct border zone. A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) with an R-on-T phenomenon strikes the vulnerable period of ventricular repolarization, initiating a self-sustaining, multiple-wavelet, chaotic re-entrant rhythm. The ventricles quiver ineffectively at 300–500 times per minute, with no coordinated contraction and no cardiac output. The underlying coronary perfusion pressure drops to zero. The amplitude of the VF waveform (coarse vs. fine) is a direct visual surrogate for the metabolic state of the myocardium. Coarse VF is a recently arrested, oxygenated heart. Fine VF is a dying, acidotic, ATP-depleted heart. Asystole is the final, terminal rhythm if VF is not corrected.[1]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls:

- **Calling Fine VF "Asystole" and Not Shocking:** The monitor gain is low, and the VF is so fine it looks like a flat line. You check a second lead and increase the gain. If it is fine VF, the rhythm strip shows a subtle, oscillating baseline, not a true flat line. A shock is the only chance for ROSC. Asystole is a true, electrical flat line. Giving adrenaline to fine VF is not enough; the patient needs a shock.
- **The "Slow Code" Error:** CPR is in progress, but the pauses between compressions for rhythm checks are long. The defibrillator is charging, but the team does not coordinate the shock. The rule is: CPR continues while the defibrillator is charging. The rhythm check is a glance at the monitor to confirm shockable. The shock is delivered, and CPR is resumed immediately after the shock without a pulse or rhythm check. The hands-off time (the time from the last compression before the shock to the first compression after the shock) must be less than 5–10 seconds. Every second without a compression is a second the coronary perfusion pressure is falling to zero.[1]

**Correct Detection:**

The diagnosis is made by the cardiac monitor and the pulseless, unresponsive patient. The rhythm is either a chaotic, irregular, disorganized waveform (VF) or a wide, rapid, monomorphic or polymorphic ventricular tachycardia at a rate >200 bpm with no pulse.

### Emergency Management (The 2025 AHA ACLS Algorithm):

- **Immediate, High-Quality CPR:** 30:2 ratio, depth ≥5 cm, rate 100–120/min, full chest recoil.
- **Defibrillation (First Shock):** 120–200 J biphasic, as soon as the defibrillator is charged. Immediately resume CPR.
- **Adrenaline 1 mg IV/IO:** After the second shock, and every 3–5 minutes thereafter.
- **Amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO Bolus:** After the third shock. A second dose of 150 mg can be given if VF/pVT is refractory.
- **Treat Reversible Causes (Hs and Ts):** The most critical in VF arrest is an acute coronary occlusion (Thrombosis, coronary). The patient who achieves ROSC after an out-of-hospital VF arrest must go immediately to the cardiac catheterization laboratory, even if the post-ROSC ECG does not show STEMI.[1]

### 9. Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA) / Asystole Cardiac Arrest

**Typical Disease Story:**

An 80-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer is admitted with urosepsis. His nurse finds him unresponsive and apneic. The cardiac monitor shows a narrow-complex, organized sinus tachycardia at 110 bpm. However, there is no palpable pulse and no end-tidal CO₂ waveform. This is a PEA arrest.

**Pathogenesis:**

PEA is not a rhythm problem; it is a mechanical pump problem. The heart has an organized electrical rhythm, but there is either no myocardial contraction or the contraction is so weak that it produces no cardiac output and no palpable pulse. The etiology is the "Hs and Ts." In sepsis, the cause is profound vasodilation and absolute or relative hypovolemia (Hypovolemia, Hydrogen ion/acidosis) causing the left ventricle to collapse on itself in systole. The ECG continues to fire because the cardiac conduction system is still functioning. Asystole is the end-stage of a prolonged cardiac arrest or a primary catastrophic event (massive PE, tension pneumothorax, exsanguination) that immediately extinguishes all electrical and mechanical activity.[1]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls:

- **Wasting Time Searching for a Pulse in a PEA:** The clinical assessment for a pulse takes no more than 10 seconds. If you are unsure, you start CPR. A palpable pulse is the definitive sign of ROSC. The arterial line waveform (if present), a sudden, sustained rise in ETCO₂ >40 mmHg, and a palpable pulse are the signs of ROSC.
- **Failing to Identify and Treat the Specific, Reversible Cause:** The single most common error in a PEA arrest is to robotically follow the algorithm (CPR, adrenaline, CPR, adrenaline) without a rapid, intelligent, and systematic search for the specific cause. The survival of a PEA arrest is entirely dependent on finding and reversing the cause in real time. This is the core intellectual task of the code leader.[2]

### The "Hs and Ts" Differential Diagnosis and Their Specific, Immediate Actions:

| Cause | The Critical, Life-Saving Action in Real Time |
|---|---|
| **Hypovolemia** | Rapid, large-bore IV infusion of warmed normal saline or blood. Look for the source of bleeding. |
| **Hypoxia** | Secure the airway, confirm bilateral breath sounds and ETCO2 waveform, give 100% FiO2. |
| **Hydrogen ion (Acidosis)** | Hyperventilate to blow off CO2. Give sodium bicarbonate only if severe metabolic acidosis with prolonged arrest (>10 min). |
| **Hyper-/Hypokalemia** | If K+ <2.5, give IV magnesium and potassium. If K+ >6.5 with ECG changes, give IV calcium. |
| **Hypothermia** | Continue CPR until the core temperature is >32°C. Shivering and severe bradycardia are not asystole; they are a hypothermic, perfusing rhythm. Do not defibrillate a hypothermic heart (<30°C) more than once. |
| **Tension Pneumothorax** | Immediate needle decompression (14G cannula, 2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line). Do not wait for a chest X-ray. |
| **Tamponade, Cardiac** | Bedside cardiac ultrasound. Immediate pericardiocentesis. |
| **Thrombosis, Pulmonary (Massive PE)** | Bedside cardiac ultrasound (RV dilation, McConnell's sign). Give a systemic thrombolytic (alteplase 50 mg IV bolus) during CPR. Continue CPR for at least 60 minutes after lysis. |
| **Thrombosis, Coronary (Massive MI)** | The patient has a witnessed VF arrest that has now degraded to a PEA. Go to the cath lab. |
| **Toxins** | Identify the specific toxin. Give the specific antidote (naloxone for opioids, sodium bicarbonate for tricyclic overdose, high-dose insulin for beta-blocker/calcium channel blocker overdose, Intralipid for local anesthetic toxicity). |

### 10. Acute Aortic Regurgitation (Type A Dissection, Endocarditis)

**Typical Disease Story:**

A 45-year-old man with a bicuspid aortic valve and known ascending aortic aneurysm presents with sudden, severe dyspnea and a tearing chest pain that radiates to his back. He is in severe respiratory distress, with a BP of 85/40 mmHg, a bounding carotid pulse (Corrigan's water-hammer pulse), and a new, loud, early diastolic, decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border. His chest X-ray shows a massively widened mediastinum.

**Pathogenesis:**

The intimal tear from a Type A aortic dissection propagates retrograde and disrupts the commissures of the native aortic valve, causing it to prolapse and become acutely, severely incompetent. During diastole, a massive column of blood from the aorta regurgitates back into the normal-sized, non-compliant left ventricle, which has not had time to remodel. The left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) rises catastrophically and rapidly exceeds the left atrial pressure, causing premature closure of the mitral valve (a pathognomonic echocardiographic sign). This prevents the backup of blood into the pulmonary veins, but the cardiac output is critically reduced, causing cardiogenic shock.[3]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls:

- **The Murmur is Soft or Inaudible:** The regurgitant orifice is so large, and the aortic and LV diastolic pressures equalize so rapidly, that the turbulent flow that creates the audible murmur is minimal. A patient with a dissection, flash pulmonary edema, and a soft diastolic murmur is having acute severe AR. The echocardiogram is the only diagnostic test.
- **Treating the Hypertension:** The patient may have a wide pulse pressure, but the diastolic pressure is collapsing. The systolic pressure may be elevated due to the catecholamine surge, but the forward cardiac output is critically low. Beta-blockers and vasodilators must be used with extreme caution, as they can cause cardiovascular collapse in this preload-dependent state.

### Emergency Management:

- **Afterload Reduction:** Nitroprusside IV is the first-line agent to reduce the regurgitant fraction and increase forward flow.
- **Avoid Bradycardia:** The prolonged diastolic filling period in bradycardia allows more time for regurgitation. The heart rate should be maintained at 80–100 bpm.
- **Definitive Treatment:** Emergency surgery to replace the ascending aorta and the aortic valve.[3]

### 11. Acute Ventricular Septal Rupture (VSR) Post-MI

**Typical Disease Story:**

A 70-year-old woman with a large, delayed-presentation anteroseptal STEMI is on day 4 of her hospital stay. She suddenly develops recurrent, severe chest pain, severe dyspnea, and flash pulmonary edema. A new, loud, harsh, grade 5/6 pansystolic murmur is heard at the left lower sternal border with a palpable thrill. She is hypotensive, with a systolic BP of 80/50 mmHg and a heart rate of 120 bpm.

**Pathogenesis:**

The infarcted, necrotic interventricular septum has been infiltrated by macrophages and metalloproteinases. On day 3–7, the necrotic tissue softens and ruptures, creating a mechanical defect between the left and right ventricles. The high-pressure left ventricle shunts a massive volume of blood directly into the low-pressure right ventricle. This causes a sudden, massive volume overload of the right ventricle and the pulmonary circulation, causing acute right heart failure and flash pulmonary edema. The left ventricle loses a significant portion of its forward stroke volume into the shunt, causing cardiogenic shock.[4]

### Classic Errors and Diagnostic Pitfalls:

- **Misdiagnosing as Acute, Severe Mitral Regurgitation (Papillary Muscle Rupture):** Both present identically: a new murmur, flash pulmonary edema, and shock on day 3–7 post-MI. The clinical discriminator is the murmur location (LLSB with a thrill for VSR, apex and axilla for MR) and the echocardiogram. The transthoracic echo shows a color flow jet crossing the interventricular septum. The treatment is entirely different: an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is specifically life-saving in VSR (reduces afterload and reduces the left-to-right shunt fraction). It is much less effective in acute MR.[4]

### Emergency Management:

- **Afterload Reduction:** The IABP is the critical, life-saving bridge. Nitroprusside IV is used to reduce the left ventricular pressure and the driving force across the shunt.
- **Definitive Treatment:** Emergency surgical repair. The mortality without surgery exceeds 90%.[4]

### 12. Digoxin (Detailed Ward-Based Adjustments)

**Mechanism:**

Digoxin is a cardiac glycoside. Its primary mechanism of action is the inhibition of the myocardial sodium-potassium adenosine triphosphatase (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) pump. This increases the intracellular sodium concentration. The elevated sodium reduces the sodium gradient that drives the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, causing an accumulation of intracellular calcium. This increased calcium availability to the contractile proteins produces a **positive inotropic effect** (increased force of contraction). At the same time, digoxin increases vagal (parasympathetic) tone centrally, which slows the firing rate of the sinoatrial node and slows conduction velocity and prolongs the refractory period of the atrioventricular node. This is its **negative chronotropic effect** , used for ventricular rate control in atrial fibrillation.[6]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Rate Control in Atrial Fibrillation with Rapid Ventricular Response:** Especially in patients with concomitant heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), where beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers may be poorly tolerated due to their negative inotropic effects. Digoxin is a positive inotrope and a negative chronotrope.
- **Symptomatic Chronic Systolic Heart Failure (HFrEF, LVEF ≤40%):** As a second-line agent, added to the four foundational pillars (beta-blocker, ACE-I/ARB/ARNI, MRA, SGLT2i), to reduce heart failure hospitalizations. It does not reduce overall mortality.

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting (Ward-Based Management):

- **The Rule for Holding Digoxin:** Hold the drug immediately if the patient develops any of the following: **new-onset nausea, vomiting, confusion, or visual disturbances (yellow-green halos around lights = xanthopsia)** . These are the classical, prodromal symptoms of digoxin toxicity. You must hold the drug, order a serum digoxin trough level, and an ECG.
- **Bradycardia or New High-Grade AV Block:** Digoxin toxicity causes excessive vagal tone. The classic ECG in digoxin toxicity is a **regularized, slow ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation** (which looks like a junctional rhythm on the monitor, but the underlying rhythm is AF) or a **paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with block (PAT with block)** . This is a hallmark of digitalis toxicity.
- **Acute Kidney Injury:** Digoxin is 70–80% cleared by the kidneys. If a patient's creatinine rises acutely (e.g., AKI from sepsis or diuresis), the serum digoxin level will proportionally rise. The digoxin dose must be held or empirically reduced, and a trough level must be checked within 3–5 days.
- **Hypokalemia (K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L):** This is a critical potentiator of digoxin toxicity. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump binding site for digoxin is the same site that binds potassium. When the extracellular potassium is low, digoxin binds more avidly to the pump, massively potentiating its toxic effect. A patient can have digoxin toxicity with a "normal" serum digoxin level if the potassium is low. The potassium must be urgently repleted to a target >4.0 mmol/L.[6]

### Monitoring:

- **Serum Digoxin Trough Level:** Must be drawn immediately before the next scheduled dose (at least 6–8 hours after the last dose). The therapeutic range for heart failure is 0.5–0.9 ng/mL. Toxicity is defined as a level >1.2 ng/mL, but clinical toxicity can occur at any level in the setting of hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, or hypercalcemia.

### 13. Ivabradine

**Mechanism:**

A selective, specific inhibitor of the "funny current" (If) in the sinoatrial node. The If channel is a mixed sodium-potassium inward current that is activated by hyperpolarization and is responsible for the spontaneous, slow diastolic depolarization (phase 4) that determines the heart rate. By blocking this current, ivabradine reduces the slope of phase 4 depolarization, thereby reducing the firing rate of the sinus node. It has no negative inotropic effect and no effect on blood pressure.[7]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Chronic Stable Angina:** When beta-blockers are contraindicated or not tolerated, and the heart rate remains >70 bpm.
- **Chronic Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (LVEF ≤35%):** In patients who are in sinus rhythm and on optimal medical therapy (including a maximally tolerated beta-blocker), and whose resting heart rate remains ≥70 bpm. It reduces heart failure hospitalizations.

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Symptomatic Bradycardia:** The drug's effect is purely heart rate reduction. If the patient is bradycardic and symptomatic, the drug must be held. It is not contraindicated in acute decompensated heart failure unless the heart rate is low.
- **Atrial Fibrillation:** Ivabradine is not effective in AF (it only affects the sinus node). It should be stopped if the patient develops AF, as it increases the risk of a visual phenomenon called "phosphenes" (transient, enhanced brightness in a limited area of the visual field).

### Part Five: How to Systematically Adjust a Patient's Cardiac Medication List (The "Lizom")

You are called to see a patient with acute decompensated heart failure. He is on a complex list of chronic cardiac medications. This is a systematic, physiologically driven thought process for adjusting them.

### The Patient's Home Medication List:

- Bisoprolol 5 mg PO once daily.
- Ramipril 5 mg PO BID.
- Spironolactone 25 mg PO once daily.
- Empagliflozin 10 mg PO once daily.
- Furosemide 80 mg PO BID.
- Digoxin 0.125 mg PO once daily.
- Apixaban 5 mg PO BID.
- Atorvastatin 80 mg PO once daily.
- Aspirin 100 mg PO once daily.
- Isosorbide Mononitrate 60 mg PO once daily.

**The Acute Clinical Scenario:**

The patient is cold, clammy, and hypotensive (SBP 85/50 mmHg). His JVP is elevated to the angle of the jaw, he has an S3 gallop, and his lungs are full of crackles. He has acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema with low cardiac output (cardiogenic shock). His creatinine has risen from 1.2 to 2.8 mg/dL. His potassium is 5.6 mmol/L.

### The Step-by-Step Adjustment Decision:

### Immediate Life-Saving Interventions (First 5 Minutes):

  - You are not thinking about his chronic oral medications yet. You are giving oxygen, placing him on CPAP, and starting an IV nitroglycerin drip. You call the ICU.

### The Hemodynamic Decision (Hold or Continue?):

  - **Bisoprolol (Beta-Blocker):** The patient is in cardiogenic shock. The beta-blocker is a negative inotrope. **Hold it immediately.** The heart rate is the only thing maintaining his cardiac output right now. Restart only when he is warm, euvolemic, and off inotropes for 24 hours.
  - **Ramipril (ACE-I):** The patient is hypotensive and his creatinine has doubled. The ACE-I is worsening his renal function by dilating the efferent arteriole in a hypoperfused kidney. **Hold it immediately.**
  - **Spironolactone (MRA):** The potassium is 5.6 mmol/L, and his renal function has acutely declined. **Hold it immediately.** This is a non-negotiable contraindication.
  - **Furosemide (Loop Diuretic):** He is volume overloaded but hypotensive. He needs diuresis, but his blood pressure will not tolerate a large IV bolus. The strategy is to start a continuous IV furosemide infusion (10–20 mg/hr) after starting the inotrope/vasopressor, or to give a small IV bolus (40–80 mg) and assess the response.
  - **Digoxin:** The acute kidney injury will cause the digoxin level to accumulate. The potassium is high (which paradoxically reduces the digoxin effect acutely, but this is an unstable state). **Hold it.** Check a trough level.

### The Long-Term Medications That Are Safe (Continue Unless Actively Bleeding):

  - **Apixaban (DOAC):** The creatinine has risen, but not to the point where apixaban is contraindicated (CrCl must be <15 mL/min for the 5 mg BID dose). Continue it, but calculate the CrCl at the bedside. If CrCl falls below 25 mL/min, the dose must be reduced to 2.5 mg BID.
  - **Atorvastatin (Statin):** Continue. It is not contributing to the acute decompensation.
  - **Aspirin:** Continue. The patient has coronary artery disease. Do not stop the aspirin unless there is active, life-threatening bleeding.
  - **Empagliflozin (SGLT2i):** This is a complex decision. The SGLT2i is a diuretic and can cause volume depletion and a small, acute drop in GFR. In cardiogenic shock, most clinicians would **hold it** until the patient is stabilized.

### The Drugs You Will Add in the Acute Setting (After Stabilization):

  - **Inotrope (Dobutamine or Milrinone):** To increase cardiac contractility. Dobutamine is a beta-1 and beta-2 agonist. Milrinone is a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor. Both are positive inotropes and vasodilators. They are started in the ICU with invasive hemodynamic monitoring.
  - **Vasopressor (Norepinephrine):** If the patient remains hypotensive despite inotropic support. Norepinephrine is an alpha-1 and beta-1 agonist, providing both vasoconstriction and a modest inotropic boost. It is the first-line vasopressor in cardiogenic shock.
  - **IV Furosemide:** As discussed.

### The Restarting Plan (The "De-escalation" Phase in the ICU):

  - When the patient is warm, euvolemic, off inotropes for 24 hours, and his creatinine is back to baseline, you restart his chronic oral medications in a specific sequence:
    - **Beta-Blocker:** Start at a low dose (Bisoprolol 2.5 mg).
    - **ACE-I/ARB/ARNI:** Start at a low dose when the creatinine is stable and the potassium is <5.0 mmol/L.
    - **MRA:** Restart last, once the potassium is <5.0 and the creatinine is back to baseline. Do not restart it if the K⁺ remains high.
    - **SGLT2i:** Restart when euvolemic and renal function is stable.
