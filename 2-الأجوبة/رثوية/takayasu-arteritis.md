## First: Definition and overview

::: mech
- Definition: chronic, idiopathic, granulomatous large-vessel vasculitis principally involving the aorta, its major branches, and — importantly for internal medicine — the pulmonary and coronary arteries, producing stenosis, occlusion, dilatation, or aneurysm. [E]
- Nosology: classified as large-vessel vasculitis (LVV) under the 2012 Revised International Chapel Hill Consensus Conference nomenclature, alongside giant cell arteritis. These are the only two primary LVVs. [E]
- Eponym: Mikito Takayasu, Japanese ophthalmologist, 1908 — reported wreath-like peripapillary arteriovenous anastomoses in a young woman. The disease was first described through its retinal consequence, not its arterial cause.
- Synonyms: pulseless disease · aortic arch syndrome · occlusive thromboaortopathy · Martorell syndrome (historical, abandoned).
:::

**Distinction from GCA** rests on three axes: age at onset (TAK typically ≤40, and age
≤60 is an absolute requirement for classification ^[3]; GCA effectively never below 50),
vessel distribution (TAK favours aorta and primary branches including subclavian,
carotid, renal, and pulmonary; GCA favours cranial branches of the external carotid,
though large-vessel GCA overlaps substantially), and geography/ethnicity. The overlap
between late-onset TAK and large-vessel GCA is genuine and unresolved. [L]

## Second: Epidemiological snapshot

**Clinical significance** — a rare disease with disproportionate impact: it strikes young
women in their productive years, causes irreversible arterial damage that accrues even
during clinical remission, and its principal killers are cardiovascular. Increased
mortality is largely driven by cardiovascular disease ^[11]. [E]

::: mech
- Incidence: 0.4–3.4 per million per year ^[8]; also estimated at approximately 2.6 per million worldwide ^[9]. [E]
- Prevalence: highest in Japan at around 40 per million, compared with about 9 per million in the USA ^[8]. [E]
- Peak age: around 90% of patients are younger than 30 at onset ^[9]. Second and third decades dominate. [E]
- Sex: strong female predominance. The magnitude varies geographically — no reliable pooled sex ratio located; do not quote a figure. [L]
- Geography: highest in Japan, India, Korea, and Thailand ^[8]; also over-represented in Chinese, Turkish, Mexican, and other Latin American populations. [E]
- Mortality rate: no reliable pooled figure located. Reported survival varies widely across single-centre cohorts. Do not quote a survival percentage. [?]
:::

::: amber "A neglected disease, in a specific sense"
Rarity plus a nonspecific inflammatory prodrome produces long diagnostic delay; the
highest-burden populations are in low- and middle-income countries where advanced
imaging and biologics are least available; and randomised trial evidence is almost
absent. The systematic review informing the current EULAR recommendations concluded
that high-quality, adequately sized randomised controlled trials in TAK are still
lacking ^[2]. Nearly all ACR/VF 2021 recommendations are **conditional** because the
evidence quality is low ^[15]. Carry that into any discussion of management.
:::

### Screening

::: teal "According to the 2025 EULAR recommendations"
**There is no population screening programme for TAK, and none is recommended.** At a
prevalence of a few tens per million, no screening test could achieve acceptable
positive predictive value. [E]

What the guidelines *do* mandate is screening **within** the diagnosed population:
those with large-vessel vasculitis should be screened for cardiovascular comorbidities,
with prophylactic and lifestyle interventions to mitigate risk ^[1]. Also mandated:
imaging should be used routinely to assess both disease activity and damage in all TAK
patients ^[1] — a stronger position than in GCA. [G]
:::

### Risk factors, each with the mechanistic reason

- **Female sex** — the strongest demographic association. Mechanism unresolved; sex-hormone modulation of Th1/Th17 differentiation is hypothesised but not established. [L]
- **East, South, or Southeast Asian, Turkish, or Latin American ancestry** — reflects underlying allele frequency differences, principally at HLA, rather than an environmental effect. [E]
- **HLA-B\*52:01** — the most consistently replicated genetic association across populations. HLA-B is a class I molecule presenting peptide to **CD8+ cytotoxic T cells**; a particular B\*52:01 binding groove is presumed to present a vascular or cross-reactive antigen, driving cytotoxic attack on the arterial media. [E] for the association, [L] for the mechanism.
- **Other loci** — *IL12B*, *FCGR2A/FCGR3A*, *IL6*, *MLX*, *RPS9/LILRB3*. Mechanisms map onto the Th1 axis (IL-12 → IFN-γ), Fc receptor signalling, and IL-6 amplification — the biological rationale for tocilizumab. [L]
- **Family history** — first-degree aggregation reported but concordance low, consistent with modest polygenic risk plus an unidentified environmental trigger. [L]
- **Prior mycobacterial exposure** — molecular mimicry between mycobacterial HSP65 and human HSP60 on stressed aortic endothelium, plus geographic co-distribution with tuberculosis. **Unproven, and confounded by the fact that tuberculous aortitis is a diagnostic mimic rather than necessarily a cause.** [L]
- **No established modifiable risk factor.** Smoking, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia do not cause TAK but compound its vascular damage — which is why they become targets once the diagnosis is made. [E]

## Third: Pattern recognition — the archetypal case

::: key "The vignette to store"
A **24-year-old woman of South Asian ancestry** presents after nine months of intermittent
low-grade fever, fatigue, night sweats, arthralgia, and 6 kg unintentional weight loss.
She has been investigated twice for pyrexia of unknown origin and treated presumptively
for a viral illness and then for possible tuberculosis. Over the last two months her
**left arm tires and aches when she hangs washing**, and she becomes lightheaded on
turning her head.

On examination she is thin and unwell. The **left radial and brachial pulses are absent**;
the right radial is normal. Blood pressure is **unrecordable in the left arm and 168/98 mmHg
in the right**. There is a **bruit over the left supraclavicular fossa and over the left
carotid**, and the left carotid is tender. A soft early diastolic murmur is audible at the
left sternal edge. Lower limb pulses are present. Fundoscopy is normal.

Investigations: **ESR 82 mm/h, CRP 48 mg/L**, haemoglobin 96 g/L normochromic normocytic,
platelets 480 ×10⁹/L, albumin 29 g/L, creatinine normal, urinalysis bland. Blood cultures
sterile; IGRA negative; syphilis serology negative. **CT angiography** shows long-segment,
smooth, **concentric mural thickening** with luminal narrowing of the left subclavian and
left common carotid arteries, circumferential thickening of the aortic arch wall, and mild
aortic root dilatation.
:::

### Discriminating features

- **Age and sex versus atherosclerosis:** a woman under 40 with no metabolic risk factors.
- **Lesion morphology:** long, smooth, tapering, **concentric** stenoses with homogeneous circumferential wall thickening — versus short, eccentric, calcified, ostial plaques.
- **Wall involvement precedes lumen involvement** — invisible on luminal-only imaging.
- **Involvement of the aorta itself**, not merely its branches. Atherosclerosis and fibromuscular dysplasia do not thicken the aortic wall circumferentially.
- **Blood pressure discrepancy between limbs** with an ipsilateral bruit.
- **Carotidynia** — tenderness of the carotid artery. Rare but strongly suggestive, and one of only four variables in a validated activity model ^[12].
- **Raised acute-phase reactants** in a young person with arterial stenosis.
- **Versus fibromuscular dysplasia:** FMD gives a beaded "string of beads" in the mid-to-distal renal or carotid artery, with normal inflammatory markers and no aortic wall thickening.
- **Versus Buerger's disease:** distal, small- and medium-vessel, heavy smoker, corkscrew collaterals, aorta spared. EULAR names Buerger's and FMD as the specific differentials to revisit when TAK appears refractory ^[1]. [G]

### Pathognomonic features

::: red "None exist — state this plainly"
No symptom, sign, laboratory value, or imaging appearance is pathognomonic for TAK. [E]

The nearest approach to specificity is the **combination** of long-segment concentric
aortic and primary-branch wall thickening in a patient aged ≤40 with raised acute-phase
reactants and no alternative explanation — highly suggestive, not diagnostic.

**Takayasu retinopathy** (wreath-like peripapillary arteriovenous anastomoses) is highly
characteristic but uncommon and mimicked by other causes of chronic ocular hypoperfusion.

The classification criteria are explicitly **not** diagnostic criteria. EULAR states as an
overarching principle that classification criteria should not substitute for clinical
diagnosis ^[1]. [G]
:::

## Fourth: Pathogenesis and pathophysiology

::: amber "Template subsections not applicable (Rule B4)"
**Infectious organism, incubation period, infectivity, transmission:** not applicable.
TAK is not infectious and not transmissible. A mycobacterial molecular-mimicry hypothesis
exists but is unproven. [L]

**Histological subtypes with percentages, and staging system:** not applicable. TAK is not
neoplastic and has no TNM, FIGO, or Ann Arbor equivalent. Its anatomical classification is
angiographic (below), which describes distribution, not stage.
:::

### Mechanistic sequence from insult to manifestation

1. **Unknown trigger in a genetically susceptible host.** HLA-B\*52:01-restricted antigen presentation is presumed central; the antigen is unidentified. [L]
2. **Adventitia-first entry.** Immune cells enter the arterial wall through the **vasa vasorum**, which supply the adventitia and outer media of large elastic arteries. This "outside-in" route is the single most useful mechanistic fact in the disease, because it explains why the adventitia and outer media are involved earliest, why **wall thickening precedes luminal narrowing** so imaging must interrogate the wall, and why the aorta and its large branches are targeted while small arteries without vasa vasorum are spared. [E]
3. **Vascular dendritic cell activation** in the adventitia recruits and activates T cells.
4. **CD4+ Th1 polarisation** — IL-12-driven, producing IFN-γ, which activates macrophages, drives their fusion into **multinucleated giant cells**, and forms **granulomas** in the media. The histological signature. [E]
5. **CD4+ Th17 polarisation** — IL-17 production, recruiting neutrophils; less steroid-responsive than the Th1 axis, part of the explanation for glucocorticoid-refractory disease. [L]
6. **CD8+ cytotoxic and γδ T cells** release perforin and granzyme B onto medial smooth muscle cells → medial smooth muscle death. [E]
7. **IL-6 and TNF-α amplification** — drives the systemic acute-phase response (fever, ESR/CRP rise, anaemia of inflammation, thrombocytosis) and sustains local inflammation. Direct biological rationale for **tocilizumab** and **TNF inhibitors**. [E]
8. **Matrix metalloproteinase-mediated destruction** — MMP-2 and MMP-9 degrade elastin and collagen. [L]
9. **Two divergent structural outcomes**, and which occurs determines the entire clinical picture:
   - **Fibrostenotic** (majority): intimal hyperplasia plus medial and adventitial fibrosis → concentric, long-segment **stenosis then occlusion**.
   - **Aneurysmal** (minority): loss of medial elastic support → **dilatation, aneurysm, dissection**, including aortic root dilatation.
   - Both can coexist in the same patient — stenosis in one segment, aneurysm in another. [E]

### Downstream pathophysiology by territory

- **Renal artery stenosis or mid-aortic narrowing** → reduced renal perfusion pressure → **RAAS activation** → renovascular hypertension. The commonest mechanism of hypertension in TAK, often severe and early. [E]
- **Cerebral hypoperfusion** from carotid and vertebral stenosis → dizziness, visual disturbance, syncope, TIA, stroke. Extensive collateralisation means symptoms lag far behind anatomical severity.
- **Subclavian steal** — stenosis proximal to the vertebral origin; arm exercise lowers distal subclavian pressure, reversing vertebral flow away from the brainstem.
- **Coronary ostial stenosis** → angina and myocardial infarction in a young woman with no atherosclerotic risk factors. TAK belongs on the differential for MI under 40.
- **Pulmonary artery involvement** → pulmonary hypertension and, in advanced disease, right heart failure. Frequently missed because it is not looked for.
- **Aortic root dilatation** → aortic regurgitation → LV volume overload → heart failure.
- **Heart failure in TAK is multifactorial:** hypertensive, valvular (AR), ischaemic (coronary ostial), pulmonary hypertensive, and occasionally directly myocarditic.
- **Mesenteric and coeliac stenosis** → post-prandial pain and weight loss; extensive collaterals make critical mesenteric ischaemia uncommon.
- **Chronic kidney disease** — from renal artery stenosis, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, occasionally an associated glomerulopathy.

::: red "The measurement trap, which follows directly from the pathophysiology"
If the subclavian arteries are stenosed, the cuff pressure in the arm **underestimates
central aortic pressure**. A patient with 110/70 in both arms may have severe central
hypertension driving target-organ damage. Conversely, treating to the arm reading can
produce dangerous central hypotension.

**Four-limb blood pressure, and where all four are affected, central pressure measurement,
are not academic refinements.**
:::

### Histopathology

Granulomatous **panarteritis** — all three layers. Adventitial fibrosis and vasa vasorum
inflammation; medial destruction with lymphocytic and macrophage infiltrate, multinucleated
giant cells, and loss of elastic lamina; intimal fibrous hyperplasia. **Skip lesions** are
characteristic — involvement is segmental, with normal wall between diseased segments. In
the burnt-out phase only dense fibrosis remains, which is why late biopsy can be
misleadingly bland. [E]

### Anatomical (angiographic) classification — Hata/Numano types

| Type | Segments involved | Pooled prevalence ^[10] |
|---|---|---|
| I | Branches of the aortic arch | 23.44–23.94% [E] |
| IIa | Ascending aorta, arch, and arch branches | 6.37–6.76% [E] |
| IIb | Ascending aorta, arch and branches, plus thoracic descending aorta | 7.92–8.20% [E] |
| III | Thoracic descending aorta, abdominal aorta, and/or renal arteries | 5.13–5.40% (least common) [E] |
| IV | Abdominal aorta and/or renal arteries | not retrieved [?] |
| V | Features of IIb and IV — diffuse involvement | 43.49% (most common) [E] |

Coronary involvement is designated **C+**, pulmonary artery involvement **P+** ^[10].

::: violet "Interpretation caveat that matters more than the numbers"
I² of approximately 78–83% means these pooled estimates conceal very large between-study
variation, driven by geography and imaging modality. **Do not use them as expected
frequencies for your own population.** Type V showed the greatest clinical severity and
types IIb and III the lowest ^[10] — meta-regression on observational data, not a validated
prognostic tool. [L]

Regional pattern: in Indian patients vasculitis generally began in the abdominal aorta
involving the renal arteries and later spread to the thoracic aorta; in Japanese patients
it generally began in the ascending aorta, arch, or its branches ^[10]. [L]
:::

## Fifth: The diagnostic pathway

### 1. Mode of presentation

Three routes, in descending order of clinical impression — **relative frequencies could
not be sourced** [?]:

- **With ischaemic or vascular complaints** — limb claudication, dizziness, visual disturbance, chest pain, or incidental discovery of an absent pulse or inter-arm BP difference.
- **With systemic inflammatory illness** — fever, weight loss, arthralgia, malaise, raised ESR/CRP, often labelled pyrexia of unknown origin for months. TAK is a recognised cause of PUO in a young woman.
- **Incidentally** — asymmetric pulses at an unrelated examination; arterial wall thickening on CT or ultrasound done for another indication; increased aortic FDG uptake on PET for PUO or malignancy work-up. Incidental detection is increasing. [L]

::: amber "On the classical triphasic model"
A systemic "pre-pulseless" inflammatory phase, then a vascular inflammatory phase, then a
burnt-out fibrotic phase. A useful teaching scaffold but **not reliably observed**: many
patients never have a recognised systemic phase, and inflammation and fibrosis coexist.
**Do not use phase assignment to make treatment decisions.** [L]
:::

### 2. Symptoms

::: red "No reliable frequency ranking located (Rule B4)"
This subsection cannot be completed as the template requests, and padding it would be a
rule violation. The only frequency dataset retrieved is a review of 43 published case
reports in which fever was the most frequent symptom, followed by chest pain, claudication,
and headache ^[14]. Forty-three case reports, selected for publication precisely because they
were instructive, cannot support a frequency ranking. **I will not present those percentages
as symptom frequencies.** No pooled, population-based frequency data were located. [?]
:::

**Constitutional (inflammatory)** [E] — fever (IL-6 and TNF-α acting on the hypothalamic
preoptic area via PGE₂; typically low-grade and prolonged) · fatigue and malaise (cytokine
effects plus anaemia of inflammation plus, later, tissue hypoperfusion) · weight loss
(inflammatory anorexia and catabolism) · arthralgia and myalgia (mechanism incompletely
defined [L]) · night sweats — **overlapping entirely with tuberculosis and lymphoma, which
is why those must be excluded before immunosuppression.**

**Upper limb ischaemic** [E] — arm claudication from subclavian, axillary, or brachial
stenosis; often described as "my arm gets tired," not as pain, and therefore not volunteered
unless asked · cold, numb, or weak hand; frank gangrene uncommon because collaterals develop
over months.

**Cerebrovascular** [E] — dizziness, lightheadedness, visual blurring (carotid or vertebral
stenosis, worse on standing or head turning) · syncope · **dizziness or drop attacks
specifically on arm exertion** (subclavian steal — this history is close to specific) ·
TIA or stroke, which **may be the first manifestation of TAK in a young adult** [L] ·
headache (hypertensive, carotid inflammation, or cerebral hypoperfusion).

**Cardiac** [E] — chest pain must be mechanistically parsed, because three processes produce it:
- **Coronary ostial stenosis** → exertional angina, or infarction.
- **Aortitis of the ascending aorta or arch** → deep, constant, sometimes interscapular pain from adventitial inflammation.
- **Aortic dissection** → abrupt, maximal-at-onset, migratory tearing pain. The emergency reading.
- Note that **angina carries +2 points** in the 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria ^[3], reflecting how strongly it associates with TAK in a young cohort.

Also: **exertional dyspnoea** — from aortic regurgitation, hypertensive heart disease,
pulmonary artery stenosis with pulmonary hypertension, or ischaemic LV dysfunction. In TAK,
"breathless young woman" has a broad cardiac differential *within the same disease*.
**Palpitations** — hypertensive LVH, atrial arrhythmia, or anaemia.

**Lower limb and abdominal** [E] — leg claudication (abdominal aortic or iliac stenosis;
also mid-aortic narrowing in types III, IV, V) · post-prandial abdominal pain with food
avoidance and weight loss (coeliac and mesenteric stenosis) · flank pain (renal infarction
or renal artery involvement).

**Attributable to hypertension** [E] — headache, visual disturbance, epistaxis, or
presentation with hypertensive emergency. In a hypertensive patient under 40, TAK belongs on
the secondary-hypertension differential alongside renal artery FMD, phaeochromocytoma,
primary aldosteronism, and coarctation.

**Ocular** [E] — transient visual obscuration or amaurosis (carotid or ophthalmic
hypoperfusion) · chronic hypoperfusive (Takayasu) retinopathy → progressive visual loss ·
hypertensive retinopathy.

**Pregnancy-specific** [E] — worsening hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and fetal growth
restriction from placental hypoperfusion with aortic or renal artery stenosis. Pregnancy in
TAK is a specialist multidisciplinary problem.

### 3. Signs

Frequency ranking again not sourceable [?] — but the **2022 ACR/EULAR criteria weights give
a defensible proxy for discriminating value**, which is more useful than an unsourced list.

::: teal "2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria — items and weights ^[3]"
Female sex **+1** · angina **+2** · limb claudication **+2** · arterial bruit **+2** ·
reduced upper extremity pulse **+2** · reduced pulse or tenderness of a carotid artery **+2** ·
blood pressure difference between arms ≥20 mmHg **+1** · number of affected arterial
territories **+1 to +3** · paired artery involvement **+1** · abdominal aorta plus renal or
mesenteric involvement **+3**. [G]
:::

- **Reduced or absent peripheral pulse** — most often radial or brachial, from proximal subclavian or axillary stenosis. Flow-limiting narrowing distal to which pulse pressure is damped. The origin of "pulseless disease." [E]
- **Inter-arm blood pressure difference** — asymmetric subclavian stenosis. ≥20 mmHg is a criteria item ^[3]. **Measure both arms in every young hypertensive patient.**
- **Arterial bruit** — turbulent flow across a stenosis. Auscultate systematically: both supraclavicular fossae, both carotids, the abdomen (epigastric and both renal areas), and both femorals. A bruit in a young person with no atherosclerotic risk factors is a high-value finding.
- **Carotid tenderness (carotidynia)** — adventitial and perivascular inflammation. One of four variables in a validated activity model, weighted 3 points ^[12], so it carries information about **activity**, not merely presence.
- **Hypertension** — renovascular or mechanical.
- **Early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge** — aortic regurgitation from root dilatation.
- **Discrepant upper and lower limb blood pressure** — mid-aortic or descending aortic narrowing; coarctation-like physiology.
- **Absent or delayed femoral pulses with radio-femoral delay.**
- **Signs of heart failure** — raised JVP, crackles, displaced apex, peripheral oedema.
- **Loud P2 and right ventricular signs** — pulmonary artery involvement with pulmonary hypertension.
- **Fundoscopic findings** — Takayasu retinopathy or hypertensive changes.
- **Pallor** — anaemia of inflammation.

### 4. Differential diagnosis — two separate lists

::: red "A. Dangerous differentials — exclude first, ranked by lethality and time-sensitivity"
1. **Aortic dissection** — immediate mortality; can also be a complication of TAK itself.
2. **Infectious aortitis** — tuberculous, syphilitic, *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*. **The most important exclusion in the entire pathway**, because high-dose glucocorticoids plus a DMARD or biologic in infectious aortitis can be lethal, and tuberculous aortitis is geographically co-distributed with TAK.
3. **Infective endocarditis** with embolic and immune phenomena — fever, murmur, raised inflammatory markers, arterial occlusions. Overlaps closely with TAK's systemic phase.
4. **Ruptured or expanding aortic aneurysm** — any cause.
5. **Cardioembolic source** (myxoma, LV thrombus) producing multi-territory arterial occlusion.
6. **Antiphospholipid syndrome** — arterial thrombosis in a young woman with raised inflammatory markers; management is anticoagulation, not immunosuppression alone.
:::

**B. Common differentials — ranked by prevalence** [E]

1. **Premature atherosclerosis** — familial hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes, CKD, post-transplant.
2. **Fibromuscular dysplasia** — young women, renal and carotid arteries, hypertension. Named by EULAR as a specific differential in refractory disease ^[1].
3. **Large-vessel giant cell arteritis** — the principal mimic over 50.
4. **Behçet's disease** — vascular Behçet's causes aneurysms and both arterial and venous thrombosis.
5. **IgG4-related disease** — inflammatory aortitis and periaortitis.
6. **Spondyloarthritis-associated aortitis** — aortic root disease and AR.
7. **Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger's)** — named by EULAR as a differential in refractory disease ^[1].
8. **Cogan syndrome** — aortitis with audiovestibular and ocular inflammation.
9. **Congenital mid-aortic syndrome and coarctation.**
10. **Neurofibromatosis type 1 vasculopathy** · **radiation arteritis** · **relapsing polychondritis** · **sarcoidosis** · **Erdheim-Chester disease** · **heritable thoracic aortopathies** (Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, vascular Ehlers-Danlos).

### 5. For each differential — the discriminating question and sign

| Differential | Ask | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Aortic dissection | Was the pain maximal the instant it started, and did it move? | Inter-arm BP difference *with* a new AR murmur and abrupt-onset pulse deficit |
| Infectious aortitis | Any TB contact, prior TB treatment, cough, or high-risk sexual history? | **Saccular** rather than concentric aortic lesion; rigor-associated fever spike |
| Infective endocarditis | Any dental or instrumentation procedure, injecting drug use, prosthetic valve? | **Changing** murmur plus splinter haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions |
| Antiphospholipid syndrome | Prior venous thrombosis or recurrent pregnancy loss? | Livedo reticularis |
| Premature atherosclerosis | Smoking, family history of early coronary disease, high cholesterol? | Tendon xanthomata, xanthelasma, corneal arcus in youth |
| Fibromuscular dysplasia | Is there **any** systemic symptom — fever, weight loss, night sweats? | Epigastric bruit **with normal ESR/CRP** and no aortic wall thickening |
| Large-vessel GCA | Age at onset; new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual loss? | Temporal artery thickening, tenderness, or pulselessness |
| Behçet's | Recurrent painful oral **and** genital ulceration? | Oral aphthae, genital scarring, pathergy |
| IgG4-related disease | Salivary or lacrimal swelling, pancreatitis, jaundice? | Bilateral submandibular gland enlargement |
| Spondyloarthritis | Inflammatory back pain, morning stiffness >30 min, improves with movement? | Reduced Schober, reduced chest expansion |
| Buerger's disease | How many cigarettes, how long; fingers and toes rather than arm or calf? | Distal digital ischaemia and superficial thrombophlebitis with **palpable proximal pulses** |
| Cogan syndrome | Hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo with red painful eyes? | Sensorineural hearing loss with interstitial keratitis |
| Heritable aortopathy | Family history of dissection, sudden death, aneurysm? | Arachnodactyly, high-arched palate, hypermobility, translucent skin |
| Neurofibromatosis 1 | Family history and childhood skin lesions? | ≥6 café-au-lait macules, axillary freckling, neurofibromas, Lisch nodules |
| Radiation arteritis | Prior radiotherapy, and to which field? | Stenosis confined precisely to a radiation field, with overlying telangiectasia |

### 6. Investigations — what, in what sequence, and why

**Step 1 — bedside, costs nothing, do it first** [E]
- **Blood pressure in all four limbs.** Identifies the inter-arm gradient (a criteria item), identifies coarctation physiology, and prevents the central-hypertension trap.
- **Systematic pulse palpation and bruit auscultation** at every site listed above.

**Step 2 — first-line laboratory** [E]
- **ESR and CRP** — elevated during active disease. **Critical limitation:** acute-phase reactants imperfectly correlate with disease activity on histopathology ^[13]. Normal ESR and CRP do **not** exclude active TAK, and elevated values do not confirm it.
- **Full blood count** — normochromic normocytic anaemia and reactive thrombocytosis.
- **Albumin** — low, from the acute-phase shift.
- **Creatinine, eGFR, urinalysis** — renal artery involvement, hypertensive nephropathy, and to exclude a glomerulopathy pointing elsewhere.
- **Liver function** — baseline before methotrexate or leflunomide.

::: red "Step 3 — mandatory exclusion panel before any immunosuppression"
**Blood cultures, at least three sets** — endocarditis and infectious aortitis ·
**IGRA or tuberculin skin test plus chest radiograph** — required both to exclude
tuberculous aortitis and as a prerequisite before TNF inhibitors or tocilizumab; in a
TB-endemic setting this is not optional · **syphilis serology** · **HIV serology** ·
**serum IgG4** · **ANA, ANCA, antiphospholipid antibodies** — to redirect toward an
alternative vasculitis or thrombophilia.

**There is no diagnostic autoantibody for TAK.** Anti-endothelial cell antibodies are a
research tool, not a clinical test. [L]
:::

**Step 4 — vascular imaging, the diagnostic centre of gravity** [E]

- **MRI/MRA of the aorta and its branches with contrast** — shows **wall thickening and mural oedema** as well as the lumen, without ionising radiation; decisive in young women needing repeated imaging over decades. Expect long-segment concentric wall thickening, mural oedema and post-contrast enhancement in active disease, smooth tapering stenoses. Less good for calcification; gadolinium considerations in CKD.
- **CT angiography** — rapid, widely available, best spatial resolution; distinguishes calcified atherosclerotic plaque from inflammatory thickening; double-ring enhancement of the thickened wall. Limitation: **cumulative radiation** across a lifetime of surveillance in a young woman, plus iodinated contrast.
- **Colour Doppler ultrasound** of carotid, subclavian, axillary, vertebral — cheap, radiation-free, repeatable; the **"macaroni sign"** of homogeneous, circumferential, mid-echoic wall thickening. Operator-dependent; cannot assess the thoracic aorta or pulmonary arteries.
- **¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT** — assesses **metabolic activity** rather than anatomy; helps in the subset with normal acute-phase reactants ^[13], precisely the group in which clinical assessment fails. Cannot reliably distinguish inflammatory from atherosclerotic uptake; radiation; cost; availability.
- **Transthoracic echocardiography** — mandatory, not optional. Aortic root diameter, AR severity, LV size and function, pulmonary pressures, proximal coronary origins where visible.
- **Catheter angiography** — now largely reserved for planning or performing intervention.

#### Diagnostic performance — what is and is not sourceable

::: violet "2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria [G]"
**Absolute entry requirements:** age ≤60 years at diagnosis **and** imaging evidence of
large-vessel vasculitis, with classification at a cumulative score of **≥5 points** on a
scale of 0–19 ^[3,5].

**Derivation and internal validation:** sensitivity 89.9% and specificity 96.6% in the
development data set (639 patients, 316 with TAK); sensitivity 93.8% (95% CI 88.6–97.1),
specificity 99.2% (95% CI 96.7–100.0), AUC 0.97 in validation (273 patients, 146 with TAK) ^[3].

**In independent external validation the specificity collapses:** among 504 patients with
TAK and 222 controls including 144 with extracranial GCA, the 2022 criteria had better
sensitivity (95.83% vs 82.94%) and NPV than the 1990 ACR criteria, but poorer specificity
(**63.51% vs 90.54%**), PPV, LR+, LR− and AUC ^[4].
:::

::: key "The lesson to internalise"
A specificity of 99.2% in the derivation cohort and 63.51% against a real-world comparator
group dominated by large-vessel GCA is the difference between a research instrument and a
diagnostic test. This is exactly why EULAR states that classification criteria must not
substitute for clinical diagnosis ^[1]. **Do not use a score of ≥5 to diagnose a patient.**
:::

**For individual imaging modalities:** **no reliable sensitivity, specificity, or likelihood
ratio figures were located** in this search [?]. Do not quote any. If you need them, the
source to interrogate is a dedicated imaging-accuracy systematic review, not a general guideline.

#### Assessing disease activity — separate from diagnosis

- **NIH (Kerr) criteria, 1994** — active disease if at least two of four features are **new or worse** since the last assessment: (1) new or worsening systemic features such as fever or musculoskeletal symptoms; (2) new or worsening elevated ESR; (3) new or worsening features of vascular ischaemia or inflammation — claudication, diminished or absent pulse, bruit, carotidynia, asymmetric blood pressure in upper or lower limbs; (4) new or worsening angiographic features ^[12]. The ESR threshold used was >20 mm/h ^[13]. [E]
- **ITAS2010** — a 44-item clinical score derived from DEI.TAK and ultimately from BVAS, scoring only abnormalities **new or worsening within the past three months and attributable to active vasculitis**; ITAS-A adds acute-phase reactants ^[13]. [E]

::: red "Their shared and disqualifying weakness"
These are clinical scores that miss silent anatomical progression. During follow-up,
**14 of 15 patients showing vascular progression on imaging were categorised as having
inactive disease according to ITAS2010** ^[13]. [L]

**This single finding is the entire justification for routine imaging surveillance.** A
patient can be clinically quiescent, have a normal ESR, score zero on ITAS2010, and still
be losing arterial lumen.
:::

A subsequently derived tool combining high ESR (3 points), NIH criterion 1 (2 points),
NIH criterion 4 (4 points), and carotidynia (3 points), with total **≥5** indicating active
disease, achieved AUC 89.37 versus NIH criteria 77.96, ITAS2010 66.12, ITAS-ESR 75.58, and
ITAS-CRP 71.34, with AUC 85.23 in validation ^[12]. [L] — single-centre derivation and
validation (N=100 and N=46), not in guideline use. Know it exists; do not adopt it unilaterally.

### 7. Gold standard for definitive diagnosis

::: teal "There is no single gold standard, and this must be stated rather than finessed"
Histology — granulomatous panarteritis with medial destruction and giant cells — is
definitive **when tissue is available**, but tissue is almost never available, since biopsy
of the aorta or its primary branches is only obtained incidentally at open vascular surgery.

In practice the diagnostic standard is: **a compatible clinical picture, plus imaging
demonstration of characteristic large-vessel wall thickening and stenosis, plus exclusion of
infection and the mimics above.** EULAR's overarching principle is that diagnosis rests on
clinical signs and symptoms, with imaging or biopsy confirmation required for suspected GCA
and TAK ^[1]. [G]

In research and trials, physician global assessment has served as the reference standard for
activity ^[12] — that is, expert opinion. Be honest with yourself about what that means for
the strength of the evidence base underneath *Treatment*.
:::

### 8. Diagnostic algorithm

1. **Trigger the thought.** A patient under 40 with unexplained inflammatory illness; a young hypertensive patient; an absent or asymmetric pulse; an arterial bruit in a young person; stroke, MI, or claudication under 40; a young woman with an unexplained raised ESR.
2. **Bedside:** four-limb BP; full pulse and bruit survey; cardiac examination; fundoscopy.
3. **First-line bloods:** ESR, CRP, FBC, albumin, creatinine, urinalysis, LFT.
4. **Exclusion panel in parallel, not afterwards:** blood cultures ×3, IGRA or TST plus chest radiograph, syphilis serology, HIV, IgG4, ANA, ANCA, antiphospholipid antibodies.
5. **Vascular imaging of the whole aorta and its primary branches** — MRA preferred in the young; CTA if MRA unavailable or contraindicated. **Image the whole aorta, not only the symptomatic territory**, because skip lesions and silent disease are the rule.
6. **Echocardiography in every patient** — root, valve, ventricle, pulmonary pressures.
7. **If imaging is equivocal or acute-phase reactants are discordant:** consider FDG-PET/CT.
8. **Synthesise.** Assign a Hata/Numano type, document C+ and P+ involvement, and record a baseline activity assessment (NIH criteria and ITAS2010) *and* a baseline damage map. **Activity and damage are separate axes and must be tracked separately.**
9. **Confirm infection is excluded before the first dose of glucocorticoid.** Unlike GCA, TAK is not a treat-first-confirm-later disease: in PMR or TAK, glucocorticoid initiation can await confirmed diagnosis; GCA is a medical emergency and a glucocorticoid trial is not a diagnostic test ^[1]. [G]
10. **Refer.** Suspected TAK should be referred to a specialist with appropriate expertise ^[1]. [G]

### 9. If a skin lesion is present

TAK is not primarily dermatological, but two cutaneous associations occur and are
examinable [L] (reported in cohort series; frequency not reliably established).

**Erythema nodosum** — *Primary morphology:* tender, deep-seated, erythematous to violaceous
**nodules**, 1–5 cm, without ulceration. *Secondary change:* resolves through bruise-like
colour change (erythema contusiforme); no scarring. *Configuration:* discrete, scattered.
*Distribution:* bilateral **anterior shins**, extensor surfaces. *Symmetry:* symmetrical.
*Surface change:* none — smooth, intact epidermis; the pathology is septal panniculitis in
the subcutis.

**Pyoderma gangrenosum** — *Primary morphology:* begins as a tender pustule or nodule.
*Secondary change:* rapidly enlarging **ulcer** with a violaceous, undermined, overhanging
border and a purulent necrotic base; heals with cribriform scarring. *Configuration:*
solitary or few; may show pathergy at sites of trauma. *Distribution:* most often lower
legs. *Symmetry:* asymmetrical. *Surface change:* frank ulceration with exudate.

::: red "Do not debride pyoderma gangrenosum"
Pathergy worsens it. This is a common and damaging error.
:::

## Sixth: Natural history

**Untreated trajectory** [E] — not a self-limiting disease. The typical course is
**relapsing–remitting with progressive anatomical damage**, over years to decades. Sequence:
inflammatory phase → progressive concentric stenosis → collateral development (which delays
and masks symptoms) → target organ consequences. Because collaterals are efficient in a
young patient, **anatomical severity outruns symptoms**, and the first clinical event may be
a stroke, a myocardial infarction, or a hypertensive emergency. Death, when it occurs, is
predominantly cardiovascular ^[11]. Spontaneous permanent remission of inflammation
("burnt-out" disease) does occur, but the accumulated stenosis and aneurysm do not reverse.
**Remission is not recovery.**

**Treated trajectory** — most patients achieve clinical and biochemical remission on
glucocorticoids. **Relapse on tapering is common, and glucocorticoid dependence is a defining
problem of the disease** — why EULAR recommends a non-biologic DMARD for **all** TAK patients
rather than for selected ones ^[1]. [E]

::: key "The central and most clinically important fact"
Anatomical progression continues in a substantial minority of patients who appear to be in
clinical and biochemical remission ^[13]. [L] **Clinical quiescence is not evidence of arrested
disease.** Long-term outcome is determined less by control of inflammation than by the damage
already sustained at diagnosis — hence the value of early recognition and the cost of
diagnostic delay.
:::

**No reliable pooled figure for remission rate, relapse rate, or survival was located** [?].
Cohort estimates vary widely by era, region, and treatment. Do not quote one.

### Complications of the disease

|---|---|---|---|

| Vascular | Aneurysm, dissection, rupture | Medial elastic destruction | Years; can be early in aneurysmal phenotype |
| Cardiac | Aortic regurgitation, LV volume overload, heart failure | Aortic root dilatation | Years |
| Cardiac | Angina, myocardial infarction | Coronary ostial stenosis | Any time; may be the presentation |
| Cardiac | Pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure | Pulmonary artery stenosis | Years; frequently missed |

| Renal | Chronic kidney disease | Renal artery stenosis plus hypertensive nephrosclerosis | Years |

### Complications of treatment [E]

**Glucocorticoids** — the dominant iatrogenic burden, precisely because these are young
patients facing years of exposure.
- *Early (weeks to months):* hyperglycaemia and steroid-induced diabetes, hypertension, weight gain and Cushingoid change, mood disturbance and insomnia, infection.
- *Intermediate (months):* osteoporosis, myopathy, cataract, glaucoma, HPA suppression, poor wound healing.
- *Late (months to years):* avascular necrosis of the femoral head, permanent adrenal insufficiency risk on abrupt withdrawal, growth suppression in children.
- Limiting this exposure is an explicit overarching aim of the ACR/VF guideline ^[15].

**Methotrexate** — hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression, mucositis, rarely pneumonitis;
**teratogenic**, requires effective contraception and cessation before conception in both sexes.

**Azathioprine** — myelosuppression (test **TPMT** and, in East and South Asian populations,
**NUDT15** before starting), hepatotoxicity, hypersensitivity, long-term malignancy risk;
interacts catastrophically with allopurinol.

**Leflunomide** — hepatotoxicity, hypertension, diarrhoea, peripheral neuropathy;
**teratogenic with a very long half-life** requiring cholestyramine washout.

**Mycophenolate mofetil** — cytopenias, diarrhoea, infection; **teratogenic**.

**Cyclophosphamide** — infertility, haemorrhagic cystitis, bladder malignancy,
myelosuppression, infection. Now rarely used in TAK; reserve for organ-threatening
refractory disease under specialist direction.

**TNF inhibitors** — **tuberculosis reactivation** (screen before starting, without
exception), hepatitis B reactivation, serious bacterial infection, demyelination,
drug-induced lupus.

::: red "Tocilizumab — a monitoring hazard, not merely a toxicity"
IL-6 receptor blockade **abolishes the CRP response**, so CRP becomes uninformative for
both disease activity **and** intercurrent infection. **A patient on tocilizumab can have a
normal CRP with a perforated colon.**

Other effects: neutropenia, transaminitis, dyslipidaemia, gastrointestinal perforation
(higher risk with diverticular disease), injection-site or infusion reactions.
:::

**Vascular intervention** — restenosis, anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, graft occlusion,
periprocedural stroke. Open interventions carry higher primary patency rates; endovascular
approaches carry lower stroke risk ^[1]. [G] Restenosis is markedly more likely if
intervention is performed during active disease — the entire reason for the timing
recommendation below.

## Eighth: Treatment

::: teal "Rule B6 declaration — two bodies, and they are not identical"
**According to the 2025 EULAR recommendations for the management of polymyalgia rheumatica
and primary large vessel vasculitis** (presented at the EULAR 2026 congress, published in
*Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases*, superseding the 2018 EULAR LVV recommendations) ^[1,2]…

**and according to the 2021 ACR/Vasculitis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Giant
Cell Arteritis and Takayasu Arteritis** ^[6,7,15]…
:::

::: amber "Rule B7 — guideline divergence, named explicitly [G]"
**1. DMARD for all versus DMARD for some.** EULAR 2025 states that non-biologic DMARDs
should be given to **all** patients with TAK ^[1]. The ACR/VF 2021 framing is initial
treatment with high-dose oral glucocorticoids for active severe TAK, with IV pulse
glucocorticoids followed by high-dose oral glucocorticoids for life- or organ-threatening
disease ^[6], with non-glucocorticoid immunosuppression added. **I could not retrieve the
exact ACR/VF wording on universal DMARD use.** Treat the EULAR position as the more recent
and more universal; verify the ACR/VF text before relying on the contrast.

**2. Imaging in monitoring.** EULAR 2025 makes routine imaging for **both activity and
damage** mandatory in all TAK patients, and states that relapse diagnosis in TAK is
substantially based on imaging in conjunction with clinical and laboratory assessment —
distinguishing it from PMR and GCA where imaging plays a more supportive role ^[1].

**3. Imaging in diagnosis more broadly.** The ACR/VF guideline explicitly notes that its
recommendations on temporal artery imaging differ from EULAR's ^[15]. The underlying
difference — US preference for histology, European preference for imaging as sufficient —
colours both documents.

**4. Upadacitinib.** EULAR 2025 adds upadacitinib as an option **in GCA only**;
for TAK the recommendation is tocilizumab or TNF inhibitors for relapsing or refractory
disease despite conventional DMARD therapy ^[1]. **Do not extrapolate upadacitinib from GCA
to TAK.** The evidence base does not support it and the guideline deliberately did not.
:::

::: amber "Which position will your local practice follow? (Rules B7, B8)"
If you are training in South or East Asia, the Middle East, or Africa, local practice is
more likely to align with the **EULAR structure** (glucocorticoid plus a conventional DMARD
in everyone, escalating to a biologic only if available), because the ACR/VF pathway's
conditional recommendations assume ready access to tocilizumab and TNF inhibitors. The
Chinese guideline ^[9] is a further regionally adapted option. **Confirm with your senior
registrar which document your unit works from before you write anything on a drug chart.**
:::

### Pharmacological therapy

#### Glucocorticoid — the backbone [G]

**Prednisolone** — systemic glucocorticoid; binds the cytosolic glucocorticoid receptor,
which translocates to the nucleus and transrepresses NF-κB and AP-1, suppressing
transcription of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and adhesion molecules.

- **Dosage:** in newly diagnosed active TAK, **40–60 mg/day**, tapered to **15–20 mg/day within 2–3 months**, with the goal of stopping **within 12–18 months** ^[1]. [G]
- **Relapse:** major relapses are managed as new-onset disease; minor relapses require escalation to at least the last effective dose with individualised tapering ^[1].
- **Organ- or life-threatening disease:** ACR/VF conditionally supports IV pulse glucocorticoids followed by high-dose oral glucocorticoids ^[6].
- **Children:** alternative steroid dosing regimens, for example IV pulse glucocorticoids with low daily oral dosing, may be preferred ^[6] — the rationale being growth suppression.
- **Contraindications:** no absolute contraindication in organ-threatening vasculitis, but **untreated systemic infection, active tuberculosis, and untreated infectious aortitis are effectively absolute** until treated. Relative: poorly controlled diabetes, severe osteoporosis, active peptic ulceration, psychosis.
- **Duration:** aiming for 12–18 months; in practice a substantial proportion remain glucocorticoid-dependent.
- **Co-prescribe from day one:** calcium and vitamin D, bone protection assessment, gastroprotection where indicated, and *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis consideration if on high-dose glucocorticoid plus another immunosuppressant.
- **Never stop abruptly** after more than roughly three weeks of suppressive dosing — HPA axis suppression risks adrenal crisis.

#### Conventional synthetic DMARDs — in all patients per EULAR 2025 [G]

Comparative efficacy between agents has **not** been established by adequate trials ^[2]. [L]

**Methotrexate** — folate antagonist; inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and, more relevantly
at low dose, AICAR transformylase, raising extracellular adenosine with anti-inflammatory
effect. *Contraindications:* pregnancy and conception planning, significant liver disease,
alcohol excess, severe renal impairment, cytopenias. *Dosage:* once-weekly oral or
subcutaneous, titrated upward, **with mandatory folic acid on a different day**. [E]
*Adverse effects:* nausea, mucositis, transaminitis, cytopenias, rarely pneumonitis.
*Monitoring:* FBC, LFT, creatinine at baseline then per local protocol. *Duration:* continued
through remission; withdrawal usually precipitates relapse.

::: red "Fatal error to avoid"
**Never prescribe methotrexate daily. Weekly dosing only.**
:::

**Azathioprine** — purine analogue; 6-thioguanine nucleotides incorporate into DNA and
inhibit lymphocyte proliferation. *Contraindications:* TPMT deficiency, concurrent
allopurinol or febuxostat without dose reduction, prior hypersensitivity. *Dosage:*
weight-based daily oral. **Check TPMT, and NUDT15 in East and South Asian patients, before
the first dose.** [E] *Adverse effects:* myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity, hypersensitivity
syndrome, long-term lymphoma and non-melanoma skin cancer risk. **Preferred conventional
agent in pregnancy planning** among the cytotoxics, being the least teratogenic of this group. [E]

**Mycophenolate mofetil** — inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, selectively
depleting guanosine nucleotides in lymphocytes. *Contraindications:* pregnancy
(**teratogenic — high-grade risk**), prior intolerance. *Adverse effects:* diarrhoea,
cytopenias, infection.

**Leflunomide** — inhibits dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, blocking pyrimidine synthesis in
activated lymphocytes. *Contraindications:* pregnancy (teratogenic, long half-life requiring
cholestyramine washout), significant liver disease. *Adverse effects:* diarrhoea,
hypertension, hepatotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, alopecia.

**Cyclophosphamide** — reserved for organ-threatening refractory disease; specialist use
only. Fertility preservation must be discussed before the first dose in a young patient.

#### Biologics — for relapsing or refractory disease [G]

For relapsing or refractory disease despite conventional DMARD therapy, **tocilizumab or
TNF inhibitors** should be considered ^[1].

**Tocilizumab** — humanised monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor; blocks both
membrane-bound and soluble IL-6R signalling, interrupting the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 axis that drives
the acute-phase response and Th17 differentiation. *Contraindications:* active serious
infection, active tuberculosis, neutropenia below local threshold, significant transaminitis.
Relative: diverticular disease. *Route:* intravenous or subcutaneous. *Monitoring hazard:*
see red box above — **CRP cannot be used to detect either flare or infection on this drug.**
Monitor with symptoms, examination, and imaging. *Duration:* while effective; optimal duration
unestablished [?].

**TNF inhibitors — infliximab or adalimumab** — bind soluble and membrane TNF-α, preventing
engagement of TNFR1/TNFR2 and interrupting macrophage activation and granuloma maintenance.
*Contraindications:* **active or latent untreated tuberculosis** (screen before every course,
without exception), active serious infection, untreated hepatitis B, moderate-to-severe heart
failure (NYHA III–IV), demyelinating disease. *Monitoring:* TB and hepatitis screening at
baseline; FBC, LFT; clinical vigilance for infection.

**Upadacitinib** — **not recommended for TAK.** EULAR 2025 introduced it for GCA only ^[1].
Do not extrapolate.

#### Adjunctive cardiovascular therapy [G] for the principle ^[1]; [E] for the pharmacology

::: red "The two antihypertensive errors that cause harm"
**1. Treating to the reading from an arm supplied by a stenosed subclavian artery** — this
systematically underestimates central pressure and leaves severe hypertension untreated.
Always use the highest of the four limb pressures, and where all four are compromised, obtain
central pressure.

**2. ACE inhibitors or ARBs in bilateral renal artery stenosis or stenosis to a single
functioning kidney** — angiotensin II is maintaining the glomerular filtration gradient
through efferent arteriolar constriction; blocking it precipitates acute kidney injury. Check
renal artery anatomy before prescribing, and check creatinine within days of starting.

**Calcium channel blockers** (e.g. amlodipine) are usually a safer first choice where renal
artery anatomy is uncertain or bilateral disease is present.
:::

- **Statin** — for cardiovascular risk reduction in a population with accelerated arterial disease and long glucocorticoid exposure. [G] for the principle of CV risk mitigation ^[1].
- **Aspirin** — often used, particularly with cerebrovascular or coronary involvement. **The evidence base in TAK specifically is weak; I could not retrieve a guideline recommendation on antiplatelet therapy in TAK.** Do not present it as guideline-mandated. [L]

### Surgical and procedural therapy [G]

::: teal "Timing is the single most important principle"
Elective vascular interventions should be performed **only during stable remission**, with
urgent vascular referral for dissection or critical ischaemia ^[1].

ACR/VF concurs: for worsening signs of limb or organ ischaemia, delay surgical intervention
until the disease is quiescent rather than operating during active disease; and for patients
with active TAK undergoing vascular surgical intervention for a disease complication,
periprocedural high-dose glucocorticoids are conditionally recommended ^[6].

**Modality trade-off to state to the patient:** open interventions carry higher primary
patency rates; endovascular approaches carry lower stroke risk ^[1].
:::

::: amber "Where ACR/VF prefers medical management over intervention [G]"
**Renovascular hypertension with renal artery stenosis** — conditionally recommend medical
management over surgical intervention. **Stenosis of a cranial or cervical vessel without
clinical symptoms** — conditionally recommend medical management over surgical intervention ^[6].

**An asymptomatic carotid stenosis in TAK is not automatically an indication to intervene.**
This runs against instinct and is worth remembering.
:::

**Genuine intervention indications** [E] — critical or threatened limb ischaemia; symptomatic
cerebrovascular stenosis with recurrent events despite medical therapy; severe
coarctation-like aortic narrowing with uncontrollable hypertension; renal artery stenosis with
refractory hypertension or deteriorating renal function after medical therapy has failed;
aneurysm meeting size or growth thresholds; severe aortic regurgitation; symptomatic coronary
ostial stenosis.

**Emergency indications requiring immediate referral** [G] — arterial vessel dissection or
critical vascular ischaemia requires urgent vascular team referral ^[1].

### Physiotherapy and rehabilitation [L]

- Structured aerobic exercise and cardiac rehabilitation where cardiac or cerebrovascular involvement exists. **No TAK-specific rehabilitation trial evidence was located** [?] — extrapolated from general cardiovascular rehabilitation.
- Avoid heavy isometric and Valsalva-generating exertion where there is significant aortic dilatation, aneurysm, or severe uncontrolled hypertension, since these produce abrupt rises in aortic wall stress. [E]
- Resistance and weight-bearing exercise for glucocorticoid-induced bone loss and myopathy. [E]
- Supervised exercise therapy for claudication where the limiting lesion is not critical. [E]

### Lifestyle modification and patient counselling [G]

- Screen for cardiovascular comorbidities, with prophylactic and lifestyle interventions to mitigate risk ^[1] — blood pressure, lipids, glucose, weight, smoking.
- **Absolute smoking cessation.** Smoking compounds arterial damage and is the discriminating feature of the main mimic (Buerger's).
- Patients require access to disease education including warning symptoms and treatment complications ^[1]. Teach explicitly: symptoms of stroke and TIA; new or changed chest pain; sudden severe tearing chest or back pain; new limb pain, pallor, or coldness; fever while immunosuppressed.
- **Steroid card and adrenal crisis education.** Never stop glucocorticoid abruptly; increase during intercurrent illness per local sick-day rules.
- **Contraception and pregnancy planning.** Methotrexate, mycophenolate, and leflunomide are teratogenic. Pregnancy must be planned during remission, on a compatible drug, with multidisciplinary obstetric and rheumatology input.
- **Vaccination.** Complete inactivated vaccinations before immunosuppression where possible; **live vaccines are contraindicated** on significant immunosuppression.
- **Tuberculosis screening before any biologic**, repeated per local policy.

### Monitoring [G]

Regular monitoring should incorporate symptoms, clinical findings, acute phase markers, and
imaging throughout the disease course, with imaging used to assess **both activity and damage**
in all TAK patients ^[1].

- **Every visit:** symptom review against the NIH criterion domains; **four-limb blood pressure**; pulse and bruit survey; cardiac examination; weight; glucocorticoid toxicity review.
- **Laboratory:** ESR and CRP — **imperfectly correlated with histological activity** ^[13] and **uninterpretable on tocilizumab**. Plus drug-specific monitoring (FBC, LFT, creatinine).
- **Imaging:** routinely, per EULAR, with modality chosen for the accessible territory and cumulative radiation minimised in young patients — Doppler ultrasound for accessible neck and arm vessels, MRA for the aorta, reserving CTA and PET/CT for specific questions.
- **Echocardiography:** serially, tracking aortic root diameter, AR severity, LV function, pulmonary pressures.
- **Targets:** absence of new or worsening symptoms; normalised acute-phase reactants where interpretable; **no anatomical progression on imaging**; blood pressure to guideline target measured from a valid limb; glucocorticoid at or below the tapering trajectory.

### Expected response and the meaning of non-response [G]

**Expected:** systemic symptoms and acute-phase reactants improve within days to weeks of
adequate glucocorticoid. Vascular wall oedema and enhancement regress over weeks to months.
**Established stenosis does not reverse** — do not expect it to, and do not interpret
persistent stenosis as treatment failure.

::: key "Non-response means the diagnosis must be revisited, not that the dose should be escalated"
Refractory TAK requires diagnostic re-evaluation, with differentials including **Buerger's
disease and fibromuscular dysplasia**; refractory PMR, GCA or TAK should have the diagnosis
re-evaluated and be considered for referral to a specialist centre ^[1].

Also reconsider: undiagnosed **infectious aortitis** (the treatment is antimicrobial, and
immunosuppression is making it worse), non-adherence, and progression of fixed damage being
misread as active inflammation.
:::

### Rule B8 — local applicability and practical substitutions

| Unavailable | Practical alternative that is actually available |
|---|---|
| Tocilizumab | Prednisolone plus methotrexate or azathioprine, optimised to maximum tolerated dose; consider leflunomide or mycophenolate before conceding refractoriness |
| TNF inhibitors | As above. If a biologic can be obtained at all, biosimilar infliximab is usually the cheapest route |
| ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT | Serial colour Doppler ultrasound of carotid, subclavian, and axillary arteries plus ESR/CRP; CTA at longer intervals for the aorta |
| MRA | CTA — accepting the radiation trade-off and explicitly documenting cumulative dose in a young woman |
| High-sensitivity CRP | ESR alone is acceptable for trend monitoring; the NIH criteria were built around ESR >20 mm/h ^[13] |
| TPMT/NUDT15 genotyping | Start azathioprine at a low dose with intensive early FBC monitoring; the NUDT15 variant is common in East and South Asian populations, so this substitution carries real risk and should be a senior decision |
| Central aortic pressure measurement | Four-limb cuff pressure, using the highest value; document the limitation |
| Specialist vascular surgery and hybrid theatre | Early referral and transfer planning; **do not attempt elective intervention locally during active disease** |

::: amber "Additionally, in a tuberculosis-endemic setting"
The threshold for excluding tuberculous aortitis before immunosuppression must be **higher,
not lower**, than in the guideline text — and IGRA availability is often the limiting factor.
Where IGRA is unavailable, tuberculin skin testing plus chest radiography plus a deliberate
clinical assessment is the fallback, and this specific decision should involve a senior.
:::

## Ninth: Red flags

### Symptoms and signs that must never be dismissed [E]

| Finding | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden severe tearing chest or interscapular pain, maximal at onset | **Aortic dissection** | Immediate imaging and vascular/cardiothoracic referral |
| New focal neurological deficit or amaurosis | Stroke or TIA from carotid/vertebral disease or embolism | Stroke pathway activation |
| Dizziness or drop attacks on arm exertion | Subclavian steal with posterior circulation compromise | Urgent vascular imaging |
| New or crescendo chest pain in a patient under 40 with TAK | **Coronary ostial stenosis; myocardial infarction** | ECG, troponin, ACS pathway |
| Rest pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, cold limb | **Acute or critical limb ischaemia** | Urgent vascular referral |
| BP ≥180/120 with headache, visual change, chest pain, breathlessness, or confusion | Hypertensive emergency, usually renovascular | Immediate assessment for target organ damage |
| Rapidly enlarging pulsatile abdominal mass with pain | **Aneurysm expansion or rupture** | Immediate imaging and surgical referral |
| New early diastolic murmur, widening pulse pressure, breathlessness | Progressive aortic regurgitation from root dilatation | Urgent echocardiography |
| Fever on glucocorticoid plus a DMARD or biologic | **Serious infection, and the inflammatory response may be blunted** | Treat as neutropenic-sepsis-equivalent until proven otherwise |
| Fever plus new murmur | Infective endocarditis, or infectious aortitis | Blood cultures before antibiotics; **do not escalate immunosuppression** |
| Breathlessness with raised JVP and loud P2 | Pulmonary artery involvement with pulmonary hypertension | Echocardiography; specialist referral |
| Progressive visual loss | Hypoperfusive or hypertensive retinopathy | Urgent ophthalmology |
| Worsening hypertension or proteinuria in pregnancy | Pre-eclampsia superimposed on TAK | Urgent obstetric assessment |

### Drug contraindications capable of killing the patient

::: red "The twelve [E]"
1. **High-dose glucocorticoids or a biologic in undiagnosed infectious aortitis, active tuberculosis, or infective endocarditis.** Disseminated infection under immunosuppression. **Exclude infection before the first dose.** This is why TAK, unlike GCA, is not treated before the diagnosis is confirmed ^[1].
2. **TNF inhibitor without prior tuberculosis screening.** Reactivation with miliary or disseminated TB.
3. **ACE inhibitor or ARB in bilateral renal artery stenosis, or stenosis to a single functioning kidney.** AKI from loss of the angiotensin II-dependent filtration gradient.
4. **Abrupt cessation of glucocorticoid after prolonged therapy.** Adrenal crisis.
5. **Methotrexate prescribed daily instead of weekly.** Fatal pancytopenia and mucositis.
6. **Azathioprine with allopurinol or febuxostat without dose reduction.** Xanthine oxidase inhibition blocks 6-mercaptopurine catabolism → profound myelosuppression.
7. **Azathioprine at full dose in undetected TPMT or NUDT15 deficiency.** Profound myelosuppression.
8. **Live vaccine during significant immunosuppression.** Disseminated vaccine-strain infection.
9. **Methotrexate, mycophenolate, or leflunomide in pregnancy or without effective contraception.** Teratogenicity and fetal loss.
10. **Treating blood pressure to the reading from a stenosed limb.** Either under-treatment of severe central hypertension, or iatrogenic central hypotension with cerebral and renal hypoperfusion.
11. **Elective vascular intervention during active disease.** Restenosis, anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, graft failure. Contraindicated by both bodies ^[1,6].
12. **Debriding pyoderma gangrenosum.** Pathergy with catastrophic wound extension.
:::

### Time-critical windows

- **Aortic dissection:** immediate. Mortality rises with every hour untreated. [E]
- **Acute ischaemic stroke:** thrombolysis and thrombectomy windows are measured in hours and are defined by the current AHA/ASA or ESO stroke guideline, **not by any vasculitis guideline** — check the stroke protocol, not this document. [G] by reference.
- **Acute limb ischaemia:** revascularisation within hours; the appearance of paralysis marks probable non-salvageability. [E]
- **STEMI:** primary PCI within the interval specified by the current ACS guideline. [G] by reference.
- **Arterial dissection or critical vascular ischaemia in known TAK:** urgent vascular team referral — **no defined numeric interval given** in the recommendation ^[1].
- **Suspected cranial GCA (for contrast, because you will be asked):** urgent specialist referral within **24 hours**, with glucocorticoid commenced immediately without waiting for confirmatory investigations ^[1]. **TAK does not carry this 24-hour rule** — the distinction is deliberate and examinable.

::: red "Rule B11 — escalation boundary"
Three decisions in this disease should not be made from an AI answer, a textbook, or a
guideline flowchart alone, because the evidence is [L] and being wrong causes irreversible harm:

1. **Whether to start immunosuppression before infection is definitively excluded** in a TB-endemic setting with limited diagnostics.
2. **Whether and when to intervene on a stenosis**, and by which modality — the timing rule is clear, the individual patient's balance of stroke risk against patency is not.
3. **Whether apparent refractoriness is active disease, fixed damage, an alternative diagnosis, or occult infection** — this determines whether the next step is escalation, de-escalation, or antimicrobials, and getting it backwards is dangerous.

**These require a senior decision, not an AI answer.**
:::
