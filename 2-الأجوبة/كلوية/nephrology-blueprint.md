## 1. Definition of the Specialty and Scope of Practice

Nephrology is the subspecialty of internal medicine dedicated to the study, diagnosis, and management of diseases of the kidney. [1] The scope of practice extends far beyond the kidneys themselves, as normal kidney function is essential for maintaining the body's internal milieu. A nephrologist manages the direct consequences of kidney dysfunction, including:

- **Fluid and Electrolyte Disorders:** Disturbances in sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium homeostasis.
- **Acid-Base Disorders:** Metabolic acidosis and alkalosis.
- **Blood Pressure Regulation:** The kidney is the central long-term regulator of blood pressure, making nephrologists experts in managing both essential and secondary hypertension. [2]
- **CKD-Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD):** The complex systemic syndrome of bone and mineral metabolism dysregulation resulting from CKD. [3]
- **Anemia of CKD:** The deficiency of erythropoietin production by failing kidneys. [3]
- **Renal Replacement Therapies (RRT):** The comprehensive management of hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation, including pre- and post-transplant care. [1]

Nephrologists work across a spectrum of clinical settings, from outpatient clinics managing early CKD, resistant hypertension, and proteinuria, to the inpatient ward managing complex AKI, severe electrolyte emergencies, and overseeing inpatient dialysis. They work closely with interventional radiologists, urologists, pathologists, and intensivists. [1]

## 2. The Grand Classification of Kidney Diseases

A systematic approach to nephrology requires a structured classification. Kidney diseases are categorized using a multi-dimensional framework:

### I. By Temporal Profile (Acute vs. Chronic)

- **Acute Kidney Injury (AKI):** A sudden (hours to days) decline in kidney function, defined by a rise in serum creatinine and/or a decrease in urine output. [4]
- **Acute Kidney Disease (AKD):** A state of kidney damage or dysfunction for a duration of 7 to 90 days, bridging the gap between AKI and CKD. [4]
- **Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD):** Abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for >3 months, with implications for health. Staged by GFR and albuminuria level. [3]

### II. By Anatomical Compartment (The "Nephron" Model)

- **Glomerular:** Diseases primarily affecting the glomerular filtration barrier (e.g., glomerulonephritis, diabetic nephropathy). Presents with proteinuria, hematuria, and often hypertension.
- **Tubulointerstitial:** Diseases primarily affecting the tubules and interstitium (e.g., acute tubular necrosis, interstitial nephritis). Presents with electrolyte wasting, reduced concentrating ability.
- **Vascular:** Diseases of the renal macro- or microvasculature (e.g., renal artery stenosis, thrombotic microangiopathy, vasculitis).
- **Collecting System & Ureters:** Obstructive uropathy, infections.

### III. By Etiological Category

- **Congenital/Hereditary:** Cystic diseases, Alport syndrome, Fabry disease.
- **Glomerular (Immunological):** Immune-complex mediated (SLE), antibody-mediated (anti-GBM disease).
- **Metabolic:** Diabetes mellitus, hyperuricemia.
- **Ischemic:** Renovascular disease, hypotension.
- **Toxic:** Drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides), contrast, heavy metals.
- **Infectious/Post-infectious:** Pyelonephritis, post-streptococcal GN.
- **Infiltrative:** Amyloidosis, lymphoma, sarcoidosis.
- **Obstructive:** Stones, tumors, prostatic hypertrophy.

### IV. By Syndromic Presentation (The Clinical Pattern)

- **Nephritic Syndrome:** Abrupt onset of hematuria (dysmorphic RBCs, casts), variable proteinuria (often <3.5g/day), hypertension, and edema.
- **Nephrotic Syndrome:** Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/day in adults), hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia, and lipiduria.
- **Mixed Nephritic-Nephrotic Pattern:** Features of both (e.g., diffuse proliferative lupus nephritis).
- **Asymptomatic Urinary Abnormalities:** Isolated hematuria or proteinuria found on screening.
- **CKD Syndrome:** The progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function manifesting as uremia, anemia, CKD-MBD, and metabolic acidosis.
- **Renal Tubular Defects:** Renal tubular acidosis (RTA), nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, Fanconi syndrome.

## 3. Complete Index of Nephrological Diseases and Conditions

This is the comprehensive map. Each entry is a door to deeper study.

### 3.1 Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) & Acute Kidney Disease (AKD)

**Definition:** A syndrome defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines as any of: a) increase in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 µmol/L) within 48 hours; or b) increase in serum creatinine ≥1.5 times baseline within the last 7 days; or c) urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. [4]

### Classification (by Etiology):

- **A. Pre-renal AKI (Functional):** Due to hypo-perfusion without intrinsic parenchymal damage.
  - Hypovolemia: Hemorrhage, dehydration, GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea).
  - Decreased Effective Circulating Volume: Congestive heart failure, decompensated cirrhosis (hepatorenal syndrome), sepsis, third-spacing (e.g., pancreatitis).
  - Altered Renal Hemodynamics: NSAIDs (inhibit afferent arteriole vasodilation via prostaglandins), ACE inhibitors/ARBs (inhibit efferent arteriole vasoconstriction via angiotensin II), hypercalcemia. [5]
- **B. Intrinsic Renal AKI (Structural):** Damage to the renal parenchyma.
  - **Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN):** The most common cause of hospital-acquired AKI.
    - *Ischemic ATN:* A severe or prolonged pre-renal state. Requires a secondary hit to the tubular cells.
    - *Nephrotoxic ATN:*
      - Endogenous toxins: Myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobin (hemolysis), uric acid (tumor lysis syndrome), light chains (multiple myeloma cast nephropathy).
      - Exogenous toxins: Aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin, radiocontrast media, vancomycin. [5]
  - **Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN):** A hypersensitivity reaction within the renal interstitium.
    - Drug-induced: NSAIDs, penicillins/cephalosporins, proton pump inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors. Characterized by the triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia (present in a minority).
    - Infection-related: Legionella, leptospirosis, streptococci.
    - Systemic diseases: Sarcoidosis, Sjögren's syndrome, IgG4-related disease.
    - Idiopathic: TINU syndrome (Tubulointerstitial Nephritis and Uveitis). [5]
  - **Glomerular Disease:** Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) causing a nephritic AKI (crescentic GN). Types: anti-GBM disease, pauci-immune (ANCA-associated), immune-complex.

### Microvascular Disease:

    - Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TMA): Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS), Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP).
    - Atheroembolic disease (cholesterol emboli): After arterial instrumentation.
    - Malignant hypertension.
    - Scleroderma renal crisis.
- **C. Post-renal AKI (Obstructive):** Obstruction of urine flow.
  - Intrinsic obstruction: Stones, blood clots, sloughed papillae.
  - Extrinsic compression: Tumors (pelvic, retroperitoneal), prostatic hypertrophy, retroperitoneal fibrosis.
  - Functional: Neurogenic bladder. [6]

### 3.2 Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

**Definition:** Abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for >3 months, with implications for health. [3]

### Staging (KDIGO GFR Categories):

- **G1:** GFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73m² (Normal or high) + evidence of kidney damage (albuminuria, abnormal imaging, histology).
- **G2:** GFR 60-89 (Mildly decreased) + evidence of kidney damage.
- **G3a:** GFR 45-59 (Mildly to moderately decreased).
- **G3b:** GFR 30-44 (Moderately to severely decreased).
- **G4:** GFR 15-29 (Severely decreased).
- **G5:** GFR <15 or on dialysis (Kidney failure). [3]

### Staging (KDIGO Albuminuria Categories):

- **A1:** AER <30 mg/day (Normal to mildly increased).
- **A2:** AER 30-300 mg/day (Moderately increased, formerly "microalbuminuria").
- **A3:** AER >300 mg/day (Severely increased, formerly "macroalbuminuria"). [3]

**Etiologies of CKD:** (Any disease below can lead to CKD)

- **Diabetic Kidney Disease (DKD):** The leading cause of CKD and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) worldwide. A clinical diagnosis, not requiring biopsy. Characterized by progressive albuminuria, hypertension, and GFR decline. Often co-exists with diabetic retinopathy. [3]
- **Hypertensive Nephrosclerosis:** Chronic kidney damage from long-standing essential hypertension. Presents with slowly progressive GFR decline and mild proteinuria. A diagnosis of exclusion in a patient with long-standing hypertension.
- **Chronic Glomerulonephritis:** Recurrent or persistent glomerular disease that has not fully resolved. IgA nephropathy is the most common primary GN leading to ESKD.
- **Chronic Tubulointerstitial Nephritis:** Analgesic nephropathy, chronic lithium use, aristolochic acid nephropathy (Balkan endemic nephropathy), heavy metal exposure.
- **Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD):** The most common hereditary cause of ESKD.
- **Obstructive Nephropathy:** Chronic, unrecognized urinary tract obstruction.

**Complications of CKD (The Uremic Syndrome):**

This is the "big picture" of what lost kidney function causes. Every organ system is affected.

- **Fluid & Electrolytes:** Volume overload (edema, pulmonary edema, hypertension); hyperkalemia; hyperphosphatemia; hypocalcemia; hypermagnesemia.
- **Acid-Base:** High-anion gap metabolic acidosis (uremic acidosis).
- **Hematologic:** Normocytic, normochromic anemia (erythropoietin deficiency); platelet dysfunction (uremic bleeding).
- **Cardiovascular:** Hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), accelerated atherosclerosis, heart failure, uremic pericarditis. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in CKD patients. [3]
- **CKD-Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD):** A triad of: 1) Abnormalities of calcium, phosphorus, PTH, vitamin D, and FGF23; 2) Renal osteodystrophy (high-turnover, low-turnover, mixed); 3) Vascular calcification. [3]
- **Neurologic:** Uremic encephalopathy (asterixis, confusion, coma); peripheral neuropathy (restless legs syndrome, sensorimotor polyneuropathy); autonomic neuropathy.
- **Endocrine:** Secondary hyperparathyroidism, decreased libido, sexual dysfunction, growth retardation in children.
- **Dermatologic:** Uremic pruritus, dry skin (xerosis), nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (with gadolinium exposure in advanced CKD), "uremic frost" (historical finding in terminal uremia). [1]

### 3.3 Glomerular Diseases (Glomerulonephritides)

Classification is based on whether the presentation is nephrotic, nephritic, or mixed, and further refined by the pathological pattern.

### A. The Nephrotic Syndrome (Heavy Proteinuria):

### Primary Glomerulopathies:

  - **Minimal Change Disease (MCD):** Most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children. Light microscopy is normal; electron microscopy shows foot process effacement. Excellent response to steroids. [7]
  - **Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS):** A pattern of scarring in some (focal) but not all glomeruli, and in parts (segmental) of those involved. Etiologies are primary (idiopathic) and secondary (hyperfiltration, HIV, obesity, reflux nephropathy). Variable response to immunosuppression; can progress to ESKD. [7]
  - **Membranous Nephropathy (MN):** Most common cause of primary nephrotic syndrome in Caucasian adults. Thickened glomerular basement membrane (GBM) on light microscopy. Primary MN is mediated by autoantibodies against the phospholipase A2 receptor (anti-PLA2R) on podocytes. Secondary causes include malignancy, NSAIDs, lupus, and infections (hepatitis B/C). [7]

### Secondary Glomerulopathies:

  - **Diabetic Nephropathy:** The most common cause of nephrotic syndrome overall. Progressive proteinuria and GFR decline.
  - **Amyloidosis:** AL (primary, light chain) and AA (secondary, chronic inflammation) amyloid deposits cause nephrotic-range proteinuria. Congo red stain with apple-green birefringence.
  - **Lupus Nephritis (Class V):** Membranous LN pattern.

### B. The Nephritic Syndrome (Hematuria, Proteinuria, HTN, Edema):

- **Acute Post-Infectious (Post-Streptococcal) GN:** Occurring 1-4 weeks after group A β-hemolytic streptococcal infection. Nephritic presentation with low C3 levels. Recovery is typical in children; adults have a worse prognosis. [7]
- **IgA Nephropathy (Berger's Disease):** The most common primary GN worldwide. Episodes of macroscopic hematuria (synpharyngitic hematuria) coinciding with upper respiratory tract infections. Diagnosis by biopsy showing IgA-dominant mesangial deposits. 20-40% progress to ESKD over 20 years. [7]
- **Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis (RPGN):** A clinical syndrome, not a single disease, characterized by rapid loss of kidney function over days to weeks. Renal biopsy shows cellular crescents.
  - **Anti-GBM Disease (Goodpasture's Syndrome):** Antibodies against type IV collagen in GBM. Linear IgG deposits on immunofluorescence. Presents with RPGN and pulmonary hemorrhage.
  - **Pauci-Immune GN (ANCA-Associated):** No or scant immune deposits on IF. Positive ANCA serology (PR3-ANCA/c-ANCA or MPO-ANCA/p-ANCA). Includes Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA), Microscopic Polyangiitis (MPA), and Eosinophilic GPA (Churg-Strauss).
  - **Immune-Complex Mediated:** RPGN superimposed on lupus nephritis, IgA nephropathy, or post-infectious GN. Granular IF deposits. [7]
- **Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN):** A histological pattern with mesangial hypercellularity and GBM thickening/tram-tracking. Reclassified by IF into immune-complex (e.g., lupus, HCV, endocarditis) and complement-mediated (C3 glomerulopathy, dense deposit disease). [7]

### C. Hereditary Glomerulopathies:

- **Alport Syndrome:** Mutation in type IV collagen (COL4A3/4/5). X-linked most common. Presents with nephritic features, sensorineural hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities (anterior lenticonus). GBM shows basket-weave appearance on EM.
- **Thin Basement Membrane Disease:** Benign familial hematuria. Heterozygous COL4A3/4 mutation. GBM is diffusely thin on EM.

### 3.4 Tubulointerstitial Diseases

- **Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN):** (See AKI section).
- **Chronic Interstitial Nephritis (CIN):** A heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and chronic inflammation.
  - **Analgesic Nephropathy:** Chronic use of phenacetin-combination analgesics. Papillary necrosis.
  - **Aristolochic Acid Nephropathy (Balkan Endemic Nephropathy):** Progressive interstitial fibrosis associated with urothelial malignancy.
  - **Lithium Nephrotoxicity:** Chronic use causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and CIN.
  - **Heavy Metals:** Lead, cadmium exposure.
- **Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN):** (See AKI section).
- **Myeloma Kidney (Cast Nephropathy):** Light chains are toxic to tubular cells and form intratubular casts with Tamm-Horsfall protein.

### Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA):

  - **Distal (Type 1) RTA:** Defect in H+ secretion by α-intercalated cells. Urine pH >5.5. Hypokalemia. Calcium phosphate stones and nephrocalcinosis.
  - **Proximal (Type 2) RTA:** Defect in proximal HCO3- reclamation. Often part of Fanconi syndrome. Urine pH <5.5 when acidemic, but the bicarbonate loss causes an alkaline urine and metabolic acidosis.
  - **Type 4 RTA (Hyperkalemic):** Hypoaldosteronism or aldosterone resistance. The most common RTA. Hyperkalemia inhibits ammoniagenesis, reducing the urine's buffering capacity. Associated with diabetic nephropathy, NSAIDs, and ACE inhibitors. Urine pH <5.5.
- **Fanconi Syndrome:** Generalized proximal tubular dysfunction causing wasting of glucose, amino acids, phosphate, bicarbonate, uric acid, and low-molecular-weight proteins. Causes include hereditary disorders (Wilson's disease, cystinosis), light chains, and toxins (tenofovir, ifosfamide).
- **Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI):** A resistance to ADH action in the collecting duct, leading to an inability to concentrate urine. Causes include lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, and hereditary mutations in the AVPR2 receptor or aquaporin-2 channel. Presents with polyuria and polydipsia. [2]

### 3.5 Hereditary & Congenital Kidney Diseases

## Cystic Diseases of the Kidney:

  - **Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD):** PKD1/PKD2 mutations. Massive bilateral kidney enlargement, hypertension, liver cysts, and intracranial (berry) aneurysms.
  - **Autosomal Recessive PKD (ARPKD):** PKHD1 mutation. Presents in infancy/childhood with massive nephromegaly, hypertension, and congenital hepatic fibrosis.
  - **Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC):** TSC1/TSC2 mutations. Renal angiomyolipomas and renal cysts. Associated with skin, neurological, and pulmonary manifestations.
  - **Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) Disease:** Renal cell carcinomas, renal cysts, pheochromocytomas, and CNS hemangioblastomas.
  - **Medullary Cystic Disease:** MUC1, UMOD mutations. Small fibrotic kidneys with medullary cysts. Causes CKD and gout.
- **Alport Syndrome:** (See Glomerular Diseases).
- **Fabry Disease:** X-linked deficiency of α-galactosidase A, leading to glycosphingolipid accumulation in podocytes, cardiomyocytes, and nerves. Causes CKD, LVH, acroparesthesias, and angiokeratomas.

### 3.6 Renal Vascular Diseases

### Renal Artery Stenosis (RAS):

  - Atherosclerotic (ostial): In older adults with risk factors for CVD.
  - Fibromuscular Dysplasia (FMD): In young women. "String of beads" appearance on angiography. [8]
- **Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TMA):** A syndrome of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury (especially AKI).
  - **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP):** ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity). More prominent neurological involvement. Treated with plasma exchange.
  - **Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS):** Shiga-toxin associated (STEC-HUS, classic HUS) with bloody diarrhea, or atypical HUS (aHUS) from complement dysregulation. Treated with supportive care (STEC-HUS) or eculizumab (aHUS). [8]
- **Scleroderma Renal Crisis:** Malignant hypertension and progressive AKI in a patient with systemic sclerosis. Associated with anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies. Treated with ACE inhibitors.
- **Cholesterol Emboli Syndrome:** AKI, livedo reticularis, blue toes, and eosinophilia following intra-arterial instrumentation or anticoagulation in patients with aortic atherosclerosis.

### 3.7 Disorders of Fluid, Electrolyte, and Acid-Base Balance

- **Hyponatremia & Hypernatremia:** Disorders of water balance (ADH dysregulation). Hyponatremia is classified by tonicity (isotonic, hypotonic, hypertonic) and volume status (hypovolemic, euvolemic/SIADH, hypervolemic). [2]
- **Hypokalemia & Hyperkalemia:** Disorders of potassium balance. Critical to evaluate for transcellular shifts (insulin, pH, beta-agonists) vs. total body deficits/excess.
- **Metabolic Acidosis:** Characterized by a low serum HCO3 and low pCO2. Analysis requires the anion gap (AG) calculation.
  - **High-Anion Gap (HAGMA):** MUDPILES mnemonic—Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, Isoniazid/Iron, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates (late).
  - **Normal-Anion Gap (NAGMA):** HARDUP mnemonic—Hyperalimentation, Acetazolamide, RTA, Diarrhea, Ureteral diversions, Pancreatic fistula. [2]
- **Metabolic Alkalosis:** Classified by urine chloride as saline-responsive (urine Cl < 20; vomiting, diuretics) and saline-resistant (urine Cl > 20; hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's). [2]

### 3.8 Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)

A disorder of supersaturated urine. Classification by stone type:

- **Calcium Oxalate:** Most common (75%). Causes: idiopathic hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria (primary or enteric), hypocitraturia.
- **Calcium Phosphate:** RTA Type 1, hyperparathyroidism.
- **Uric Acid:** Radiolucent. Form in concentrated, acidic urine. Associated with metabolic syndrome.
- **Struvite (Magnesium Ammonium Phosphate):** "Staghorn calculi." Infection stones from urease-producing organisms (Proteus, Klebsiella).
- **Cystine:** Hereditary cystinuria. Pathognomonic hexagonal crystals. [6]

### 3.9 Urinary Tract Obstruction (Obstructive Nephropathy)

A functional or structural impedance to urine flow. Etiologies by level:

- **Intraluminal:** Stones, blood clots, sloughed papillae.
- **Intramural (Wall):** Urothelial carcinoma, ureteral stricture, neurogenic bladder.
- **Extrinsic:** Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), pelvic malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis.
- Post-obstructive diuresis is a potentially life-threatening polyuria following relief of obstruction. [6]

### 3.10 Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) and Pyelonephritis

- **Lower UTI (Cystitis):** Urethritis, cystitis, prostatitis. Presents with dysuria, frequency, urgency.
- **Upper UTI (Acute Pyelonephritis):** Infection of the renal parenchyma. Presents with fever, flank pain, nausea/vomiting, and costovertebral angle tenderness. Often caused by ascending bacteria (E. coli is most common).
- **Complicated UTI:** Occurs in structurally or functionally abnormal urinary tracts (stones, obstruction, catheters, pregnancy, male sex). Requires a longer course of antibiotics.
- **Chronic Pyelonephritis:** Reflux nephropathy (from vesicoureteral reflux) leading to cortical scarring, blunted calyces, and progressive CKD. [6]

### 3.11 Kidney Disease in Special Populations

### Pregnancy:

  - Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia: New-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks. AKI can occur. The glomerular lesion is endotheliosis.
  - HELLP Syndrome: Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets. A severe form of pre-eclampsia/TMA.
  - Pregnancy-associated TTP/HUS.
  - Physiological hydronephrosis of pregnancy (right > left due to ureteral compression by the gravid uterus).

### Onconephrology:

  - Tumor Lysis Syndrome: Hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hypocalcemia from rapid cell lysis after chemotherapy.
  - Myeloma Cast Nephropathy.
  - Chemotherapy-related nephrotoxicity (cisplatin, ifosfamide, methotrexate).
  - Paraneoplastic glomerulopathies (membranous nephropathy with solid tumors).

### 3.12 Renal Replacement Therapy & Transplantation

- **Hemodialysis (HD):** Blood is pumped through an artificial kidney (dialyzer). Solute clearance is by diffusion, fluid removal by ultrafiltration. Requires vascular access: arteriovenous fistula (AVF, the gold standard), AV graft, or central venous catheter. [3]
- **Peritoneal Dialysis (PD):** Dialysate is infused into the peritoneal cavity. The peritoneum acts as the dialysis membrane. Solute clearance by diffusion, fluid removal by an osmotic gradient (dextrose or icodextrin). Types: Continuous Ambulatory PD (CAPD) and Automated PD (APD).
- **Kidney Transplantation:** The optimal treatment for ESKD. Requires lifelong immunosuppression (induction and maintenance). Complications include rejection (hyperacute, acute cellular, antibody-mediated, chronic), infection (CMV, BKV, PCP), and malignancy (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, skin cancers).

## 4. The Diagnostic Arsenal

A nephrologist uses a clinical framework to answer two questions: 1) Is the problem acute or chronic? 2) Which renal compartment is injured?

- **History:** Must differentiate AKI from CKD. Review old creatinine values. Probe for NSAID use, recent infections, antibiotic exposure, and systemic symptoms (rash, arthralgias, sinusitis for vasculitis; hemoptysis for Goodpasture's).
- **Physical Exam:** Look for volume status (JVP, edema, lungs), rashes, fundoscopy, and pulses/femoral bruits.
- **Urinalysis:** The single most important non-invasive test.
  - **Dipstick:** Detects heme (RBC, myoglobin, hemoglobin), protein (primarily albumin), leukocyte esterase, nitrites.
  - **Microscopy:** Pathognomonic findings include dysmorphic RBCs/acanthocytes and RBC casts (pathognomonic for glomerulonephritis), muddy brown granular casts (ATN), WBC casts (AIN/pyelonephritis), fatty oval bodies/Maltese crosses (nephrotic syndrome).
- **Urine Chemistries (in AKI):** Used to distinguish pre-renal from ATN.
  - **FENa (Fractional Excretion of Sodium):** FENa=(UNa×PCr)/(PNa×UCr)×100*FENa*=(*UNa*​×*PCr*​)/(*PNa*​×*UCr*​)×100. <1% suggests pre-renal; >2% suggests ATN. (Cannot be used on diuretics; FeUrea used instead). [4]
- **Blood Tests:** Creatinine, eGFR, BUN, electrolytes, calcium, phosphate, albumin, serology (ANA, ANCA, anti-GBM, anti-PLA2R, C3/C4, SPEP/UPEP, free light chains, HIV, HCV, HBV).
- **Imaging:** Renal ultrasound is first-line. It assesses kidney size (small and shrunken = CKD), cortical echogenicity, hydronephrosis (obstruction), and cysts. Doppler ultrasound assesses renal blood flow. CT/MRI/Angiography for stones, masses, and RAS.
- **Kidney Biopsy:** The gold standard for diagnosing glomerular disease and unexplained AKI/CKD. Requires light microscopy, immunofluorescence (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, kappa/lambda light chains), and electron microscopy. [7]

## 5. The Pharmacological Armamentarium

The nephrologist's toolbox contains drugs that act on the kidney, or require meticulous dose adjustment due to renal elimination.

### Diuretics:

  - *Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors* (acetazolamide): Proximal tubule.
  - *Loop Diuretics* (furosemide): Thick ascending limb. Block NKCC2.
  - *Thiazides* (HCTZ, chlorthalidone): Distal convoluted tubule. Block NCC.
  - *K+-Sparing Diuretics* (spironolactone, eplerenone): Collecting duct. Block aldosterone receptor (MR antagonists).
  - *ENaC Blockers* (amiloride, triamterene): Collecting duct. Block epithelial sodium channel.
  - *Vasopressin V2 Receptor Antagonists* (tolvaptan): Collecting duct. Treat SIADH and slow cyst growth in ADPKD.
- **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) Inhibitors:** ACE inhibitors and ARBs. These are the cornerstone of nephroprotection in proteinuric CKD. They reduce intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria, slowing CKD progression. Dual blockade is contraindicated. [3,5]
- **SGLT2 Inhibitors:** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin). Initially diabetes drugs, now standard-of-care nephroprotective and cardioprotective agents in proteinuric and non-proteinuric CKD, independent of diabetes status. [3]
- **Non-Steroidal Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (nsMRAs):** (finerenone). Offer cardiorenal protection in diabetic CKD with less hyperkalemia risk than spironolactone.

### Immunosuppressants:

  - *Glucocorticoids:* First-line for MCD, AIN, and many vasculitides.
  - *Cyclophosphamide, Rituximab:* Induction for severe ANCA vasculitis, anti-GBM disease, or resistant MCD/FSGS.
  - *Calcineurin Inhibitors* (tacrolimus, cyclosporine): Used in FSGS, MN, and transplant immunosuppression.

### CKD-MBD Management:

  - Phosphate binders (calcium acetate, sevelamer, lanthanum).
  - Active vitamin D analogs (calcitriol, paricalcitol).
  - Calcimimetics (cinacalcet): CaSR agonist. Suppresses PTH secretion.

### Anemia Management:

  - Iron supplementation (oral or IV).
  - Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents (ESAs): epoetin alfa, darbepoetin alfa.
  - Hypoxia-Inducible Factor Prolyl Hydroxylase Inhibitors (HIF-PHIs): daprodustat, roxadustat. A novel class that stabilizes HIF and stimulates endogenous EPO production.

### 1. The Nephrology Clinic (Outpatient Service)

### 1.1 Structure, Purpose, and Patient Flow

The nephrology clinic is the command center for long-term disease management. Its primary goal is to slow the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD), manage its complications, and prepare patients for renal replacement therapy (RRT) in a controlled, elective fashion, while also providing post-transplant care. [1] An efficient clinic is designed to handle a high volume of complex patients through a protocol-driven approach. The flow typically involves an initial nursing assessment (vital signs, weight, urine dipstick), blood and urine sample collection, the physician or advanced practice provider consultation, and a wrap-up with a pharmacist, dietitian, or social worker if needed. [2]

### 1.2 The Patient Cohorts: A Complete List of Clinic Types

The nephrology outpatient service is not a single clinic; it is a constellation of subspecialty clinics.

- **General Nephrology Clinic:** The workhorse clinic. Manages all stages of CKD (G3a to G5 non-dialysis), hypertensive nephropathy, and stable tubular disorders.
- **Progressive CKD / Low Clearance Clinic:** For advanced CKD (G4, G5). The focus shifts from pure risk-factor modification to education on RRT options, anemia and CKD-MBD management, vaccination, and vascular access planning. [1]
- **Glomerulonephritis (GN) Clinic:** A tertiary referral clinic for managing primary and secondary glomerular diseases (e.g., lupus nephritis, FSGS, IgA nephropathy). Heavy focus on immunosuppressive medication monitoring, serial proteinuria quantification, and managing drug side effects.
- **Transplant Clinic:** For all post-kidney transplant recipients. Focuses on monitoring graft function, maintaining the delicate balance of immunosuppression, and screening for post-transplant complications like infections (CMV, BKV), malignancy, and cardiovascular disease. [3]
- **Stone Prevention (Nephrolithiasis) Clinic:** A metabolic clinic for recurrent stone formers. Focuses on 24-hour urine collection analysis to identify metabolic defects (hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia, etc.) and dietary/pharmacological prevention strategies.
- **Resistant Hypertension Clinic:** Evaluates patients with uncontrolled hypertension on 3+ drugs, searching for secondary causes (renal artery stenosis, primary hyperaldosteronism) and tailoring medication.
- **Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD) Clinic:** A dedicated clinic for ADPKD patients. Monitors total kidney volume, manages pain, screens for intracranial aneurysms, and prescribes tolvaptan for eligible patients.
- **Vascular Access Clinic:** A combined surgical/nephrology clinic to plan, create, and maintain hemodialysis vascular access (AV fistulas and grafts). Involves pre-operative vessel mapping and post-operative maturation monitoring. [4]
- **Transition Clinic:** A critical service that bridges the gap for adolescents and young adults moving from pediatric to adult nephrology care, ensuring no patient is lost to follow-up during a high-risk period. [2]

### 1.3 Core Clinical Activities and Monitoring Protocols

Each clinic visit is an opportunity to methodically screen for and manage the multifaceted complications of CKD. This is not just about creatinine.

- **Blood Pressure Control:** Titrating antihypertensives to a target often <130/80 mmHg. RAAS blockade is the cornerstone. [1]
- **Proteinuria Monitoring:** Quantifying urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) or albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is the single most powerful predictor of CKD progression. The goal is to suppress proteinuria as much as possible. [1]
- **CKD Progression Monitoring:** Tracking the slope of eGFR decline. A rapid decline requires investigation (e.g., new GN, AIN, renovascular disease).
- **CKD-MBD Management:** Following KDIGO guidelines. This involves monitoring phosphate, calcium, PTH, and vitamin D levels. Initiating phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, or calcimimetics at the right threshold. [1]
- **Anemia Management:** Monitoring hemoglobin and iron studies. Prescribing oral or IV iron and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) or HIF-PH inhibitors. [1]
- **Metabolic Acidosis Management:** Monitoring serum bicarbonate. Prescribing oral sodium bicarbonate supplementation, as treating acidosis slows CKD progression. [1]
- **Cardiovascular Risk Modification:** Managing dyslipidemia with statins (when indicated), optimizing glycemic control, and promoting smoking cessation.
- **RRT Education and Planning:** Starting at CKD G4, a dedicated education process begins with the patient and family. This includes discussing the pros and cons of hemodialysis (HD), peritoneal dialysis (PD), kidney transplantation (including pre-emptive living donor transplant), and conservative kidney management. [5]
- **Vaccinations:** Ensuring the patient is up-to-date with the augmented vaccine schedule for CKD (hepatitis B double-dose series, pneumococcal vaccines, influenza, and shingles). [2]

### 1.4 The Multidisciplinary Team in the Clinic Setting

A high-functioning clinic is a team sport. Key members include:

- **Nephrology Nurse Educator:** The linchpin of RRT education, teaching patients and families about dialysis and transplant.
- **Renal Dietitian:** Provides medical nutrition therapy, crucial for managing potassium, phosphate, sodium, protein, and fluid intake.
- **Renal Pharmacist:** Reviews medication lists for nephrotoxic drugs, contraindications, and dose adjustments for level of kidney function.
- **Social Worker:** Addresses the psychosocial and financial burdens of kidney disease, transport, housing, and employment.
- **Vascular Access Coordinator:** Tracks fistula creation, maturation, and patency, ensuring a functional access is ready when needed. [4]
- **Advanced Practice Provider (Nurse Practitioner/Physician Assistant):** Often manages a panel of stable CKD patients under a supervising nephrologist, improving clinic capacity.

### 2. The Nephrology Inpatient Ward

### 2.1 Admission Criteria and Ward Structure

Patients are admitted under the nephrology service for problems that cannot be managed in an outpatient setting. The ward is often a high-acuity unit where fluid and electrolyte disorders are the central focus. It may be a dedicated nephrology ward or a general medical ward with a primary nephrology team (consultants, fellows, residents, and medical students). Bedside dialysis capability (either mobile reverse osmosis units for HD or PD cyclers) must be available. [5]

### 2.2 The Inpatient Patient Cohorts

- **Acute Kidney Injury (AKI):** The most common reason for an emergency nephrology consult. Management focuses on identifying the cause (pre-renal, renal, post-renal), optimizing hemodynamics, avoiding secondary insults, and initiating acute dialysis if needed.
- **Electrolyte Emergencies:** Symptomatic hyponatremia, life-threatening hyperkalemia (K >6.5 mmol/L or ECG changes), severe metabolic acidosis, severe hypercalcemia.
- **Glomerulonephritis Flares:** Patients with RPGN, severe lupus nephritis, or ANCA-associated vasculitis presenting with AKI, hemoptysis, and nephritic syndrome. This is a medical emergency requiring urgent serology and kidney biopsy.
- **Dialysis Initiation:** Patients with advanced CKD who present with uremic symptoms (pericarditis, encephalopathy, intractable nausea/vomiting) and need an urgent start to dialysis, often via a temporary central venous catheter. [5]
- **Dialysis Access Complications:** Thrombosed AV fistula/graft, dialysis catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI), or catheter malfunction.
- **Complications of Dialysis:** Volume overload due to missed treatments, severe intradialytic hypotension, peritonitis in a PD patient.
- **Transplant Admissions:** Admission for new kidney transplant, or acute graft dysfunction, infection in an immunocompromised host (CMV viremia, BKV nephropathy), or rejection.
- **Intoxications:** Toxic alcohol poisonings (methanol, ethylene glycol) that require urgent hemodialysis.
- **Refractory Volume Overload:** Heart failure, cirrhosis, or nephrotic syndrome patients unresponsive to high-dose IV diuretics.

### 2.3 Daily Workflow: The Ward Round and Management Plan

The inpatient nephrology round is a precision exercise in volume and electrolyte assessment.

- **Volume Status Assessment:** The most critical and challenging skill. It combines a targeted history (thirst, orthopnea), intake/output chart scrutiny, and a physical exam focused on the jugular venous pressure (JVP), daily weight, orthostatic vital signs, and the presence of edema or lung crackles. [6]
- **Urinalysis:** A urine dipstick and microscopy are performed by the team on nearly every new consult. Identifying a muddy brown cast changes the diagnosis to ATN. An RBC cast mandates an urgent GN workup.
- **Medication Reconciliation:** A "nephrotoxic timeout" is performed daily. This means scrutinizing the medication list for NSAIDs, nephrotoxic antibiotics, or dose adjustments for renally-cleared medications. [6]
- **Dialysis Orders:** For the patient on maintenance dialysis, the ward team writes a comprehensive dialysis prescription, including estimated dry weight, total ultrafiltration goal, dialysate potassium and calcium baths, and net fluid removal target for the session.
- **Procedures:** Scheduling and performing ultrasounded-guided temporary dialysis catheter insertions and native kidney biopsies.

### 2.4 Common Inpatient Procedures and Interventions

- **Non-Tunneled (Temporary) Hemodialysis Catheter Insertion:** A bedside procedure, typically in the right internal jugular vein under ultrasound guidance. Requires meticulous sterile technique (full barrier precautions) to prevent CRBSI. [4]
- **Percutaneous Native Kidney Biopsy:** Indicated for unexplained AKI, nephrotic syndrome, or RPGN. Requires informed consent, normal coagulation parameters, controlled blood pressure, and post-biopsy monitoring for bleeding. [7]
- **Acute Peritoneal Dialysis Catheter Insertion:** A bedside procedure for initiating acute PD, especially in infants or patients where HD access is impossible.
- **Management of CRRT in the ICU:** The nephrology team directs the CRRT prescription (modality, dose, fluid removal rate, anticoagulation) in collaboration with the intensivists.

### 2.5 The Multidisciplinary Team on the Ward

- **Ward Nurses:** Trained in strict fluid balance charting, daily weights, and monitoring for dialysis access infections.
- **Dialysis Nurses:** A specialized team that runs the mobile dialysis machines on the ward and manages the hemodialysis treatments for inpatients.
- **Infectious Disease Consultant:** Critical for managing CRBSIs and infections in transplant patients.
- **Dietitian:** Calculates precise nutritional requirements for the catabolic AKI or malnourished CKD patient.
- **Pharmacist:** Rounds with the team daily to ensure all medications are dose-adjusted for the dynamic changes in kidney function.

### 3. The Dialysis Unit: A Department in Its Own Right

This is the operational core where life is sustained between transplantations or for life. It is a high-volume, protocol-driven, and technically demanding environment.

### 3.1 The Hemodialysis Unit

### 3.1.1 Physical Layout, Water Treatment, and Infrastructure

An HD unit is a large, open-plan clinical area with individual patient stations, each equipped with a dialysis machine, a reclining chair or bed, and connections for electricity, treated water, and acid/bicarbonate concentrates. The most critical piece of infrastructure is the **water treatment system**—a chain of sediment filters, carbon tanks (to remove chlorine/chloramines), water softeners, and a reverse osmosis (RO) unit. Any breach in the RO membrane exposes patients' blood to contaminants in municipal water, a potentially fatal event. [8]

### 3.1.2 Patient Cohorts and Shift Scheduling

Patients are grouped into shifts (e.g., Monday-Wednesday-Friday or Tuesday-Thursday-Saturday). Infection control is paramount; patients with transmissible blood-borne viruses (hepatitis B) are segregated into an isolation room with a dedicated machine and staff cohort. Hepatitis C patients may also be isolated depending on unit policy.

### 3.1.3 The Hemodialysis Procedure: A Step-by-Step Breakdown

- **Pre-Dialysis Assessment:** The nurse weighs the patient, assesses vital signs (including orthostatic BP), and checks their vascular access.
- **Machine Setup and Cannulation:** The arterial and venous needles are aseptically inserted into the AV fistula or graft. The machine's blood lines are connected. For a catheter, the lumens are opened and connected using sterile technique.
- **Initiation:** Blood is pumped at a set rate (typically 300-450 mL/min) through the dialyzer (artificial kidney). The prescribed ultrafiltration (UF) goal is programmed. Unfractionated heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin is often infused to prevent clotting of the circuit.
- **Intradialytic Monitoring:** The nurse monitors the patient's blood pressure, heart rate, and symptoms at regular intervals (every 15-30 minutes). The machine monitors venous and arterial line pressures, dialysate temperature and conductivity, and any blood leak. [8]
- **Termination:** At the end of the session (typically 3.5-5 hours), the blood is returned to the patient ("rinsed back"). The needles are removed, and the nurse holds firm pressure over the access sites for 10-15 minutes to achieve hemostasis.
- **Post-Dialysis Assessment:** The patient is re-weighed to determine the actual fluid removed. This is compared to the target. Orthostatic vital signs are checked.

### 3.1.4 Vascular Access: The Lifeline

A functioning access is a constant preoccupation. The hierarchy is "Fistula First, Catheter Last." [4]

- **Arteriovenous Fistula (AVF):** An autologous surgical connection of an artery to a vein. The gold standard for its longevity and lower infection risk. Takes 6-12 weeks to "mature" (dilate and arterialize). Assessed clinically by palpation for a continuous thrill and auscultation for a loud, continuous bruit.
- **Arteriovenous Graft (AVG):** A synthetic tube connecting an artery to a vein. Can be used sooner (2-3 weeks) than an AVF but has a higher thrombosis and infection rate.
- **Tunneled Central Venous Catheter (CVC):** A double-lumen catheter placed in a central vein (internal jugular, subclavian) with a subcutaneous tunnel and a Dacron cuff that anchors it and provides a barrier to infection. A last resort due to high risk of central venous stenosis, thrombosis, and CRBSI. [4]

### 3.1.5 Intradialytic Complications and Emergency Management

This is a high-stakes environment. Complications are common and can be fatal.

- **Hypotension:** The most common complication. Caused by an overly aggressive UF rate, autonomic neuropathy, or a cardiac event. Management: Trendelenburg position, stopping UF, and giving a 100-200 mL normal saline bolus. [8]
- **Muscle Cramps:** Commonly due to rapid fluid removal.
- **Disequilibrium Syndrome:** Seen with the first few HD treatments. An osmotic gradient between brain and blood causes cerebral edema. Presents with headache, nausea, confusion, and seizures. Prevention: gentle, short initial dialysis sessions.
- **Anaphylactoid Reactions:** Can be due to the dialyzer membrane ("first-use syndrome") or ethylene oxide sterilization.
- **Hemolysis:** From kinked blood lines, overheated dialysate, or dialysate contaminated with disinfectants (chloramine).
- **Air Embolism:** A catastrophic, now rare, event if air is allowed to enter the blood circuit.
- **Cardiac Arrhythmias:** Precipitated by rapid shifts in potassium, calcium, and pH.

### 3.1.6 Quality Metrics and Adequacy (Kt/V, URR)

Dialysis adequacy is quantified, not guessed. A blood sample is taken before and after a session. [8]

- **Kt/V:** A dimensionless measure of urea clearance, where K is dialyzer urea clearance, t is time, and V is the patient's total body water volume. The minimum target for thrice-weekly HD is spKt/V ≥ 1.4.
- **Urea Reduction Ratio (URR):** The percentage reduction in blood urea nitrogen during a session: URR=(pre-BUN−post-BUN)/pre-BUN×100URR=(pre-BUN−post-BUN)/pre-BUN×100. Target is >65%.

### 3.2 The Peritoneal Dialysis Unit

This unit is an educational hub, not just a treatment center, as most care is performed by the patient at home.

### 3.2.1 The Peritoneal Dialysis Catheter and Its Care

A soft, double-cuffed Tenckhoff catheter is surgically placed into the pelvis, with the internal cuff buried in the rectus muscle and the external cuff in the subcutaneous tunnel. The cuff provokes a fibrotic reaction, sealing the tract. The exit site must be cleaned daily with an antiseptic, and the patient is taught to immobilize the catheter to prevent exit-site trauma and infection. [5]

### 3.2.2 PD Modalities

- **Continuous Ambulatory Peritoneal Dialysis (CAPD):** A manual technique. The patient performs 3-5 exchanges per day using gravity. Typically, 2 liters of dialysate are infused, dwell for 4-6 hours, and then drained. Requires no machine, offering lifestyle flexibility.
- **Automated Peritoneal Dialysis (APD):** An automated cycler machine performs multiple short exchanges overnight while the patient sleeps. The morning exchange may be left in for a long daytime dwell. Minimizes manual exchanges and is ideal for patients with fast membrane transport characteristics (who get poor ultrafiltration with long dwells). [8]

### 3.2.3 Patient Training and Home Support

The PD nurse is central. A new patient undergoes 1-2 weeks of intensive, one-on-one training. They must demonstrate strict sterile technique (hand washing, masking), learn to connect and disconnect, and record their daily weight and ultrafiltration volume. The unit provides 24/7 on-call phone support and arranges monthly delivery of supplies to the patient's home. [5]

### 3.2.4 PD-Specific Complications

- **PD Peritonitis:** The Achilles' heel of PD. Presents with abdominal pain and cloudy dialysate. A PD fluid cell count, Gram stain, and culture are sent immediately. The cell count will show an elevated white count with >50% neutrophils. Empiric intraperitoneal (IP) antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin + ceftazidime) are started urgently. [8]
- **Exit-Site and Tunnel Infection:** Presents with erythema, pain, and purulent drainage at the catheter exit site. If it tracks down the tunnel, it can lead to catheter loss.
- **Ultrafiltration Failure:** An insidious loss of ability to remove fluid due to peritoneal membrane changes (type I, high solute transport) or encasing peritoneal sclerosis (type II failure). Diagnosed with a peritoneal equilibration test (PET). [8]
- **Catheter Malfunction:** Obstruction from fibrin plugs, omental wrapping, or catheter migration out of the pelvis. Often requires catheter manipulation or surgical revision.

### 3.3 Home Hemodialysis

A more intensive form of dialysis, performed by the patient and a partner at home.

- **Short Daily HD:** 5-6 sessions per week, each 2-3 hours.
- **Nocturnal HD:** 6-8 hours overnight, often every other night.
- Home HD provides superior phosphate control, blood pressure control, and quality of life but requires immense patient and caregiver training, dedication, and a partner. The unit provides remote monitoring technology. [5]

### 4. The Kidney Transplantation Service

A separate, comprehensive clinical service that manages the patient from the day they are deemed a candidate to the end of the graft's life.

### 4.1 Pre-Transplant Evaluation and Waitlisting

This is a rigorous process to exclude contraindications (active malignancy, uncontrolled infection, severe untreatable cardiovascular disease) and optimize the patient's surgical and medical fitness. It involves a full cardiac, vascular, infectious disease (HIV, HBV, HCV, TB, CMV/EBV status), and psychosocial workup. The patient is then registered on the national waiting list, which is managed based on allocation algorithms that prioritize patients by factors like waiting time, degree of sensitization (calculated Panel Reactive Antibody, cPRA), and pediatric status. [3]

### 4.2 Deceased vs. Living Donor Transplantation

- **Deceased Donor Transplant:** An organ from a donor after brain death (DBD) or circulatory death (DCD) is procured, matched for ABO blood group and HLA compatibility, and transported to the recipient's center. This is an unscheduled event requiring the patient to arrive at the hospital immediately.
- **Living Donor Transplant:** A healthy donor (often a relative or friend, or an altruistic non-directed donor) donates a kidney electively. This allows for a scheduled surgery, better long-term graft survival, and the opportunity for a pre-emptive transplant (before the recipient ever starts dialysis). The donor undergoes an exhaustive medical and psychological evaluation to ensure their safety. [3]

### 4.3 The Transplant Admission (Perioperative Care)

The new kidney is transplanted into the iliac fossa; the patient's native kidneys are typically left in place. A vascular anastomosis is made to the external iliac artery and vein, and the donor ureter is implanted into the bladder. The immediate post-operative period demands strict fluid management to perfuse the new kidney, which may initially produce massive amounts of urine. Meticulous monitoring for surgical bleeding and vascular thrombosis is essential. [3]

### 4.4 The Post-Transplant Clinic and Long-Term Follow-Up

This is the longest and most complex phase. The frequency of visits is high initially, then tapers. Every visit is a systematic check for the three major threats: rejection, infection, and drug toxicity.

- **Graft Function Monitoring:** Trending serum creatinine and proteinuria.
- **Immunosuppressant Drug Level Monitoring:** Measuring trough levels (tacrolimus, sirolimus) to maintain a narrow therapeutic window.
- **Infection Screening:** Serial PCR screening for BKV and CMV reactivation. Prophylactic antibiotics are given (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for PCP, valganciclovir for CMV). [3]
- **Malignancy Screening:** Thorough skin checks for squamous cell carcinoma and age-appropriate cancer screening.
- **Cardiovascular Risk Management:** Aggressive treatment of post-transplant hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, which are worsened by immunosuppressants.

### 4.5 Immunosuppression Management

A triple-drug maintenance regimen is typical: 1) Calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus); 2) Antimetabolite (mycophenolate mofetil); 3) Corticosteroid (prednisone). [3]

- **Induction Therapy:** A powerful antibody given at the time of transplant to deplete T-cells (rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin) or block IL-2 receptors (basiliximab).
- **Rejection Treatment:** Acute cellular rejection is treated with high-dose IV pulse steroids and, if refractory, anti-thymocyte globulin. Acute antibody-mediated rejection is treated with plasma exchange, IVIG, and other agents.

### 4.6 Post-Transplant Complications

- **Delayed Graft Function (DGF):** ATN in the new kidney, requiring post-transplant dialysis. More common with DCD kidneys.
- **Surgical Complications:** Vascular thrombosis, ureteral leak or stricture, lymphocele.
- **Rejection:** Acute cellular, acute antibody-mediated, chronic antibody-mediated (leading to chronic allograft nephropathy).
- **Infection:** BKV nephropathy (a direct threat to the graft), CMV colitis, EBV-driven post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD). [3]
- **Disease Recurrence:** The original disease (FSGS, IgA nephropathy, aHUS) can recur in the transplant, causing proteinuria and graft loss.
- **Malignancy:** The high-intensity immunosuppression drives a dramatically increased risk of skin cancers, PTLD, and other solid tumors.

### 5. Interventional Nephrology

A rapidly growing subspecialty where nephrologists perform procedures to manage access and diagnostics.

### 5.1 Vascular Access Procedures (Fistulogram, Angioplasty)

In an angiography suite, a nephrologist accesses the fistula/graft, injects contrast to image the entire outflow, and identifies stenosis. A balloon angioplasty can be performed to restore blood flow. Thrombosed accesses can be treated with mechanical or pharmacological thrombolysis. This "access salvage" is critical to preserving the patient's lifeline. [4]

### 5.2 Kidney Biopsy: Percutaneous and Transjugular

The gold standard for diagnosing parenchymal kidney disease. An interventional nephrologist or radiologist performs a real-time ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy of the kidney's lower pole. The sample is immediately examined under a dissecting microscope to ensure adequacy (glomerular yield). For high-risk patients with an uncorrectable bleeding diathesis, a transjugular approach through the internal jugular vein and into the kidney's venous system is used. [7]

### 5.3 Tunneled Dialysis Catheter Insertion

This is a step above the temporary CVC. In a sterile environment with fluoroscopic guidance, the catheter is tunneled under the skin on the chest wall, inserted into the central vein, and the tip is positioned at the cavoatrial junction. This is the definitive catheter for patients awaiting fistula maturation or who have exhausted all other options. [4]

### 6. The Acute Dialysis and Critical Care Nephrology Service

This is the 24/7 emergency response team for the sickest patients in the hospital.

### 6.1 Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT)

The standard of care for hemodynamically unstable ICU patients with AKI. Blood is circulated through a hemofilter slowly and continuously over 24 hours. Unlike intermittent HD, fluid and solute removal is gradual, avoiding catastrophic hypotension. CRRT modalities include CVVH (hemofiltration), CVVHD (hemodialysis), and CVVHDF (hemodiafiltration). Anticoagulation of the circuit, usually with regional citrate, is a core competency. [9]

### 6.2 Sustained Low-Efficiency Dialysis (SLED)

A hybrid therapy: a conventional HD machine run for a prolonged period (6-12 hours) at lower blood and dialysate flow rates. It offers some of the hemodynamic stability of CRRT but is less resource-intensive. Used for patients who are too sick for standard HD but too stable for CRRT. [9]

### 6.3 Apheresis: Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE or PLEX)

A procedure that separates whole blood and removes the patient's plasma, replacing it with albumin or fresh frozen plasma. It is a life-saving frontline treatment in nephrology for:

- Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's syndrome): To remove the pathogenic anti-GBM antibodies.
- TTP: To replace the deficient ADAMTS13 enzyme and remove ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers.
- Catastrophic Antiphospholipid Syndrome (CAPS).
- Recurrent FSGS in a transplant graft. [7]

### 6.4 Acute Peritoneal Dialysis in the ICU

In select situations, particularly in neonates and infants with AKI, acute PD is a gentle and effective method of RRT via a surgically placed, rigid acute PD catheter.

### 7. Other Critical Domains

### 7.1 The Kidney-Pancreas and Other Multi-Organ Transplant Programs

In patients with type 1 diabetes and ESKD, a simultaneous pancreas-kidney (SPK) transplant can render the patient both dialysis-free and insulin-independent. Nephrologists co-manage these patients with transplant surgeons. Other multi-organ transplants include heart-kidney, liver-kidney, and lung-kidney, requiring complex coordination between services. [3]

### 7.2 Onconephrology Service

A dedicated service managing the complex two-way relationship between cancer and the kidney: cancer-related kidney diseases (myeloma cast nephropathy, TMA), chemotherapy-induced nephrotoxicity, and the safe delivery of dialysis and transplant in patients with active or historical malignancy. [7]

### 7.3 Obstetric Nephrology

Caring for the pregnant patient with CKD, managing acute kidney injury in pregnancy (pre-eclampsia, HELLP), and managing hypertension in the setting of the physiological changes of pregnancy. [6]

### 7.4 Palliative and Supportive Kidney Care

A crucial, growing subspecialty. This service cares for patients who opt for conservative kidney management (CKM)—the choice to not pursue dialysis. It focuses on aggressive symptom management (uremic pruritus, nausea, restless legs), management of volume overload with diuretics, and advance care planning to ensure a dignified death. [5]

### 7.5 Quality Improvement, Registries, and Data Management

Nephrology is driven by data. Every dialysis unit reports its quality metrics to a national registry. The United States Renal Data System (USRDS) and similar registries globally track epidemiology, mortality, and clinical outcomes, forming the evidence base for new guidelines and benchmarks. A nephrologist must be literate in QI methodology to improve care delivery. [8]

### 7.6 The Nephrology Laboratory and Urine Microscopy Suite

The nephrologist's personal diagnostic tool. Urine is freshly voided, centrifuged, and the sediment is examined under a bright-field microscope, ideally with phase contrast. The ability to identify an acanthocyte (a spiky, dysmorphic RBC pathognomonic for glomerular bleeding) or a muddy brown cast (diagnostic of ATN) at the bedside provides an immediate, gold-standard diagnosis that no blood test can replicate. This skill defines the specialty. [7]

### 1. The Attributes and Daily Bread of the Nephrologist

### 1.1 The Core Attributes (The Nephrologist's Mindset)

The practice of nephrology shapes a specific cognitive and procedural identity. The effective nephrologist is defined not just by knowledge, but by a characteristic approach to clinical problems.

- **The Physiologist:** Nephrology is applied renal physiology. The nephrologist instinctively translates every clinical problem—a change in blood pressure, a new medication, a bout of vomiting—into its effect on glomerular hemodynamics, tubular transport, and the countercurrent multiplier. They do not just see a potassium of 6.2 mEq/L; they immediately assess for transcellular shifts, reduced GFR, and impaired distal tubular flow. [1]
- **The Master of Homeostasis:** The nephrologist is the guardian of the body's internal milieu. They are comfortable managing a patient with simultaneous hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and metabolic acidosis, understanding how the correction of one disturbance affects the others. They are the ultimate balancer.
- **The Longitudinal Strategist:** Unlike many specialties that treat episodic illness, nephrology is defined by a decades-long relationship with a patient. The nephrologist is always thinking five steps ahead. Seeing a GFR of 28 mL/min today triggers planning for a fistula that will be needed in two years, not just management of the current lab value. [2]
- **The Pragmatic Proceduralist:** The modern nephrologist is not purely cognitive. They must be skilled in the physical act of maintaining the patient's lifeline—examining a fistula for a thrill, interpreting a fistulogram, and inserting a temporary dialysis catheter under ultrasound guidance. [3]
- **The Realist and Truth-Teller:** Nephrologists routinely navigate conversations about mortality, quality of life on dialysis, and the option of conservative kidney management. They must be able to deliver honest prognostic information with clarity and compassion, guiding patients through the choice between a life-prolonging but demanding therapy and a focus on comfort. [2]

### 1.2 The Daily Bread: A Typical Workday Breakdown

The "daily bread" of a nephrologist is a mentally intense cycle of managing chronic disease, preventing catastrophe, and responding to emergencies.

- **The Morning Inpatient Round (The Acute Survey):** The day begins on the ward or in the ICU. The focus is razor-sharp:
  - **Fluid Balance Scrutiny:** A meticulous review of the 24-hour intake/output chart and daily weights. The nephrologist often draws a vertical fluid balance chart at the bedside to visualize the trend. [1]
  - **The Dialysis Prescription:** For each dialysis patient, a detailed order is written: estimated dry weight, total ultrafiltration goal for the session, dialysate potassium (e.g., K2, K3 bath), dialysate calcium (e.g., Ca2.5, Ca3.5 bath), blood flow rate, and dialyzer type. This is not a standard order; it is tailored daily.
  - **The Catheter and Line Check:** A direct physical inspection of every tunneled and temporary dialysis catheter exit site for erythema, discharge, or tenderness. Central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) is the enemy.
  - **The Nephrotoxic Timeout:** A rapid review of the medication list to identify and stop NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, and other insults, and to adjust the dose of cleared medications (enoxaparin, gabapentin, meropenem). [4]
- **The Outpatient Clinic (The Longitudinal Battle):** This is the center of CKD progression prevention. The workflow is protocolized:
  - **The Triple Target:** The nephrologist lives by three numbers for every CKD patient: blood pressure (<130/80 mmHg), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR, suppressed as much as possible), and eGFR slope (stable). [5]
  - **The Complication Checklist:** A systematic screen and treat approach for the seven pillars of CKD complication management: anemia, acidosis, CKD-MBD, volume status, potassium, cardiovascular risk, and RRT preparation.
  - **The Modality Discussion:** A large portion of the clinic visit with a CKD G4 patient involves education about hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, home dialysis, transplantation, and conservative care. This is a continuous, evolving conversation. [5]
- **The Hemodialysis Unit (The Circuit Check):** The nephrologist must be present on the dialysis floor, often in between clinic and inpatient duties.
  - **Rounding on Stable Patients:** A quick "chairside" check focusing on any intradialytic symptoms (cramps, hypotension), access patency, and post-dialysis weight.
  - **Reviewing Quality Metrics:** Checking monthly adequacy reports (spKt/V, URR) and hemoglobin trends for the panel of patients. [6]
  - **Troubleshooting the Access:** If a patient's flow rates are dropping or venous pressures are rising, the nephrologist orders a fistulogram and plans for angioplasty to save the failing access. [3]
- **Procedures:** A typical day may be punctuated by a percutaneous ultrasound-guided kidney biopsy, the insertion of a non-tunneled internal jugular dialysis catheter, or the supervision of a complex CRRT prescription in the ICU.
- **The End-of-Day Reconciliation:** A final check on all active inpatients, looking for missed morning labs, critical evening values (a potassium of 6.0 mmol/L), and ensuring the next day's dialysis schedule and orders are complete.

### 2. Nephrological Emergencies: The Must-Know List

These are the conditions that demand immediate recognition and action, as delay leads directly to mortality or permanent loss of kidney function.

### 2.1 Hyperkalemia (Life-Threatening)

Not just any high potassium, but a K+ level that is rising rapidly and has electrophysiological consequence. A K+ of 7.0 mmol/L with ECG changes (peaked T-waves, loss of P-waves, widened QRS) is a pre-cardiac arrest situation. The potassium must be stabilized immediately (IV calcium gluconate to protect the myocardium) and then shifted (insulin + dextrose, beta-agonists, bicarbonate). The definitive treatment is emergent hemodialysis to remove potassium from the body. [1]

### 2.2 Acute Pulmonary Edema in the Dialysis Patient

A patient on maintenance HD who missed a session, or a CKD G5 patient with no access, presenting with severe respiratory distress, hypoxia, and crackles. This is a volume-overload crisis. The definitive therapy is emergent isolated ultrafiltration (rapid fluid removal without solute clearance) or emergent hemodialysis. CPAP and IV nitrates are temporizing bridge measures. IV loop diuretics are futile in an anuric patient. [6]

### 2.3 Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis (RPGN)

This is the "renal code." A patient with a nephritic presentation (hematuria, proteinuria, mild hypertension) whose creatinine is rising daily. This is not a UTI or a stone; this is an active, crescentic glomerulonephritis (anti-GBM disease, ANCA vasculitis, or severe lupus). The urgency is because the crescents are forming hours to days, and delay means irreversible fibrosis and permanent dialysis dependence. The workup (serologies: ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, C3/C4) and a kidney biopsy must be performed within 24-48 hours. [7]

### 2.4 Dialysis Catheter-Related Bloodstream Infection (CRBSI)

A hemodialysis patient with a tunneled catheter who develops fevers and rigors during or shortly after a dialysis session. This is a clinical diagnosis for septicemia originating from the catheter hub. The nephrologist must draw blood cultures from the catheter lumens and peripherally, and start empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (covering Gram-positive organisms, particularly MRSA, and Gram-negatives) immediately. Catheter salvage or removal is the next critical decision, guided by the organism. Delayed treatment leads to endocarditis and septic shock. [3]

### 2.5 Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TTP, HUS)

A patient presenting with the classic pentad (or some of its components): microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes on blood smear), thrombocytopenia, AKI, fever, and neurological changes. TTP (ADAMTS13 deficiency) and HUS (complement or Shiga-toxin mediated) are both on the differential. The critical emergency is TTP, which has a 90% mortality without plasma exchange. The nephrologist must initiate empiric plasma exchange (PLEX) urgently, often before the ADAMTS13 level result returns, if the clinical suspicion is high. [7]

### 2.6 Malignant Hypertension / Scleroderma Renal Crisis

A patient with severe hypertension (often >200/120 mmHg), bilateral retinal hemorrhages and exudates (grade III/IV retinopathy), and rising creatinine. Scleroderma renal crisis is a specific and catastrophic subset occurring in patients with diffuse systemic sclerosis, presenting with a microangiopathic picture and AKI. The nephrologist's immediate action is an aggressive, controlled reduction in blood pressure, using an IV infusion (e.g., nicardipine, clevidipine) and initiating an oral ACE inhibitor, which is the specific, organ-saving therapy in scleroderma renal crisis, even if it causes a further creatinine rise initially. [1]

### 2.7 Severe Symptomatic Hyponatremia

A serum sodium that has fallen acutely (<125 mmol/L or a rapid drop) and is causing cerebral edema—manifesting as confusion, seizures, or coma. This is a neurological emergency. The nephrologist must immediately initiate controlled correction with hypertonic (3%) saline. The goal is a 4-6 mmol/L rapid rise to reverse cerebral herniation risk, while meticulously avoiding overcorrection and the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome. [1]

### 2.8 Urgent Indications for Dialysis (AEIOU)

These are the "code blue" indications for an emergency, unscheduled hemodialysis session. The nephrologist's brain is programmed to the "AEIOU" mnemonic:

- **A**cidosis: Severe, refractory metabolic acidosis (e.g., pH <7.1).
- **E**lectrolytes: Refractory, life-threatening hyperkalemia (usually K >6.5 with ECG changes).
- **I**ntoxications: Dialyzable toxins (see below).
- **O**verload: Volume overload causing pulmonary edema refractory to medical management.
- **U**remia: Uremic pericarditis (a friction rub is a harbinger of cardiac tamponade), uremic encephalopathy, or bleeding. [6]

### 2.9 Intoxications Requiring Dialysis

The nephrologist is the definitive consultant for specific poisonings. The classic, memorized list includes toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), salicylates (aspirin), lithium, and metformin (associated lactic acidosis). Hemodialysis actively removes the toxin and its toxic metabolites, and in some cases corrects the associated severe metabolic acidosis. Dialysis is a life-saving emergency procedure in these contexts. [1]

### 2.10 PD Peritonitis with Sepsis

A PD patient who calls the unit with cloudy fluid and severe abdominal pain, presenting to the hospital hypotensive and tachycardic. This is severe peritonitis with hemodynamic compromise. The nephrologist, who manages this entirely, must obtain PD fluid for cell count and culture, start empiric broad-spectrum intraperitoneal (IP) antibiotics immediately (cefazolin and ceftazidime, or vancomycin and an aminoglycoside), and admit the patient for IP and IV antibiotics and monitoring. Catheter removal is indicated if the infection is refractory. [6]

### 3. The Nephrologist's Defining Concerns: The Distinguishing Focus Areas

These are the clinical domains that a nephrologist views with an ownership and intensity that distinguishes the specialty from all others.

### 3.1 The Sanctity of the Long-Term Access Plan

No other specialist cares about a patient's arm veins as deeply as a nephrologist. From the moment a patient crosses into CKD G4, the nephrologist begins aggressively preserving the non-dominant arm's veins. This means a firm, explicit directive in the chart and to the patient: "No IVs, no blood draws, no blood pressure cuffs on this arm." The goal is to protect the venous real estate for a future life-sustaining arteriovenous fistula. The nephrologist thinks in terms of an access life plan: fistula first, then graft, then catheter as a last resort. [3]

### 3.2 Volume Status: The Sixth Vital Sign

To the nephrologist, volume status is not just a component of the physical exam; it is the central determinant of blood pressure, cardiovascular health, and dialysis tolerance. A nephrologist develops a refined, almost obsessive skill in estimating a patient's "dry weight." This is the post-dialysis weight below which the patient would become hypotensive. The clinical assessment—interrogating the JVP, checking for orthostasis, weighing the patient daily at the same time on the same scale—is the constant, daily practice of the specialty. [1]

### 3.3 The Urinalysis as a Personal Diagnostic Tool

The nephrologist does not delegate urine microscopy. The ability to spin a fresh urine sample in a bench centrifuge, resuspend the sediment, place a drop on a slide with a coverslip, and interpret it under a microscope with polarizing and phase-contrast capabilities is the defining diagnostic act of the specialty. Seeing an acanthocyte (a dysmorphic RBC with blebs, the "Mickey Mouse" cell) is pathognomonic for glomerular bleeding. Spotting a muddy brown granular cast instantly diagnoses acute tubular necrosis. This is a bedside skill that bypasses the lab and provides an immediate answer. [7]

### 3.4 Drug Dosing and Nephrotoxin Stewardship

The nephrologist is the ultimate guardian of medication safety in the setting of reduced kidney function. The "nephrotoxic timeout" is a daily ritual on rounds. It involves:

- **Absolute Avoidance:** Actively searching the medication chart for contraindicated nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides in AKI).
- **Dose Adjustment:** Manually calculating the eGFR and adjusting the dose of every renally-cleared drug (e.g., gabapentin, LMWH, beta-lactams). Pharmacists are essential partners, but the nephrologist is the final safety net. [4]
- **Contrast Stewardship:** Being the voice that questions every CT scan with IV contrast, exploring alternative imaging or prophylactic volume expansion protocols to prevent contrast-associated AKI. [1]

### 3.5 The Dialysis Prescription as a Precision Instrument

To an outsider, dialysis may seem like a standardized procedure. To the nephrologist, it is a precisely tailored biological manipulation. The prescription—written anew for each patient for each session—weighs multiple variables: the exact amount of fluid to remove (ultrafiltration volume), the ideal rate of removal to prevent hemodynamic collapse (UF rate <10-13 mL/kg/hr), the dialysate composition (potassium, calcium, bicarbonate, sodium) that will gently correct electrolyte abnormalities without triggering cardiac arrhythmias, and the anticoagulation protocol (heparin vs. citrate) to keep the circuit running. [6]

### 3.6 The End-Stage Kidney Disease Life Plan

Managing advanced CKD is not just about reaching dialysis. The nephrologist is uniquely responsible for guiding a patient through a life-altering decision matrix. This is the comprehensive, pre-emptive ESKD Life Plan, which aims to start the right patient on the right modality at the right time, without a crisis. The plan asks: Is the patient a candidate for a pre-emptive living donor transplant? If on dialysis, are they a candidate for peritoneal dialysis or home hemodialysis, which offer better outcomes and quality of life? If in-center HD is inevitable, has a fistula been created and is it mature? And, fundamentally, is the patient's best option a non-dialytic conservative care pathway focused on quality of life? The nephrologist orchestrates this entire plan. [5]

### 3.7 The Cardio-Renal-Metabolic Axis

The nephrologist now operates firmly at the intersection of cardiology, endocrinology, and metabolism. They do not see CKD in isolation but as a component of a systemic syndrome. This understanding has been revolutionized by the SGLT2 inhibitor class. A nephrologist now thinks: "This patient has proteinuric CKD and heart failure. The foundational therapy is an SGLT2 inhibitor, as it simultaneously slows nephron loss, reduces cardiovascular mortality, and prevents hyperkalemia from RAAS inhibitors." This holistic, mechanism-based approach is the modern defining feature of the discipline. [5]

### 4. The Essential Guidelines a Nephrologist Must Follow

A nephrologist's practice is entirely frameworked by these documents. To be current is to live inside these guidelines.

### 4.1 KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes)

KDIGO is the single most authoritative global guideline body in nephrology. Its documents are the nephrologist's "Bible." The critical, must-know KDIGO guidelines are:

- **KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease:** The absolute masterpiece. This is the new global standard that integrates SGLT2 inhibitors as foundational care, defines the GFR and albuminuria heat map, and provides a comprehensive roadmap for every aspect of CKD care from diagnosis to RRT. It is the single most important guideline to read and re-read. [5]
- **KDIGO 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Blood Pressure in CKD:** Defines the SBP target of <120 mmHg for most CKD patients based on SPRINT trial evidence, utilizing standardized blood pressure measurement. [8]
- **KDIGO 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Glomerular Diseases:** A monumental text that serves as the pathological and therapeutic atlas for every primary and secondary GN. [7]
- **KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline for Acute Kidney Injury (2012, with ongoing updates):** Defines the staging of AKI and management bundles. [9]
- **KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline on the Evaluation and Care of Living Kidney Donors (2017).**
- **KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline for the Care of the Kidney Transplant Recipient (2009, with ongoing topic updates).**

### 4.2 KDOQI (Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative)

The National Kidney Foundation's (NKF) KDOQI guidelines provide the evidence-based foundation for vascular access, nutrition, and dialysis adequacy in the United States.

- **KDOQI Clinical Practice Guideline for Vascular Access: 2019 Update:** The comprehensive, absolute reference for everything related to fistula, graft, and catheter management. It defines the "End Stage Kidney Disease Life-Plan" approach to access. [3]
- **KDOQI Clinical Practice Guideline for Nutrition in CKD: 2020 Update.**
- **KDOQI Clinical Practice Guideline for Hemodialysis Adequacy: 2015 Update.** Defines the target Kt/V. [6]

### 4.3 AHA/ACC Guidelines (Cardiovascular Risk in CKD)

The nephrologist must manage the leading cause of death in their patients. They must know the latest joint committee guidelines, which will be integrated into their practice. The 2025 update to the AHA/ACC Hypertension and CPR/ECC guidelines will be seminal. The nephrologist specifically focuses on the sections relevant to hypertension management in CKD, secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease, and the management of heart failure. The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure is critical reading, as it codifies the role of SGLT2 inhibitors in heart failure, a therapy initiated by nephrologists. [10]

### 4.4 ADA Standards of Care (Diabetic Kidney Disease)

Published annually in the journal *Diabetes Care*, this is the authoritative guide on the screening, prevention, and management of diabetic kidney disease, the leading cause of ESKD worldwide. The nephrologist strictly follows its algorithm, which now mandates SGLT2 inhibitors for all patients with type 2 diabetes and evidence of DKD to slow progression, and finerenone (a non-steroidal MRA) for those with residual albuminuria and hyperkalemia risk. [11]

### 4.5 ASN/ERA-EDTA/ISN Position Statements and Consensus Documents

Where KDIGO guidelines are silent or out of date, these major societies fill the gap with rapidly published consensus statements. A current example is the joint statement on the definition and classification of resistant hypertension, or consensus on the management of onconephrology cases. The nephrologist monitors these publications in journals like *JASN* and *Kidney International*.

### 4.6 Other Critical Guidelines

- **Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) Guidelines:** Essential for managing sepsis-associated AKI and delivering effective CRRT in the ICU. [12]
- **The Onconephrology Forum and American Society of Onconephrology (ASON) Consensus Statements:** Provide the framework for managing the novel nephrotoxicities of checkpoint inhibitors and modern targeted cancer therapies.
- **ACOG Guidelines (Obstetric Nephrology):** For managing hypertension and AKI in pregnancy (pre-eclampsia).
- **CDC/ACIP Vaccination Schedules:** The nephrologist must know the modified, augmented vaccine schedule for CKD and dialysis patients (hepatitis B, pneumococcal, influenza, herpes zoster). [2]

### Nephrology Disease Classification

### I. Classification by Anatomical Compartment

**A. Prerenal (Functional) Disorders**

Diseases resulting from hypoperfusion of the kidneys without intrinsic parenchymal damage. The kidney is structurally normal but functionally impaired due to decreased renal blood flow. [1]

- Hypovolemia: Hemorrhage, dehydration, gastrointestinal losses, diuretic overuse, burns.
- Decreased effective arterial blood volume: Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, sepsis.
- Renal artery stenosis: Atherosclerotic, fibromuscular dysplasia.
- Pharmacological impairment of autoregulation: NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, calcineurin inhibitors.
- Hepatorenal syndrome: Functional renal failure in advanced liver disease with portal hypertension.

**B. Intrinsic Renal (Parenchymal) Disorders**

Diseases that directly damage the renal parenchyma, affecting one or more of the four major structural components: glomeruli, tubules, interstitium, and vasculature. [1,2]

### Glomerular Diseases

  - *Nephrotic Syndrome*: Minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), diabetic nephropathy, amyloidosis, lupus nephritis (class V).
  - *Nephritic Syndrome*: Post-infectious glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis (class III/IV), anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's), ANCA-associated vasculitis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis), Henoch-Schönlein purpura.
  - *Asymptomatic Urinary Abnormalities*: Isolated hematuria (IgA nephropathy, thin basement membrane nephropathy, Alport syndrome), isolated proteinuria.

### Tubulointerstitial Diseases

  - *Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)*: Ischemic ATN, nephrotoxic ATN (aminoglycosides, cisplatin, amphotericin B, radiocontrast, myoglobin, hemoglobin).
  - *Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN)*: Drug-induced (beta-lactams, NSAIDs, proton pump inhibitors, sulfonamides), infection-related, autoimmune (sarcoidosis, Sjögren's, IgG4-related disease), idiopathic.
  - *Chronic Tubulointerstitial Nephritis*: Analgesic nephropathy, chronic lithium toxicity, lead nephropathy, aristolochic acid nephropathy (Balkan nephropathy), chronic obstructive nephropathy, sickle cell nephropathy.
  - *Cystic Diseases*: Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD), medullary sponge kidney, nephronophthisis, simple renal cysts, acquired cystic kidney disease.
  - *Myeloma Cast Nephropathy*: Light chain precipitation in distal tubules.

### Vascular Diseases

  - *Large Vessel*: Renal artery stenosis (atherosclerotic, fibromuscular dysplasia), renal artery thrombosis/embolism, renal vein thrombosis.
  - *Small Vessel (Microvascular)*: Thrombotic microangiopathies (hemolytic uremic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura), malignant hypertension/scleroderma renal crisis, atheroembolic renal disease (cholesterol emboli), scleroderma renal disease, preeclampsia/eclampsia.
  - *Vasculitis*: Polyarteritis nodosa, Takayasu arteritis (involving renal arteries), ANCA-associated vasculitis.

**C. Postrenal (Obstructive) Disorders**

Diseases causing obstruction to urine flow at any level from the renal pelvis to the urethral meatus. Bilateral obstruction or unilateral obstruction in a solitary kidney is required to cause significant azotemia. [1]

- *Intraluminal*: Stones, blood clots, sloughed papillae, fungal balls.
- *Intramural*: Urothelial carcinoma, ureteral stricture, posterior urethral valves, benign prostatic hyperplasia, neurogenic bladder.
- *Extramural*: Retroperitoneal fibrosis, pelvic malignancy (cervical, prostate, colorectal), pregnancy, retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, pelvic radiation.

### II. Classification by Clinical Syndrome

**A. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)**

An abrupt (within hours to days) decrease in kidney function, defined by KDIGO criteria: increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or increase to ≥1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. [3]

- *Staging (KDIGO)*: Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3 based on creatinine rise and urine output.
- *Etiological Classification*: Prerenal (55-60%), intrinsic ATN (35-40%), postrenal (<5%).

**B. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)**

Abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for >3 months with implications for health. Defined by GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² or markers of kidney damage (albuminuria, urine sediment abnormalities, electrolyte disorders, histological abnormalities, structural abnormalities on imaging, history of kidney transplantation). [4]

- *Staging by GFR (KDIGO)*:
  - G1: ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m² (normal or high).
  - G2: 60-89 (mildly decreased).
  - G3a: 45-59 (mildly to moderately decreased).
  - G3b: 30-44 (moderately to severely decreased).
  - G4: 15-29 (severely decreased).
  - G5: <15 (kidney failure).
- *Staging by Albuminuria (KDIGO)*:
  - A1: <30 mg/g creatinine (normal to mildly increased).
  - A2: 30-300 mg/g (moderately increased, formerly microalbuminuria).
  - A3: >300 mg/g (severely increased, formerly macroalbuminuria).
- *Etiological Classification*: Diabetic kidney disease, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, glomerulonephritis, tubulointerstitial disease, cystic kidney disease, obstructive nephropathy, unknown.

**C. End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD)**

The final common pathway of progressive CKD where kidney function is insufficient for long-term survival without renal replacement therapy. Defined by GFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m² with uremic symptoms or complications necessitating dialysis or transplantation. [4]

**D. Nephrotic Syndrome**

A clinical syndrome characterized by massive proteinuria (>3.5 g/24 h), hypoalbuminemia (<3.0 g/dL), peripheral edema, hyperlipidemia, and lipiduria. Results from increased glomerular permeability to proteins due to podocyte injury or basement membrane disruption. [1,2]

**E. Nephritic Syndrome**

Acute onset of hematuria (dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts), hypertension, oliguria, and variable degrees of proteinuria (usually <3.5 g/day) and renal impairment. Represents glomerular inflammation with breaks in the glomerular basement membrane. [1,2]

### F. Tubular Syndromes

- *Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)*: Distal (Type 1), Proximal (Type 2), Hyperkalemic (Type 4), Combined (Type 3).
- *Fanconi Syndrome*: Generalized proximal tubular dysfunction with phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, glycosuria, bicarbonaturia, and uricosuria.
- *Bartter Syndrome*: Defect in thick ascending limb; hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism with normal blood pressure.
- *Gitelman Syndrome*: Defect in distal convoluted tubule; hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hypocalciuria.
- *Liddle Syndrome*: Gain-of-function ENaC mutation; hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, suppressed renin and aldosterone.
- *Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus*: Resistance to ADH at the collecting duct; polyuria, polydipsia, hypernatremia.

### G. Urinary Tract Infection Syndromes

- *Cystitis*: Infection of the bladder; dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain.
- *Pyelonephritis*: Infection of the renal parenchyma; fever, chills, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea/vomiting.
- *Renal/Perinephric Abscess*: Complicated infection with loculated pus.
- *Emphysematous Pyelonephritis*: Gas-forming infection, usually in diabetics.

**H. Nephrolithiasis**

Formation of calculi within the urinary tract. Classified by stone composition: calcium oxalate (most common, 60-70%), calcium phosphate (15-20%), uric acid (5-10%), struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate, 5-10%, associated with urease-producing organisms), cystine (1-2%, in cystinuria). [5]

### III. Classification by Histopathology (Glomerular Diseases)

**A. Non-Proliferative Glomerulopathies**

No increase in glomerular cellularity.

### Minimal Change Disease (MCD)

  - Light microscopy: Normal or minimal mesangial prominence.
  - Immunofluorescence: No immune deposits.
  - Electron microscopy: Diffuse podocyte foot process effacement.
  - Most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (90% of cases in children <10 years). [2]

### Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)

  - Light microscopy: Segmental sclerosis and hyalinosis in some glomeruli (focal), with tubulointerstitial fibrosis proportional to glomerular sclerosis.
  - Immunofluorescence: IgM and C3 in sclerotic segments.
  - Electron microscopy: Diffuse podocyte foot process effacement.
  - *Histological Variants*: Perihilar, cellular, tip lesion, collapsing, not otherwise specified (NOS). Collapsing variant has worst prognosis.

### Membranous Nephropathy

  - Light microscopy: Diffuse glomerular basement membrane thickening; subepithelial spikes on silver stain.
  - Immunofluorescence: Granular capillary loop IgG and C3.
  - Electron microscopy: Subepithelial electron-dense deposits, podocyte foot process effacement.
  - *Primary (idiopathic)*: Anti-PLA2R antibodies (70-80% of primary cases).
  - *Secondary*: Malignancy, SLE (class V), drugs (NSAIDs, penicillamine, gold), infections (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis).

### Diabetic Nephropathy

  - Light microscopy: Mesangial expansion, glomerular basement membrane thickening, nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules), afferent and efferent arteriolar hyalinosis.
  - Immunofluorescence: Linear IgG staining of GBM and tubular basement membrane (non-immune, due to increased permeability).
  - Electron microscopy: Homogeneous basement membrane thickening, increased mesangial matrix.

### Amyloidosis

  - AL amyloidosis: Light chain deposition in primary amyloidosis or multiple myeloma.
  - AA amyloidosis: Serum amyloid A protein deposition in chronic inflammatory conditions.
  - Congo Red stain: Apple-green birefringence under polarized light.
  - Electron microscopy: Randomly oriented, non-branching fibrils 8-12 nm in diameter.

**B. Proliferative Glomerulonephritis**

Increased glomerular cellularity (mesangial, endothelial, epithelial, inflammatory cells).

### IgA Nephropathy (Berger Disease)

  - Light microscopy: Mesangial expansion and hypercellularity; may have crescents.
  - Immunofluorescence: Dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA deposition with C3.
  - Electron microscopy: Mesangial electron-dense deposits.
  - Most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide.

### Post-Infectious (Post-Streptococcal) Glomerulonephritis

  - Light microscopy: Diffuse endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis with neutrophils.
  - Immunofluorescence: Granular capillary loop and mesangial IgG and C3 ("starry sky" pattern).
  - Electron microscopy: Large subepithelial electron-dense deposits ("humps").
  - Typically follows group A beta-hemolytic streptococcal pharyngitis (1-3 weeks) or skin infection (3-6 weeks).

### Lupus Nephritis (ISN/RPS Classification)

  - Class I: Minimal mesangial.
  - Class II: Mesangial proliferative.
  - Class III: Focal proliferative (<50% of glomeruli).
  - Class IV: Diffuse proliferative (≥50% of glomeruli); worst prognosis.
  - Class V: Membranous.
  - Class VI: Advanced sclerosing.
  - Immunofluorescence: "Full house" pattern (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q).

### Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis (MPGN)

  - *Immune Complex-Mediated*: MPGN with subendothelial (Type I) or intramembranous (Type III) deposits; associated with infections (hepatitis C with cryoglobulinemia), autoimmune diseases (SLE), monoclonal gammopathies.
  - *Complement-Mediated (C3 Glomerulopathy)*: C3 glomerulonephritis, dense deposit disease (Type II); associated with autoantibodies to C3 convertase (C3 nephritic factor).
  - Light microscopy: Mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity, GBM double contour ("tram-tracking").
  - Immunofluorescence: C3 predominance in C3 glomerulopathy; IgG and C3 in immune complex MPGN.

### Anti-GBM Disease (Goodpasture Disease)

  - Light microscopy: Diffuse crescentic glomerulonephritis; segmental necrosis.
  - Immunofluorescence: Linear IgG deposition along GBM (also linear IgG along tubular basement membrane).
  - Serum anti-GBM antibodies present.
  - May present with pulmonary hemorrhage (anti-GBM cross-reactivity with alveolar basement membrane).

### ANCA-Associated Vasculitis (Pauci-Immune)

  - Light microscopy: Focal segmental necrotizing and crescentic glomerulonephritis; little or no glomerular hypercellularity.
  - Immunofluorescence: Paucity of immune deposits (hence "pauci-immune").
  - Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA): c-ANCA/PR3 positive.
  - Microscopic Polyangiitis (MPA): p-ANCA/MPO positive.
  - Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA): p-ANCA/MPO positive in ~40%, asthma, eosinophilia.

### C. Hereditary Glomerulopathies

### Alport Syndrome

  - X-linked (80%): COL4A5 mutation.
  - Autosomal recessive (15%): COL4A3, COL4A4 mutations.
  - Autosomal dominant (5%): COL4A3, COL4A4 mutations.
  - Electron microscopy: Lamellated, "basket-weave" appearance of GBM.
  - Clinical: Hematuria, sensorineural hearing loss, ocular abnormalities (anterior lenticonus).

### Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy (Benign Familial Hematuria)

  - Heterozygous COL4A3/COL4A4 mutation.
  - Electron microscopy: Diffuse thinning of GBM (<250 nm in adults).
  - Clinical: Persistent microscopic hematuria, normal renal function, excellent prognosis.

### IV. Classification by Etiology

**A. Genetic/Congenital**

ADPKD (PKD1, PKD2), ARPKD (PKHD1), Alport syndrome, thin basement membrane nephropathy, Fabry disease (alpha-galactosidase A deficiency), cystinosis, oxalosis, congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT).

**B. Metabolic**

Diabetic nephropathy, hyperuricemic nephropathy, hypercalcemic nephropathy, hypokalemic nephropathy, oxalate nephropathy, cystinuria.

**C. Immunologic/Autoimmune**

SLE, ANCA-associated vasculitis, anti-GBM disease, IgA nephropathy, primary membranous nephropathy, minimal change disease, FSGS (primary forms), Sjögren's tubulointerstitial nephritis, IgG4-related tubulointerstitial nephritis.

**D. Infectious**

Post-streptococcal GN, hepatitis B-associated MN/MPGN, hepatitis C-associated MPGN/cryoglobulinemia, HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN, collapsing FSGS), pyelonephritis, renal abscess, schistosomiasis, tuberculosis.

### E. Toxic/Nephrotoxic

- *Drugs*: Aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin, tenofovir, bisphosphonates, NSAIDs, PPIs, lithium, calcineurin inhibitors, vancomycin, polymyxins, ifosfamide.
- *Radiocontrast*: Contrast-induced AKI.
- *Heavy metals*: Lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic.
- *Environmental toxins*: Aristolochic acid (Balkan nephropathy), ochratoxin.
- *Endogenous*: Myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobin (hemolysis), light chains (myeloma), uric acid (tumor lysis syndrome), calcium/phosphate (tumor lysis).

**F. Hemodynamic**

Renal artery stenosis, malignant hypertension, heart failure, cirrhosis, hepatorenal syndrome, cardiorenal syndrome types 1-5.

**G. Neoplastic/Paraneoplastic**

Renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, membranous nephropathy (associated with solid tumors), minimal change disease (associated with Hodgkin lymphoma), FSGS (associated with various malignancies), myeloma cast nephropathy, amyloidosis (AL and AA), tumor lysis syndrome.

**H. Obstructive**

Nephrolithiasis, benign prostatic hyperplasia, pelvic malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis, posterior urethral valves, ureteropelvic junction obstruction.

**I. Pregnancy-Related**

Preeclampsia/eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, pregnancy-associated thrombotic microangiopathy, physiological hydronephrosis of pregnancy.

**J. Infiltrative**

Amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, lymphoma, leukemia, monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease, light chain deposition disease.

### V. Classification by Temporal Course

**A. Acute (Hours to Days)**

Acute tubular necrosis, acute interstitial nephritis, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (crescentic GN: anti-GBM, ANCA, immune complex), acute post-streptococcal GN, hemolytic uremic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, acute pyelonephritis, acute renal artery/vein thrombosis, acute obstructive nephropathy, prerenal azotemia, cholesterol emboli syndrome.

**B. Subacute (Weeks to Months)**

Some forms of crescentic GN, lupus nephritis, mixed cryoglobulinemia, progressive IgA nephropathy with crescents, post-infectious GN with delayed resolution, drug-induced interstitial nephritis with continued exposure.

**C. Chronic (Months to Years)**

Diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, chronic glomerulonephritis (IgA, FSGS, membranous, MPGN), ADPKD, chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, ischemic nephropathy (renal artery stenosis), reflux nephropathy, chronic obstructive nephropathy.

**D. Relapsing/Remitting**

Minimal change disease, FSGS (post-transplant recurrence), membranous nephropathy (anti-PLA2R antibody titers), lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis (relapse rate 30-50% within 5 years), IgA nephropathy (recurrent macroscopic hematuria with mucosal infections).

### VI. Classification by Kidney Function Level (KDIGO CKD Classification) [4]

**A. By Cause (C)**

Identify the specific cause of kidney disease (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, transplant).

### B. By GFR (G)

- G1: GFR ≥90 (normal or high)
- G2: GFR 60-89 (mildly decreased)
- G3a: GFR 45-59 (mildly to moderately decreased)
- G3b: GFR 30-44 (moderately to severely decreased)
- G4: GFR 15-29 (severely decreased)
- G5: GFR <15 (kidney failure)

### C. By Albuminuria (A)

- A1: ACR <30 mg/g (<3 mg/mmol) — normal to mildly increased
- A2: ACR 30-300 mg/g (3-30 mg/mmol) — moderately increased
- A3: ACR >300 mg/g (>30 mg/mmol) — severely increased

### VII. Classification by Electrolyte and Acid-Base Disorders

**A. Disorders of Sodium and Water**

Hyponatremia (hypovolemic, euvolemic, hypervolemic), hypernatremia (hypovolemic, euvolemic, hypervolemic), diabetes insipidus (central, nephrogenic), SIADH.

**B. Disorders of Potassium**

Hypokalemia (decreased intake, increased cellular uptake, increased losses: renal, gastrointestinal), hyperkalemia (increased intake, transcellular shift, decreased renal excretion).

**C. Disorders of Acid-Base**

Metabolic acidosis (normal anion gap: RTA, diarrhea; elevated anion gap: uremia, ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, toxins), metabolic alkalosis (chloride-responsive, chloride-resistant), respiratory acidosis, respiratory alkalosis, mixed disorders.

**D. Disorders of Calcium and Phosphate**

Hypocalcemia, hypercalcemia, hypophosphatemia, hyperphosphatemia.

**E. Disorders of Magnesium**

Hypomagnesemia (renal losses: loop diuretics, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, Gitelman syndrome; GI losses), hypermagnesemia.
