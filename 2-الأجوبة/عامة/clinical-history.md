### Section I: The Chief Concern (CC) and the Opening Inquiry

The Purpose: Setting the Stage for the Differential Diagnosis

Most students are taught to ask: "What brings you in today?"

This is correct, but the real skill is what you do with the first 60 seconds of the answer. You are not just recording a string of words; you are triaging the system involved and assessing the acuity.

### The Practical Application: The "Open-to-Closed" Cone

Do not start with a binary question (e.g., "Is the pain sharp or dull?"). You must start with an open-ended directive.

What you say: "Tell me about the problem that brought you here today. Use your own words."

Why this matters (The Hidden Data):

Mental Status & Language: You immediately assess if the patient is oriented, if there is dysarthria, dysphasia, or a thought disorder (e.g., flight of ideas, circumstantiality).

Functional Impact: A patient with a STEMI might say, "I was just sitting and... I can't breathe." A patient with chronic arthritis might say, "Well, it started about 3 years ago when I was gardening..." The verb choice and temporal anchor (acute vs. insidious) are the first clues to the pathological process (Vascular/Inflammatory vs. Degenerative).

The "Doorway Diagnosis": As they speak, you are filtering into broad categories based on their first few words:

### "I can't catch my breath..." ➔ Cardiopulmonary

### "My stomach has been killing me..." ➔ GI/GU/Vascular

### "I woke up and my arm was dead..." ➔ Neurological Emergency

Practical Drill: After they finish their first paragraph (do not interrupt for 60-90 seconds), summarize it back to them: "So, if I understand correctly, you were fine until this morning when you felt a heavy weight on your chest while walking the dog." This is reflective listening. It builds trust and corrects any immediate misinterpretation.

Section II: The History of Presenting Illness (HPI) - The 7 Dimensions Deconstructed

The mnemonic OLDCARTS (or SOCRATES) is standard, but it is useless without understanding WHY each letter points to a specific pathology. Let's apply it to the cardinal symptom of Pain.

### 1. Onset

The Question: "What exactly were you doing the second this started?"

The Goal: Distinguish Vascular Catastrophe from Inflammatory Flare from Mechanical Strain.

Sudden, Maximal Severity at Onset: "I felt a pop and then the worst headache of my life." ➔ Red Flag. Think: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, Aortic Dissection, Renal Colic.

Gradual Over Hours/Days: "It got worse as the day went on." ➔ Think: Infection, Inflammatory Arthritis.

Related to Exertion: "Only when I climb stairs." ➔ Think: Stable Angina, Pulmonary Hypertension.

### 2. Location & Radiation

The Question: "Point with one finger to where it hurts the most. Does it travel anywhere else?"

The Goal: Anatomical Precision.

Patient Points to Epigastrium, but clutches fist over sternum: This is Levine's Sign. The patient's hand gesture often diagnoses myocardial ischemia before the EKG does.

Radiation to the Scapula: Biliary colic or, more dangerously, Aortic Dissection.

Radiation to the Groin: Ureteral calculus (Kidney stone).

### 3. Duration & Frequency

The Question: "How long does each episode last? How many seconds? Minutes? Hours?"

The Goal: Is this Colic (hollow organ spasm) or Inflammation (constant)?

Lasts < 2 minutes: Likely Musculoskeletal spasm or Tic Douloureux.

Lasts 2-15 minutes: Angina Pectoris. This is a hallmark. If chest pain lasts seconds or all day, it is unlikely to be cardiac ischemia.

Lasts Hours, Constant: Myocardial Infarction, Pancreatitis, Musculoskeletal Strain.

### 4. Character

The Question: "Describe the feeling. Is it a pressure, a stabbing, a burn, or an ache?"

The Goal: Tissue Origin.

Tearing/Ripping: Aortic Dissection. This is a must-ask word. If the patient says "tearing," you stop the routine history and move to vascular emergency protocol.

Crampy/Griping: Hollow Viscus Obstruction (Bowel, Bile Duct, Ureter). It builds and fades as peristalsis fights the blockage.

Burning: Neuropathic (e.g., shingles before rash) or Chemical (GERD).

Pressure/Heaviness: Ischemia (Angina, Compartment Syndrome).

### 5. Aggravating & Alleviating Factors

The Question: "What makes it worse? What makes it better? What position did you sleep in last night?"

The Goal: Mechanical vs. Inflammatory vs. Ischemic.

Worse with Movement, Better with Rest: Mechanical/Musculoskeletal.

Worse with Eating (esp. fatty food): Biliary Colic.

Relieved by Leaning Forward: Pericarditis or Pancreatitis. (This is a classic, high-yield physical exam correlation).

Relieved by Nitroglycerin: Suggests smooth muscle relaxation (Coronary Vasospasm, Esophageal Spasm).

6. Associated Symptoms (The "Review of Systems" Within the HPI)

The Question: "When you have this pain, what else happens to your body?"

The Goal: Systemic Connection.

Pain + Sweating (Diaphoresis) + Nausea: The "Vagal Triad". Regardless of what the patient calls the pain, this combination is Ischemia (Cardiac or Mesenteric) until proven otherwise.

Pain + Vision Changes + Jaw Claudication: Giant Cell Arteritis.

Pain + Rash: Vasculitis or Herpes Zoster.

Section III: The Past Medical History - Why It's Not Just a List

The Mistake: "Do you have any medical problems?" (Patient: "No.")

The Correction: Targeted Hypothesis Testing.

You do not ask about every disease. You ask about diseases that change the differential for their chief concern.

### Chest Pain

"Has anyone ever told you about your cholesterol, high blood pressure, or diabetes?"

You are establishing Pre-Test Probability for CAD. If they have 3 risk factors, even atypical pain is high-risk.

### Shortness of Breath

### Section IV: The Medication History - The Hidden Clues

The Mistake: Asking "What pills do you take?" without follow-up.

The Practical Skill: Phenotyping the Patient by Pharmacy.

You must ask Why? for each drug. The drug is often the most accurate diagnosis the patient has.

Furosemide, Lisinopril, Carvedilol: This is the GDMT Triad for Heart Failure. Even if the patient denies "heart problems," their pill bottle tells you they have a cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction.

Metformin, Empagliflozin, Insulin: Confirms Type 2 Diabetes. Now every symptom of "dizziness" must be ruled out for Hypoglycemia or Autonomic Neuropathy.

Apixaban or Warfarin: Atrial Fibrillation or DVT/PE. This means they are at high risk for Hemorrhagic Stroke if they hit their head, and Ischemic Stroke if they stop the drug.

Section V: The Social History - Beyond Cigarettes and Alcohol

The Goal: Risk Stratification for the Specific Complaint.

The "Tobacco Pack-Year" Calculation: "How many packs a day? For how many years?"

Why: A 50 pack-year history + Hemoptysis = Lung Cancer until proven otherwise.

Why: A 50 pack-year history + Leg Pain = Peripheral Artery Disease.

The "Where Were You Born/Have You Traveled?" Question:

Why: Eosinophilia + Immigrant from sub-Saharan Africa = Strongyloides or Schistosomiasis. This is a "Hidden in Plain Sight" diagnosis missed in Western clinics daily.

The "Who Lives With You?" Question:

Why: It's not just social work. It tells you about Functional Baseline and Discharge Planning. If an 85-year-old lives alone on a 3rd floor walk-up, they cannot go home with a new diagnosis of unstable angina. You must think Rehab placement now, not on discharge day.

القصة هي جمع المعلومات اللازمة لوضع التشاخيص التفريقية بالطريقة التي تعطي انطباع إيجابي للمريض وتكسبه ثقة المريض وتمكن الطبيب من الحصول على اكبر قدر ممكن من الأجوبة والمعلومات  (ماذا اسال و كيف اساله)

القصة ليست مجرد حوار عادي بين شخصين , هي تركيز تام وتشغيل كامل الحواس لالتقاط كل ما يقوله ويعبر عنه المريض (أجوبة الأسئلة , طريقة الجواب , تخوفات المريض , قلق المريض , ما يتجنبه او يخفيه المريض) واستخدام هذه المعلومات لتعبئه النموذج المسبق في ذهن الطبيب لوضع الصورة السريرية لما يشتكي منه المريض ومقارنته مع الصورة السريرية للامراض المختلفة

القصة السريرية هي الأساس في مقاربة أي مريض (تم اجراء دراسة بحيث سمحوا للأطباء باخذ القصة السريرية فقط او عرضو عليهم القصة بدون ان يسمحوا لهم بالقيام بالفحص او طلب أي وسيلة استقصائية وطلبوا منهم ان يضعو التشخيص حيث ان الأطباء استطاعو تشخيص 80 – 90 ٪ من المرض بناء على القصة فقط)

تلعب القصة الدور الرئيسي للتشخيص في معظم الحالات والامراض يوجد بعض الحالات التي يلعب بها الفحص او الاستقصاءات دور اكبر (مثال: ECG في احتشاء العضلة القلبية , الفحص السريري في اختصاص الاذنية /حيث تشاهد الافة بشكل مباشر من العين) ولكن هذا لا يعني اهمال او تهميش القصة حيث انه بدون القصة لما امكن اجراء الاستقصاء المناسب او فحص الجهاز المناسب (بدون قصة الم صدري لما تم طلب اجراء تخطيط القلب) وفي حالات أخرى تلعب القصة الدور المطلق (الشكايات والامراض النفسية)

لتصور مدى أهمية القصة ممكن تخيل السيناريو الاتي انت كطبيب عرضت عليك مريض بمشكلة ما ومنعت عليك التحدث مع المريض ومن الغير مسموح للمريض ان يخبرك باي شيء وطلب منك ان تضع التشخيص , الأمور سيكون اصعب بكثير و اكثر تكلفة بكثير مقارنة مع القصة (القصة تخبرك على أي جهاز تركز بالفحص وما هي الاستقصاءات الواجب طلبها) وفي حالات مستحيل ان تضع التشخيص بدون قصة

Your experience with history taking and physical examination will grow, and will trigger the steps of clinical reasoning from the first moments of the patient encounter: identifying symptoms and abnormal findings; linking findings to underlying pathophysiology or psychopathology; and establishing and testing a set of explanatory hypotheses
