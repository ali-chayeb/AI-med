## First: Definition and General Overview

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) is defined as a sudden (within hours to days) decrease in kidney function, resulting in the inability to maintain fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base balance, and the accumulation of nitrogenous waste products (urea and creatinine).[1] It is not a single disease but a heterogeneous clinical syndrome encompassing multiple etiologies. The current consensus definition, established by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, relies on a rise in serum creatinine or a decrease in urine output.[2]

AKI has replaced the older term "acute renal failure" to emphasize the spectrum of injury from minor changes in renal function to the need for renal replacement therapy. The KDIGO definition stages AKI severity into three stages based on the magnitude of creatinine rise or duration of oliguria.[2] Prompt recognition and management are critical, as AKI is a powerful predictor of adverse outcomes, including progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and death.[1]

## Second: Epidemiology

AKI is a common and severe global health problem. It occurs in approximately 10–15% of all hospital admissions, with incidence rising to over 50% in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, where over 20% require renal replacement therapy.[3,4] It is a major cause of in-hospital morbidity and mortality. The mortality rate for all AKI is substantial, but it is highest among ICU patients, particularly those with severe AKI requiring dialysis, where mortality exceeds 50%.[3,5]

AKI affects all age groups but is most common in the elderly, who have a reduced renal reserve and a higher burden of comorbid conditions.[4] There is no strong sex predilection, though some epidemiological studies suggest a slight male predominance. The incidence is higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where it often affects younger individuals without pre-existing comorbidities due to community-acquired causes like infection (e.g., malaria, leptospirosis), toxins, and obstetric complications, representing a neglected disease entity that warrants greater global attention.[6,7]

AKI is an important cause of morbidity, associated with longer hospital stays, de novo or progressive CKD, and significant disability.[3] Screening for AKI in high-risk hospitalized patients (e.g., those with sepsis, shock, or exposure to nephrotoxins) is crucial and can reduce morbidity. Risk factors include:

- **Advanced Age:** Age-related decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal functional reserve.[4]
- **Pre-existing Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD):** The most significant risk factor; diseased kidneys are more vulnerable to further injury.[2]
- **Diabetes Mellitus:** Causes diabetic nephropathy and predisposes to contrast-induced and prerenal AKI.[8]
- **Sepsis:** The most common cause of AKI in the ICU, responsible for nearly 50% of cases, due to a complex interaction of hemodynamic, inflammatory, and direct tubular injury.[9]
- **Major Surgery:** Especially cardiac surgery requiring cardiopulmonary bypass, due to hypotension, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and inflammation.[10]
- **Hypovolemia:** Causes prerenal AKI by reducing renal perfusion.[2]
- **Nephrotoxins:** A wide array of drugs (e.g., aminoglycosides, vancomycin, NSAIDs, chemotherapeutic agents) and exogenous substances (e.g., radiocontrast dye) directly injure renal tubules.[11]
- **Heart Failure and Liver Cirrhosis:** Both lead to hepatorenal or cardiorenal syndromes via complex hemodynamic and neurohormonal mechanisms that reduce effective renal perfusion.[12]

## Third: Typical Disease Story / Model Case History

The clinical presentation of AKI is highly variable and depends entirely on the underlying cause. There is no single "typical" story; instead, a pattern recognition approach must integrate the patient's baseline risk and the clinical context. The most common framework is to categorize AKI into prerenal, intrinsic renal (acute tubular necrosis being most common), and postrenal causes.

- **The Case of "Prerenal AKI" (Functional):** An 85-year-old nursing home resident with diabetes and hypertension is brought in with a 3-day history of profuse watery diarrhea, confusion, and decreased oral intake. She takes lisinopril and furosemide daily. On examination, she is confused, has dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, a heart rate of 110 bpm, and a blood pressure of 90/55 mmHg. Key features: history of volume loss (diarrhea, poor intake), use of drugs that impair renal autoregulation (ACE inhibitor, diuretic), objective signs of volume depletion, and a low blood pressure. Lab findings will show a disproportionately high BUN:Creatinine ratio (>20:1), concentrated urine (high specific gravity), and a low urine sodium (<20 mEq/L). The condition resolves completely within 24-48 hours after restoring renal perfusion with intravenous fluids. The key distinguishing feature is that this is a functional, rapidly reversible form of AKI without structural tubular damage.
- **The Case of "Acute Tubular Necrosis" (Intrinsic):** A 70-year-old man is in the ICU for septic shock secondary to community-acquired pneumonia. He required high-dose vasopressors for 72 hours to maintain a mean arterial pressure above 65 mmHg. His baseline creatinine was 0.9 mg/dL. On day 3 of admission, his creatinine begins to rise to 2.1 mg/dL, and his urine output drops to 20 mL/hour despite adequate central venous pressure. He recently started piperacillin-tazobactam and vancomycin. Key features: a major ischemic or nephrotoxic insult (sepsis, shock, nephrotoxic antibiotics) followed by a progressive rise in creatinine and oliguria over days. On urinalysis, muddy brown granular casts (pathognomonic) are seen. The condition does not reverse immediately with fluid boluses and has a prolonged course (1-3 weeks) with a high risk of mortality.
- **The Case of "Postrenal AKI" (Obstructive):** An 72-year-old man with benign prostatic hyperplasia presents with 24 hours of anuria. He reports severe lower abdominal discomfort and a sensation of incomplete bladder emptying. On examination, a large, firm mass is palpable arising from the pelvis, which is dull to percussion (distended bladder). Key features: sudden anuria, a history of prostatic disease, and a palpable distended bladder on examination. A bladder scan or ultrasound confirms a post-void residual volume >1000 mL with bilateral hydronephrosis. Relief of the obstruction (Foley catheter placement) results in post-obstructive diuresis and rapid improvement in kidney function.

## Fourth: Pathogenesis and Pathophysiology

AKI is a multifaceted syndrome with complex pathogenesis that varies by etiology. The key mechanisms are categorized into prerenal, intrinsic renal, and postrenal pathways.

**1. Prerenal AKI (Functional Azotemia):**

The pathogenesis is a reduction in renal blood flow (RBF) and glomerular filtration pressure without intrinsic parenchymal damage.

- **Hypoperfusion:** True volume depletion (hemorrhage, GI losses) or effective circulating volume depletion (heart failure, cirrhosis) reduces renal perfusion pressure.
- **Autoregulation and Compensation:** Healthy kidneys maintain constant RBF and GFR across a mean arterial pressure (MAP) range of ~80–170 mmHg via myogenic and tubuloglomerular feedback. When MAP falls below this range, GFR drops sharply.[13]
- **Neurohormonal Response:** Baroreceptors activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and sympathetic nervous system (SNS), causing efferent arteriolar constriction (via angiotensin II) to maintain GFR and afferent arteriole vasodilation (via local prostaglandins). Drugs like NSAIDs (block prostaglandin synthesis) and ACE inhibitors/ARBs (block angiotensin II) impair these compensatory mechanisms, increasing susceptibility to prerenal AKI.[14]

**2. Intrinsic AKI (Structural Damage):**

The most common form is Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN), caused primarily by ischemia, sepsis, or nephrotoxins.

- **Ischemic ATN:** Prolonged or severe prerenal insult leads to cellular injury. The proximal tubule (S3 segment) and thick ascending limb are most vulnerable due to high metabolic demand.[15] Injury cascades include ATP depletion, causing Na/K-ATPase pump failure, cellular swelling, cytoskeletal disruption leading to loss of apical brush border, and detachment of tubular cells into the lumen.

### The Phases of ATN:

  - *Initiation Phase:* The ischemic/toxic insult begins; cellular injury occurs, but GFR is still falling. This phase lasts hours to days.
  - *Maintenance Phase:* GFR is at its nadir and stays low for 1–3 weeks. Oliguria is common. The pathogenesis is a triad of: **(a) Tubular obstruction** by casts composed of detached cells and Tamm-Horsfall protein; **(b) Backleak** of glomerular filtrate across the denuded, injured tubular epithelium into the interstitium and circulation; and **(c) Afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction** mediated by tubuloglomerular feedback (increased sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa from injured, non-reabsorbing tubules causes adenosine-mediated afferent vasoconstriction).[15]
  - *Recovery Phase:* Tubular cells regenerate, and function is gradually restored, often with a polyuric phase due to the return of GFR before tubular concentrating ability is fully restored.
- **Sepsis-Associated AKI:** The pathogenesis is distinct from pure ischemia. It involves a "microvascular orchestra" of events: afferent arteriolar vasodilation and efferent vasodilation, reducing GFR pressure without global hypoperfusion; inflammation (DAMPs and PAMPs) causing direct tubular injury; microcirculatory shunting and heterogeneous perfusion; and cell-cycle arrest as a protective mechanism to prevent cells with damaged DNA from dividing.[9]

**3. Postrenal AKI (Obstructive Nephropathy):**

Obstruction to urine flow (from the renal pelvis to the urethra) causes a rise in intratubular pressure.

- Initially, a protective vasodilation maintains RBF and GFR for a short period.
- Over hours to days, a sustained high intratubular pressure opposes glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, drastically reducing GFR. Prolonged obstruction leads to afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction and inflammatory interstitial fibrosis, causing permanent damage if not relieved.[16] The obstruction must involve both kidneys or a solitary kidney to cause AKI.

## Fifth: How to Diagnose AKI

### 5.1. From Presentation to Diagnosis: The Diagnostic Approach

AKI does not typically present as a primary complaint like chest pain. It is a syndrome detected on routine laboratory testing or through a change in urine output. The clinical task is pattern recognition: to detect AKI early (or identify those at high risk) and perform an analytical, hypothetico-deductive workup to determine the underlying cause.

### Step 1: Detect AKI and Confirm Severity

- The presentation is usually an **asymptomatic rise in serum creatinine** found on routine blood work, or a **drop in urine output** noted on the ward’s fluid balance chart.
- Apply the **KDIGO criteria** to confirm the diagnosis and stage severity (see Section 5.2).
- Immediately distinguish oliguric (<0.5 mL/kg/hr) from non-oliguric AKI, as oliguria carries a worse prognosis.

**Step 2: The Hypothetico-Deductive Diagnostic Framework**

Your initial differential should be broad but rapidly narrowed by a focused workup. Start with a main anatomical framework: **Prerenal, Intrinsic Renal, or Postrenal.**

### Dangerous Differential Diagnoses (Must Not Miss):

  - **Obstructive Uropathy (Postrenal):** Potentially reversible if caught early.
    - *Key Question:* "Any history of urinary stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia, pelvic cancer, or sudden anuria?"
    - *Key Sign:* Palpable distended bladder. Perform a post-void residual bladder scan immediately.
  - **Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis (Intrinsic):** Life-threatening, causes irreversible renal failure within days to weeks if not treated with immunosuppression.
    - *Key Questions:* "Have you coughed up blood (hemoptysis)? Have you noticed dark, tea-colored urine or a change in urine volume? Any recent sinusitis, rash, or joint pain?"
    - *Key Signs:* Hypertension, edema, purpura, or an active urinary sediment (dysmorphic red blood cells and red blood cell casts).
  - **Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (Postrenal/Prerenal):** Tensely distended abdomen, high peak airway pressures on ventilator, oliguria.
    - *Key Sign:* Elevated intra-abdominal pressure > 20 mmHg measured by bladder manometry.
  - **Acute Interstitial Nephritis (Intrinsic):** Drug-induced (e.g., beta-lactams, NSAIDs, PPIs), with a classic triad of fever, rash, and eosinophilia present in only a minority of cases.
    - *Key Question:* "What new medications were started 1-3 weeks ago?"
  - **Thrombotic Microangiopathy (Intrinsic):** TTP/HUS leading to multi-organ failure.
    - *Key Question:* "Any fever, neurological changes, or unexplained bruising/bleeding?"
    - *Key Signs:* Schistocytes on a peripheral blood smear, thrombocytopenia.

### Common Differential Diagnoses:

  - **Prerenal Azotemia:** Secondary to volume loss or poor cardiac output.
  - **Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN):** Secondary to prolonged prerenal insult, sepsis, or nephrotoxin.
  - **Contrast-Induced Nephropathy:** Rise in creatinine 24-72 hours after intravascular contrast administration.

### 5.2. History, Physical Examination, and Investigations

The diagnostic approach integrates history, physical exam, and investigations to answer a specific clinical question: "Why is this patient's kidney function acutely declining now?"

**History:**

A meticulous history is paramount and must explore:

- **Patient Baseline:** Establish true baseline creatinine. Ask about known kidney disease, diabetes, hypertension, and heart failure.
- **The "Renovascular" Timeline:** When was the last normal creatinine?
- **Fluid Status History:** Ask about orthostatic dizziness, thirst, weight changes, vomiting, diarrhea, diuretic use, or poor oral intake to assess for hypovolemia. For hypervolemia, ask about orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND), and leg swelling.
- **Urinary History:** Ask about oliguria (<400 mL/24 hrs) or anuria (<100 mL/24 hrs). Anuria is a classic red flag for complete obstruction. Enquire about colicky flank pain radiating to the groin (suggests stone) and symptoms of bladder outlet obstruction (hesitancy, poor stream, incomplete emptying).
- **Drug and Toxin History:** A complete review of medications is non-negotiable. Specifically ask about:
  - *Nephrotoxins:* NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, amphotericin B, acyclovir, cisplatin.
  - *Agents affecting renal hemodynamics:* ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, cyclosporine, tacrolimus.
  - *Culprits for Acute Interstitial Nephritis:* Beta-lactam antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), allopurinol, sulfonamides.
  - *Iodinated radiocontrast:* Timing and location of any recent CT scans or angiograms.
- **Systemic Disease History:** Ask for symptoms of systemic inflammation. A comprehensive review of systems (ROS) for rash, joint pain, sinusitis, hemoptysis, new cardiac murmurs, and fever is necessary to evaluate for vasculitis, endocarditis, and glomerulonephritis.

**Physical Examination:**

The physical exam focuses on volume status and signs of systemic disease.

### Vital Signs:

  - Orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia are critical signs of intravascular volume depletion and prerenal AKI.
  - Marked hypertension may suggest glomerulonephritis or thrombotic microangiopathy.

### Fluid Status Assessment:

  - *Hypovolemia:* Dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor, low jugular venous pressure (JVP), orthostatic hypotension.[17]
  - *Hypervolemia:* Elevated JVP, fine basilar inspiratory crackles (pulmonary edema), peripheral pitting edema, sacral edema, ascites, S3 gallop.
- **Abdominal Examination:** Palpate and percuss for a distended bladder (postrenal obstruction). Check for costovertebral angle tenderness (pyelonephritis, obstructing stone). Listen for an abdominal bruit (renal artery stenosis as a pre-existing risk factor).
- **Skin Examination:** Look for livedo reticularis or digital ischemia (atheroemboli), purpura (vasculitis), malar rash (lupus), or a maculopapular rash (acute interstitial nephritis).
- **Vascular Access and Muscle Examination:** Check for "track marks" at catheter access sites from recent angiography, which suggests cholesterol emboli syndrome. Examine muscles for tenderness or necrosis in rhabdomyolysis.

**Investigations:**

The systematic investigation answers the key questions: Is it prerenal, intrinsic, or postrenal? What is the specific intrinsic cause?

### Step 1: Core Lab Tests (Complete for Every Patient)

- **Serum Creatinine and eGFR:** The cornerstone for diagnosis and staging.[2] A rise of ≥0.3 mg/dL from baseline within 48 hours or a 1.5x rise from baseline within 7 days constitutes AKI.
- **Basic Metabolic Panel:** Assesses for life-threatening hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis (low serum bicarbonate), and hyponatremia. The BUN:Creatinine ratio helps differentiate:
  - *Ratio > 20:1:* Suggests prerenal AKI. Urea reabsorption is enhanced in the proximal tubule due to increased water reabsorption, while creatinine is not.
  - *Ratio < 10-15:1:* Suggests intrinsic renal injury (ATN), where tubular damage impairs urea reabsorption.
- **Complete Blood Count:** Looking for eosinophilia (acute interstitial nephritis), thrombocytopenia, and schistocytes (thrombotic microangiopathy).
- **Urinalysis with Microscopy:** This is the single most critical test for intrinsic renal disease. A pristine urine sediment is consistent with a prerenal or postrenal cause. The key findings are:
  - *Normal or hyaline casts:* Prerenal or postrenal AKI.
  - *Muddy brown granular casts and renal tubular epithelial (RTE) cells:* **Pathognomonic for ATN.** Sensitivity is moderate (~75%), but specificity is >95% in the right clinical context.[18]
  - *Dysmorphic red blood cells (RBCs) and RBC casts:* **Pathognomonic for glomerulonephritis.** Specificity approaches 100%.
  - *White blood cells (WBCs) and WBC casts:* Suggestive of acute interstitial nephritis or pyelonephritis.
  - *Urinary eosinophils:* Historically used for AIN, now known to have poor sensitivity and specificity and is not recommended as a definitive test.[19]
- **Urine Chemistry (Sodium, Creatinine):** Used to calculate the Fractional Excretion of Sodium (FENa), which helps differentiate prerenal from ATN in oliguric patients. FENa = (UNa × PCr) / (PNa × UCr) × 100.
  - *FENa < 1%:* Highly suggestive of prerenal AKI. The tubules are intact and avidly reabsorbing sodium.
  - *FENa > 2%:* Highly suggestive of ATN. Damaged tubules cannot reabsorb sodium efficiently.
  - *Pitfalls:* The FENa is unreliable in patients on diuretics (which raise UNa). In these patients, the Fractional Excretion of Urea (FEUrea) is preferred; a FEUrea < 35% suggests prerenal state.

### Step 2: Imaging

- **Renal and Bladder Ultrasound:** The gold-standard first-line imaging study to **rule out postrenal obstruction.** It has a sensitivity and specificity >95% for detecting hydronephrosis.[20] It also provides information on kidney size (small, shrunken kidneys suggest underlying CKD), cortical echogenicity, and calculi.

### Step 3: Additional Tests for Specific Diagnoses

- **Serological Workup for Glomerulonephritis:** If urinalysis shows RBC casts and dysmorphic RBCs, send: Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), Anti-double-stranded DNA (lupus nephritis), complement C3 and C4, Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA-MPO and PR3 for granulomatosis with polyangiitis and microscopic polyangiitis), anti-GBM antibody (Goodpasture's disease), and serum protein electrophoresis/free light chains (myeloma kidney).
- **Creatine Kinase (CK):** Markedly elevated in rhabdomyolysis. Urinalysis is positive for blood on a dipstick, but no RBCs are seen on microscopy (pigment-induced myoglobinuria).
- **Renal Biopsy:** The definitive **gold standard** for diagnosing intrinsic parenchymal diseases like glomerulonephritis, acute interstitial nephritis, and thrombotic microangiopathy when the cause remains unknown or specific treatment is required.[1] Indicated when non-invasive tests are inconclusive and a diagnosis will change management.

## Sixth: Disease Evolution

The progression of AKI is highly dependent on the etiology, severity, patient’s pre-existing health, and rapidity of management.

- **Prerenal AKI:** If the cause of hypoperfusion is promptly corrected (e.g., fluid resuscitation), prerenal AKI is rapidly reversible, usually within 24-48 hours, with complete recovery of renal function if it did not progress to ATN. If uncorrected, it invariably progresses to ischemic ATN.
- **Intrinsic AKI (ATN):** The clinical course follows the phases of initiation, maintenance, and recovery (see Section Four). The maintenance phase typically lasts 1-3 weeks. In the recovery phase, GFR begins to improve, often heralded by a polyuric phase. Most survivors (70–80%) recover some function, but complete restoration of GFR to baseline may not occur, particularly in those with severe ATN, advancing age, or pre-existing CKD.[21] If treated correctly, patients avoid life-threatening hyperkalemia and pulmonary edema, allowing time for tubular regeneration. However, even an episode of AKI that "resolves" to baseline creatinine is a major risk factor for future CKD, ESRD, and cardiovascular death.[22]
- **Postrenal AKI:** The outcome depends on the duration and completeness of the obstruction. Complete relief of obstruction within days usually leads to rapid improvement (post-obstructive diuresis). Obstruction lasting weeks or months may cause irreversible interstitial fibrosis and permanent renal function loss.
- **Overall Mortality:** AKI-associated in-hospital mortality ranges from 10% (non-ICU) to >50% (ICU patients with dialysis-requiring AKI). Death is rarely from kidney failure itself but from the underlying catastrophic illness (e.g., sepsis, cardiogenic shock) or associated complications (infection, arrhythmia from hyperkalemia).[3]

## Seventh: Complications of AKI

AKI causes a cascade of systemic effects, as the kidney's role in maintaining homeostasis fails. Sequelae can be acute and life-threatening or chronic.

- **Fluid Overload:** Volume retention leads to pulmonary edema, peripheral edema, and worsening cardiac function.

### Electrolyte Disturbances:

  - *Hyperkalemia:* Due to impaired renal excretion and transcellular shifts from acidosis. This is the most immediately fatal complication, causing cardiac arrhythmias and asystole. (See Red Flags).
  - *Hyponatremia:* Usually dilutional from free water excess.
  - *Hyperphosphatemia and Hypocalcemia:* Impaired phosphate excretion and reduced 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D production by the injured proximal tubule.
- **Metabolic Acidosis:** Accumulation of non-volatile acids (e.g., sulfate, phosphate, urate) leads to high anion-gap metabolic acidosis. This increases work of breathing (Kussmaul respirations), worsens hyperkalemia, and impairs cardiac contractility.
- **Uremic Syndrome:** Accumulation of nitrogenous waste products causes a spectrum of clinical toxicity: uremic pericarditis (a friction rub is an absolute indication for urgent dialysis), asterixis, encephalopathy (confusion to coma), platelet dysfunction (leading to bleeding), and anorexia/nausea.
- **Malnutrition:** A catabolic state due to the underlying illness, metabolic acidosis, and protein losses during dialysis (if applied).
- **Chronic Sequelae:** An episode of AKI directly incites maladaptive repair, leading to tubulointerstitial fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis, and vascular rarefaction. This transition dramatically increases the long-term risk of developing de novo CKD, progressing existing CKD, and developing end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).[22] Survivors also have a significantly elevated risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke.

## Eighth: Treatment (According to KDIGO Guidelines)

According to the KDIGO 2012 Clinical Practice Guideline for Acute Kidney Injury, management is generally supportive, aiming to eliminate the inciting cause, prevent further injury, and treat complications. There is no specific pharmacotherapy to reverse established ATN.[2]

### 1. General Management for All AKI Stages

- **Optimize Renal Perfusion:** This is the cornerstone.
  - In hypovolemia, administer isotonic crystalloids (0.9% normal saline or balanced crystalloids like Lactated Ringer's/Plasma-Lyte) to restore euvolemia. The KDIGO guidelines caution against the use of hydroxyethyl starches (HES) due to nephrotoxicity.[2]
  - In hypervolemic states (e.g., heart failure), provide inotropic/vasopressor support as needed.
- **Hemodynamic Support:** For patients with shock, target a MAP > 65 mmHg with vasopressors (norepinephrine is first-line) and fluid therapy.[23]
- **Stop All Nephrotoxins:** Discontinue or dose-adjust all potential nephrotoxic agents (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, ACE-I/ARBs in the setting of hyperkalemia or hypotension, etc.).
- **Dose-Adjust Renally Cleared Medications:** All medications must be reviewed and doses adjusted for the current level of GFR.
- **Nutritional Support:** Enteral nutrition is preferred, with a total energy intake of 25–35 kcal/kg/day and avoidance of protein restriction (aim for 0.8–1.2 g/kg/day in non-catabolic, non-dialysis patients).[2]

### 2. Specific Pharmacological Interventions

### Diuretics:

  - *Furosemide:* A loop diuretic. Mechanism: Inhibits the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter in the thick ascending limb, blocking reabsorption and increasing water and solute excretion. In AKI, it is NOT recommended for preventing or treating AKI, as it does not improve survival or renal recovery.[2] Its only indicated role is to manage symptomatic fluid overload (e.g., pulmonary edema) and avoid the need for dialysis in volume-sensitive patients.
    - Important side effects: Ototoxicity (with high doses or rapid IV push), hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis.
    - Duration: Use the minimum effective dose to achieve desired volume status.

### Dopamine:

  - Low-dose ("renal-dose") dopamine is **specifically NOT recommended** for the prevention or treatment of AKI due to lack of efficacy and risk of tachyarrhythmias and cardiac ischemia.[2]

### 3. Management of Complications

- **Hyperkalemia (K+ > 6.5 mmol/L or ECG changes):** This is a medical emergency.
  - *Calcium Gluconate or Calcium Chloride (IV):* Stabilizes the cardiac myocyte membrane against the depolarizing effect of potassium within 1-3 minutes. Does not lower serum potassium. Mechanism: Antagonizes potassium's effect on resting membrane potential. This is the first and most critical step if ECG changes are present.
  - *Insulin (Regular) + Dextrose (IV):* Shifts potassium intracellularly via activation of the Na+/K+-ATPase pump. Onset in 10-20 minutes, effect lasts 2-4 hours. Give 10 units of regular insulin IV with 50 mL of 50% dextrose (D50W).
  - *Beta-agonists (Inhaled Salbutamol):* Shifts potassium intracellularly. Effect is additive to insulin. Onset 30 minutes.
  - *Sodium Bicarbonate (IV):* Shifts potassium into cells by raising systemic pH. Less reliable and not a first-line sole agent but useful if severe metabolic acidosis is also present.
  - *Potassium Binders (e.g., Patiromer, Sodium Zirconium Cyclosilicate):* Newer gastrointestinal binders that exchange sodium or calcium for potassium in the GI tract, eliminating it from the body. Onset is 1-2 hours, not suitable for acute life-saving management. Chronic old binders like Sodium Polystyrene Sulfonate have a very slow onset and risk of colonic necrosis.
  - *Hemodialysis:* The definitive and most rapid method to remove potassium from the body. Indicated if medical management fails or in severe cases with renal failure.
- **Metabolic Acidosis:** Sodium bicarbonate therapy is recommended only if the serum bicarbonate is <15 mmol/L and there is evidence of cardiovascular compromise. Aggressive bicarbonate infusion can cause hypernatremia, hypervolemia, and a paradoxical intracellular acidosis.[2]

**4. Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)**

Dialysis is the mainstay of treatment for the most severe AKI. The absolute ("A-E-I-O-U") indications for urgent RRT are:[2]

- **A**cidosis: Severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.15) refractory to medical therapy.
- **E**lectrolytes: Severe hyperkalemia (>6.5 mmol/L) refractory to medical management.
- **I**ntoxication: Overdose with a dialyzable toxin (e.g., lithium, methanol, salicylates).
- **O**verload: Fluid overload (pulmonary edema) refractory to diuretics.
- **U**remia: Uremic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding).
- KDIGO guidelines do not mandate a specific modality (continuous RRT (CRRT) vs. intermittent hemodialysis (IHD)), but CRRT is often preferred in hemodynamically unstable patients. The timing of initiation in the absence of absolute indications has been studied in major trials (STARRT-AKI, AKIKI), which showed no benefit to an "accelerated" strategy over a "watchful waiting" strategy in terms of overall survival.[24,25]

## Ninth: Red Flags

These are the matters you must recognize and act on immediately to prevent death or irreversible damage.

- **Hyperkalemic ECG Changes:** Any patient with AKI and a K+ > 6.0 mmol/L must have an immediate ECG. Peaked T waves, loss of P waves, widening of the QRS complex (a sine-wave pattern is a pre-terminal rhythm) demand **immediate IV calcium** to stabilize the myocardium, followed by all other potassium-lowering therapies. This is a seconds-to-minutes emergency.
- **Sudden Anuria:** Acute, complete cessation of urine output is not ATN. It is a **postrenal obstruction until proven otherwise.** A palpable bladder confirms a lower urinary tract obstruction, and an emergent ultrasound is required to look for bilateral ureteric obstruction.
- **Pulmonary Edema and Hypoxia:** Rapid fluid accumulation causing respiratory failure in an anuric patient is a life-threatening condition. If diuretics are ineffective, urgent intubation and emergent hemodialysis for volume removal are necessary.
- **Uremic Pericarditis:** A pericardial friction rub on auscultation in a patient with AKI is a specific and ominous sign of severe uremia. It is an absolute indication for urgent hemodialysis, as it can rapidly progress to cardiac tamponade or hemorrhagic pericarditis.[26]

### Systemic Symptoms with AKI (The "Don't-Miss" Intrinsic Causes):

  - *Hemoptysis + AKI:* Goodpasture's syndrome or ANCA-associated vasculitis (pulmonary-renal syndrome) is a clinical diagnosis requiring immediate serological testing and often emergency plasmapheresis and immunosuppression. Mortality without treatment is near 100%.
  - *Rash, Fever, Eosinophilia, AKI + Recent New Drug:* Suggests Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN). The red flag is a new offending drug started 1-3 weeks prior. Discontinuation of the drug is the critical and most effective early intervention; failure to do so leads to irreversible interstitial fibrosis.
  - *Livedo Reticularis + AKI + Eosinophilia after Vascular Intervention:* Suggests Cholesterol Emboli Syndrome. This has a poor prognosis and no specific treatment, but it is a red flag because continued anticoagulation (which is often the instinct after a vascular procedure) can worsen embolization.
- **Time Window for Postrenal Obstruction:** Relief of a complete urinary obstruction within days offers a good chance of functional recovery. Obstruction lasting more than several weeks causes permanent, irreversible renal parenchymal damage due to progressive fibrosis.
