## First: Definition and overview

**Definition** [G]

Chronic coronary syndromes are the range of clinical presentations arising from structural and/or functional alterations of the coronary arteries and/or coronary microcirculation, producing transient, reversible myocardial oxygen supply–demand mismatch [1]. This 2024 ESC definition deliberately widens the older "stable angina from a fixed epicardial stenosis" concept to include microvascular and vasospastic disease as equal members of the same syndrome family, not footnotes to it.

**Nosological position** [E]

IHD/CAD encompasses: **chronic coronary syndromes** (this document), **acute coronary syndromes** (unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI — covered separately), and their shared downstream consequence, **ischaemic cardiomyopathy**, when repeated or extensive infarction impairs LV function. All share one substrate — atherosclerotic or functional coronary disease — but diverge sharply in urgency and management, which is why the 2019/2024 ESC terminology reframes them as points on one continuum rather than separate diseases.

**Synonyms** — coronary artery disease (CAD), coronary heart disease (CHD), stable angina (older term), chronic coronary disease (CCD, 2023 AHA/ACC term) [1].

## Second: Epidemiological snapshot

**Clinical significance and severity** [E]

IHD is the leading cause of death and disability worldwide and the leading cardiovascular contributor to global DALYs [7,11].

**Incidence and prevalence** [E]

- According to Global Burden of Disease 2021, IHD remains the leading cause of death and illness worldwide, with 8.99 million deaths reported in 2021 [7].
- IHD resulted in 8.99 million deaths and 188.3 million DALYs in 2021, the leading cause of health loss among non-communicable diseases [10].
- Globally IHD affects around 126 million individuals (1,655 per 100,000), approximately 1.72% of the world's population, with prevalence projected to exceed 1,845 per 100,000 by 2030 [9] — this estimate is from GBD data through 2017; treat the trend direction as more reliable than the exact figure at this distance. [L]
- Ischaemic heart disease was among the leading cardiovascular causes of DALYs globally in 2023, alongside intracerebral haemorrhage, ischaemic stroke, and hypertensive heart disease [11].

**Peak age of onset** [E] — incidence typically starts in the fourth decade and increases with age [9].

**Sex distribution** [E] — men are more commonly affected than women [9], though the gap narrows and reverses in relative risk after menopause; a reliable pooled sex ratio across all ages was not retrieved. [L]

**Geographic burden** [E]

Eastern European countries sustain the highest prevalence [9]. Regional mortality trends show pronounced declines in Western and Eastern Europe, moderate declines in Latin America and East Asia, minimal change in Sub-Saharan Africa, and a slight increase in South Asia [8]. The variation tracks access to interventional cardiology and preventive programmes, not biology alone.

**Mortality and disability contribution** [E] — as above: leading global cause of death, and leading non-communicable cause of DALYs [7,10]. Age-standardised mortality has generally declined globally over 2000–2021 [8], so absolute case burden is rising even as age-adjusted risk per person falls — an ageing-population effect, not a failure of prevention.

**Screening** [G]

There is no population-wide screening test for CAD in asymptomatic low-risk adults (per Part 3 of this project's cardiology review). What the 2024 ESC guideline does support is **opportunistic risk-factor screening**: use of SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, and incidental coronary artery calcium data from prior imaging performed for other indications, to refine cardiovascular risk classification — carotid ultrasound as an alternative when CAC is unavailable [6]. This is risk stratification, not disease screening, and is aimed at primary prevention rather than early CAD detection.

**Risk factors, each with the mechanistic reason** [E] unless noted

- **Hypertension** — chronic endothelial shear stress and mechanical injury promote lipid entry into the subendothelial space and accelerate plaque formation.
- **Dyslipidaemia (elevated LDL-C, Lp(a))** — LDL particles infiltrate the arterial intima, undergo oxidative modification, and are engulfed by macrophages to form foam cells — the initiating lesion of atherosclerosis.
- **Diabetes mellitus / insulin resistance** — hyperglycaemia promotes advanced glycation end-product formation, endothelial dysfunction, and a pro-thrombotic, pro-inflammatory milieu; also blunts anginal warning symptoms via autonomic neuropathy (Part 1, item 2).
- **Smoking** — direct endothelial toxicity, increased platelet aggregability, carbon monoxide-mediated hypoxaemia, and accelerated LDL oxidation.
- **Obesity / metabolic syndrome** — visceral adiposity drives a chronic low-grade inflammatory state and insulin resistance that compound the other metabolic risk factors.
- **Family history of premature CAD** — reflects polygenic risk (including lipoprotein(a) and monogenic causes such as familial hypercholesterolaemia) plus shared environment.
- **Chronic kidney disease** — accelerated vascular calcification, uraemic toxin-mediated endothelial injury, and a disproportionately high burden of traditional risk factors.
- **Age and male sex** — cumulative exposure duration to the above; oestrogen's relative vascular protection is lost after menopause, narrowing the sex gap with age.
- **Sedentary lifestyle** — reduces endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability and worsens the entire metabolic risk cluster.

## Third: Pattern recognition — the archetypal case

### The vignette to store

A **62-year-old man**, hypertensive, a smoker of 30 years, presents with **exertional retrosternal pressure** occurring reproducibly after climbing two flights of stairs or walking briskly for 200 metres, relieved within 2–3 minutes of rest, present for the past four months and unchanged in threshold. He denies pain at rest. On examination, blood pressure is 152/88 mmHg, heart sounds are normal, no murmur, peripheral pulses intact, no oedema. Resting ECG is normal. An exercise ECG or CT coronary angiogram (per local pathway) demonstrates a flow-limiting stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending artery.

**Discriminating features** [E]

- **Reproducibility at a fixed exertional threshold**, relieved by rest within minutes — this is the single feature separating chronic coronary syndrome pain from the crescendo, unpredictable pattern of ACS.
- **Absence of autonomic accompaniments** (profuse cold sweating, vomiting) that mark an acute plaque event.
- **A stable symptom trajectory over months**, not days.
- **Versus musculoskeletal chest pain:** reproducible on palpation of the chest wall (Tietze/costochondritis) — CCS pain is not reproduced by palpation.
- **Versus gastro-oesophageal reflux:** related to meals or posture rather than exertion, though overlap exists and both may coexist.
- **Versus anxiety/panic:** typically longer-lasting, associated with hyperventilation and paraesthesia, and not reliably exertion-linked.

**Pathognomonic features** [E]

**None exist.** No symptom pattern, sign, or single test is pathognomonic for CCS; the diagnosis rests on the combination of a compatible history, a validated pre-test probability estimate, and objective evidence of ischaemia or anatomical stenosis. Transient ST depression developing during exercise **and resolving with symptom resolution** is highly suggestive but not unique to CAD (it also occurs in severe LVH, cardiomyopathy, and as a false positive in women with a low pre-test probability).

## Fourth: Pathogenesis and pathophysiology

**Template subsections not applicable, per Rule B4:** IHD is not infectious and has no organism, incubation period, or transmission route; it is not neoplastic and has no histological subtyping or staging system in the oncological sense.

**Mechanistic sequence** [E]

- **Endothelial injury and dysfunction** — from shear stress (hypertension), hyperglycaemia, smoking toxins, or dyslipidaemia — reduces nitric-oxide-mediated vasodilatation and increases endothelial permeability and adhesion molecule expression (VCAM-1, ICAM-1).
- **Lipid infiltration and retention** — LDL particles cross the dysfunctional endothelium into the subendothelial space, where they are oxidised.
- **Monocyte recruitment and foam cell formation** — monocytes adhere via the upregulated adhesion molecules, migrate into the intima, differentiate into macrophages, and engulf oxidised LDL to become foam cells — the fatty streak, the earliest visible lesion.
- **Smooth muscle migration and fibrous cap formation** — smooth muscle cells migrate from the media into the intima and secrete collagen, forming a fibrous cap over a lipid-rich necrotic core — the mature atherosclerotic plaque.
- **Two divergent plaque phenotypes**, and which one predominates determines clinical behaviour:
  - **Stable plaque** — thick fibrous cap, smaller lipid core, heavy calcification → progressive luminal narrowing → the exertional, reproducible symptom pattern of chronic coronary syndrome.
  - **Vulnerable ("unstable") plaque** — thin fibrous cap, large lipid core, active inflammatory infiltrate, prone to **rupture or erosion** → acute thrombus formation → acute coronary syndrome. Plaque vulnerability, not luminal severity alone, determines acute risk — a haemodynamically "insignificant" plaque can rupture and occlude a vessel.
- **Ischaemia physiology** — beyond a critical stenosis (classically >70% luminal diameter for flow-limiting epicardial disease, or any degree in microvascular/vasospastic disease), myocardial oxygen demand during exertion outstrips supply → subendocardial ischaemia first (Part 1, item 1) → anginal symptoms and/or ECG changes.
- **Collateral circulation** — chronic, repeated sub-threshold ischaemia stimulates angiogenic collateral vessel development, which can substantially delay symptom onset relative to angiographic severity — part of why anatomical disease often outpaces the clinical picture, as in Takayasu arteritis (Part Fourth, earlier in this project) though via a different primary mechanism.
- **Microvascular and vasospastic contributions** — the 2024 ESC reframing formally incorporates **ANOCA/INOCA** (angina/ischaemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries) as a first-class CCS phenotype [1,4], reflecting coronary microvascular dysfunction (impaired vasodilator reserve at the arteriolar level) or epicardial vasospasm (endothelium-dependent), either of which can produce genuine ischaemia with an angiographically "normal" epicardial tree.

## Fifth: The diagnostic pathway

### 1. Mode of presentation

- **With exertional symptoms** — the classical route: chest discomfort, dyspnoea, or fatigue on exertion.
- **Incidentally** — coronary artery calcification found on imaging performed for another indication, or an asymptomatic positive stress test in a high-risk individual undergoing pre-operative assessment.
- **As anginal equivalents** without chest pain — particularly in diabetes, the elderly, women, and CKD (Part 1, item 2).
- **Silently** — objectively documented ischaemia with no symptom at all (Part 1, item 3); the first presentation may then be an MI, arrhythmia, or heart failure.

### 2. Symptoms, ranked qualitatively with mechanism[E]

- **Exertional angina** (most characteristic) — retrosternal pressure/tightness/heaviness, reproducible threshold, relieved by rest/nitrates within minutes. Mechanism: supply–demand mismatch across a flow-limiting stenosis or vasospasm.
- **Anginal equivalents** — exertional dyspnoea, fatigue, epigastric discomfort, nausea (Part 1, item 2).
- **Palpitations** — from ischaemia-provoked ectopy or arrhythmia.
- **Presyncope/syncope** — uncommon in stable CCS; when present, raises concern for an ischaemia-triggered arrhythmia and warrants urgent evaluation.

A reliable population-based percentage ranking of these symptoms in CCS specifically was **not retrieved** and is not stated. [?]

### 3. Signs[E]

Often none between episodes. During an anginal episode: transient S4, transient mitral regurgitation murmur from papillary muscle ischaemic dysfunction, diaphoresis. Chronically: signs of the underlying risk factor burden (hypertensive retinopathy, xanthelasma/tendon xanthomata in dyslipidaemia, peripheral arterial disease signs reflecting shared atherosclerotic burden) rather than signs of the coronary disease itself.

### 4. Differential diagnosis — two separate lists

::: **A. Dangerous differentials — exclude first**

- **Acute coronary syndrome** — new, prolonged, rest, or crescendo pattern; the single most important distinction, since management diverges completely.
- **Aortic dissection** — abrupt maximal-onset tearing pain, migratory.
- **Pulmonary embolism** — pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, hypoxaemia with a clear chest film.
- **Tension pneumothorax** — sudden dyspnoea with unilateral absent breath sounds.
- **Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave)** — severe pain after vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema.
- :::

**B. Common differentials — ranked by prevalence** [E]

- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.
- Musculoskeletal chest wall pain (costochondritis).
- Anxiety/panic disorder.
- Biliary colic (can mimic inferior ischaemic pain via shared referral pathways).
- Pericarditis (positional, pleuritic, ECG changes as in Part 1, item 5).

### 5. Discriminating question and sign, per differential

| **Differential** | **Ask** | **Sign** |
|---|---|---|
| ACS | Is the pain new, at rest, or escalating despite the same activity level? | Dynamic ECG changes, rising troponin |
| Aortic dissection | Was the pain instantaneous at maximal intensity, and did it migrate? | Inter-arm BP difference, new AR murmur |
| PE | Any recent immobility, leg swelling, haemoptysis? | Hypoxaemia with a clear chest X-ray |
| GORD | Related to meals, worse lying flat, relieved by antacids? | No relationship to exertion |
| Costochondritis | Reproduced by pressing the chest wall? | Focal tenderness reproducing the pain exactly |
| Anxiety | Associated with hyperventilation, paraesthesia, a situational trigger? | Normal exertional tolerance for other activities |

### 6. Investigations — what, in sequence, and why[G]

**Step 1 — clinical likelihood assessment.** The 2024 ESC guideline's first of three formal steps: careful characterisation of symptoms and calculation of pre-test probability of obstructive CAD using age, sex, and symptom character, refined further by incorporating cardiovascular risk factors [3,6]. [G]

**Step 2 — resting ECG and basic cardiac assessment**, including consideration of echocardiography to assess LV function and exclude structural alternative explanations [3].

**Step 3 — diagnostic testing, chosen by pre-test probability** [1,3]:

- **CT coronary angiography (CCTA)** — first-line in low-to-moderate risk patients to diagnose or exclude obstructive CAD, per the 2024 ESC guideline [6]. [G] Excellent negative predictive value; excludes disease reliably in this population.
- **Functional (stress) imaging** — stress echocardiography, SPECT, stress CMR, or PET — used to correlate symptoms with actual ischaemia, assess viability, and guide treatment, particularly where CCTA shows intermediate anatomical stenosis or is non-diagnostic [3,6].
- **Invasive coronary angiography (ICA) with functional assessment (FFR/iFR)** — reserved for those with an uncertain diagnosis after non-invasive testing, or high clinical risk, to confirm or exclude obstructive CAD or ANOCA/INOCA [3]. [G] The guideline explicitly aims to **minimise unnecessary ICA**, reversing the older reflex of "when in doubt, angiogram."
- **CT-based FFR** — for a known intermediate stenosis in a proximal or mid coronary segment on CCTA, to determine physiological significance without an invasive procedure [3].
- **Troponin** — measured where instability is suspected clinically; using high-sensitivity assays, low but detectable troponin levels are common even in stable angina and are prognostic, but do **not** yet have a diagnostic role for obstructive CAD [3]. [L] If instability is suspected, management shifts to the ACS pathway [3,12].
- **NT-proBNP** — to confirm or exclude coexisting heart failure, not to diagnose CAD itself [3].

### 7. Gold standard for definitive diagnosis

There is no single universal gold standard across the whole CCS spectrum. For **obstructive epicardial CAD**, invasive coronary angiography with physiological assessment (FFR/iFR) is the definitive anatomical-and-functional reference. For **ANOCA/INOCA**, the reference standard is invasive coronary function testing (acetylcholine provocation for vasospasm, adenosine-based indices for microvascular resistance) at the time of angiography — a test that is frequently omitted, which is why microvascular disease is systematically under-diagnosed. [E]

### 8. Diagnostic algorithm

- Characterise symptoms and calculate pre-test probability (age, sex, symptom pattern, risk factors).
- Resting ECG; consider echocardiography.
- If instability is suspected clinically at any point, **divert immediately to the ACS pathway** — troponin, serial ECG, urgent risk stratification.
- For stable presentations: CCTA in low-to-moderate risk; functional imaging where CCTA is equivocal or risk is higher; ICA with functional assessment where non-invasive testing is inconclusive or risk is high.
- If epicardial disease is excluded but ischaemia is objectively demonstrated, evaluate for ANOCA/INOCA with invasive coronary function testing.
- Risk-stratify (clinical factors, LVEF, ischaemia burden on functional testing) to guide the intensity of secondary prevention and the threshold for revascularisation.

## Sixth: Natural history

**Untreated/undertreated trajectory** [E] — slow, progressive luminal narrowing with episodic plaque instability; the risk is not linear with anatomical severity, because an acute event can arise from a previously non-flow-limiting but vulnerable plaque. Over years, repeated ischaemic insults or a major infarction can produce ischaemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure.

**Treated trajectory** [E] — with risk-factor control and guideline-directed therapy, event rates fall substantially; revascularisation relieves symptoms and, in specific high-risk anatomical or functional subsets, improves survival, though for most stable patients optimal medical therapy is non-inferior to routine revascularisation for hard outcomes (a nuanced, trial-dependent point requiring the primary literature for specific comparisons, which is beyond this compact template entry). [L] for the general claim without a specific trial citation.

## Seventh: Complications and sequelae

**Of the disease** [E] — acute coronary syndrome (plaque rupture/erosion); ischaemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure; ventricular arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death from scar-related re-entry; ischaemic mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle dysfunction; silent progression to critical multivessel disease.

**Of treatment** [E] — antiplatelet/anticoagulant bleeding; statin myopathy and rarely hepatotoxicity; beta-blocker bradycardia and bronchospasm in reactive airway disease; revascularisation-specific risks (restenosis, stent thrombosis for PCI; stroke, bleeding, and graft failure for CABG).

## Eighth: Treatment

::: **According to the 2024 ESC guideline for the management of chronic coronary syndromes** [1,3,6] [G]

### Pharmacological — anti-anginal therapy

- **Beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, long-acting nitrates** — first-line anti-anginal agents.
- **Second-line agents (nicorandil, ranolazine, ivabradine, trimetazidine)** — the 2024 guideline **removed the strict second-line positioning** these drugs previously held after beta-blockers/CCBs/nitrates, because there are no studies supporting the superiority of any one anti-anginal over another [4]. [G] This is a genuine practice shift worth flagging: choice among agents is now individualised (heart rate, blood pressure, comorbidity, tolerability) rather than strictly sequential.

**Event-prevention therapy** — antiplatelet therapy (typically aspirin monotherapy in stable disease), high-intensity statin to target per the current lipid guideline, and blood pressure control per the current hypertension framework (Part 3 of this project's cardiology review covers the ESC/AHA divergence on thresholds).

**Revascularisation** — selected by anatomical and functional findings from large randomised trials and individual patient data meta-analyses, per the guideline; indications include persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, high-risk anatomy (e.g. left main, proximal LAD, multivessel disease with reduced LVEF), or a large ischaemic burden on functional testing [3].

**Lifestyle and adherence** — the guideline places explicit emphasis on strategies to improve adherence to both medical therapy and prescribed lifestyle change, alongside a structured informed discussion of individual risk and treatment benefit [3,6].

**ANOCA/INOCA-specific management** — an expanded section in the 2024 guideline separately addresses diagnosis and treatment of angina/ischaemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries, and refractory/recurrent angina management strategies [3]. ## Ninth: Red flags

| **Finding** | **What it means** | **Action** |
|---|---|---|
| Pain at rest, new, or crescendo pattern | Possible ACS | Immediate ECG and troponin; ACS pathway |
| Chest pain with syncope or near-syncope | Ischaemia-triggered arrhythmia | Urgent rhythm assessment |
| Angina with new heart failure signs | Ischaemic LV dysfunction | Urgent echocardiography |
| Chest pain with a pulse or BP asymmetry | Aortic dissection until excluded | Do not treat as routine angina |
| Refractory angina despite optimal therapy | High-risk anatomy or ANOCA/INOCA needing invasive characterisation | Specialist referral, not simply more anti-anginal drugs |

### Drug contraindications capable of harm[E]

- **Beta-blockers in severe reactive airway disease or high-grade AV block without a pacemaker.**
- **Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers combined with beta-blockers** — additive risk of severe bradycardia/heart block.
- **Nitrates with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors** — severe, potentially fatal hypotension.
- **Abrupt beta-blocker withdrawal** in a patient with significant CAD — risk of rebound tachycardia and ischaemia.

### Time-critical windows

Once instability is suspected, this is no longer a chronic coronary syndrome — revert immediately to ACS timing (STEMI reperfusion windows defined by the current ACS guideline [G] by reference, already covered in Part 4 of this project's cardiology review).

::: **Rule B11 — escalation boundary**

Two judgements here should not be made from this document alone: distinguishing a genuine new instability from a benign fluctuation in a known stable anginal pattern, and the choice between medical therapy alone versus revascularisation in any individual patient with intermediate-risk findings — both require the full guideline, the actual test results, and a senior or cardiology decision.
