## Part One: The Emergency Cardiac Drugs (Resuscitation and Acute Stabilization)

These are the drugs you push, infuse, or administer during a code or an acute cardiovascular emergency. They act within seconds to minutes. You must know their mechanism, dose, administration, and the exact monitoring parameter.

## 1. Adrenaline (Epinephrine)

**Mechanism of Action:**

A direct-acting, non-selective adrenergic agonist. It acts on alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 receptors. At low doses, beta-1 and beta-2 effects predominate (increased cardiac contractility and heart rate, and bronchodilation with vasodilation in skeletal muscle). At high doses (e.g., cardiac arrest dosing), the alpha-1 effect is dominant, causing profound systemic vasoconstriction. In cardiac arrest, the alpha-1-mediated vasoconstriction is the critical effect: it squeezes the arterial circulation, increasing the aortic diastolic pressure, which is the primary driver of coronary perfusion pressure (CPP). Achieving a CPP >15 mmHg is essential for return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).[1]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Cardiac Arrest (VF/pVT, Asystole, PEA):** 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 minutes. This is a universal, non-negotiable ACLS drug for all rhythms. It is given as early as possible in non-shockable rhythms, and after the second shock in shockable rhythms.[1]
- **Anaphylactic Shock with Cardiovascular Collapse:** 0.5 mg IM (0.5 mL of 1:1000 solution) into the anterolateral thigh. If profound shock is present, a slow IV bolus of 50–100 mcg (0.5–1 mL of 1:10,000 solution) can be given by a specialist under cardiac monitoring. The alpha-1 effect reverses vasodilation and laryngeal edema. The beta-2 effect causes bronchodilation and stabilizes mast cells.
- **Post-Cardiac Arrest Hypotension:** An infusion at 2–10 mcg/min titrated to a target MAP ≥65 mmHg.

**Contraindications:**

There are no absolute contraindications in a cardiac arrest or peri-arrest situation. Relative contraindications in non-arrest situations include active labor (relaxes the uterus) and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- **Cardiac Arrest Bolus:** The pre-filled syringe is 1 mg in 10 mL (1:10,000). Give it as a rapid IV bolus, followed immediately by a 20 mL normal saline flush to push the drug into the central circulation. Elevating the arm helps.
- **Infusion:** 1 mg of adrenaline in 100 mL of 0.9% saline or D5W. This is a standard 10 mcg/mL concentration. Run it through a dedicated, central intravenous line. The dose is titrated in mcg/min on a syringe driver or infusion pump.

### Monitoring and Key Points of Attention:

- **During a Code:** The effect is on the arterial waveform. After the dose, you will see the ETCO2 rise (indicating increased cardiac output from the vasoconstriction and improved coronary perfusion) and a rise in the diastolic blood pressure on the arterial line. This is the sign the drug is working.
- **Extravasation Injury:** Adrenaline is a potent vasoconstrictor. If the IV line tissues, the drug causes severe, local ischemic necrosis. Always ensure the IV access is patent and flush freely before and after administration. The phentolamine infiltration protocol (5–10 mg phentolamine in 10 mL of saline injected into the extravasated area) is the specific antidote if this occurs.
- **Post-ROSC:** An adrenaline infusion causes tachycardia, myocardial oxygen consumption, and a drop in serum potassium (beta-2-mediated shift). Monitor the ECG and serum potassium.

## 2. Amiodarone

**Mechanism of Action:**

A class III antiarrhythmic agent with multiple class effects (classes I, II, III, and IV). Its primary antiarrhythmic mechanism in the acute, emergency setting is a non-competitive block of alpha and beta adrenergic receptors and a blockade of the inward sodium and calcium currents, which slows conduction and prolongs the refractory period in all cardiac tissue. Its long-term, class III effect is the blockade of the outward potassium rectifier channels (IKr and IKs), which prolongs the action potential duration and the QT interval.[1]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia/Ventricular Fibrillation (pVT/VF) Refractory to Defibrillation:** 300 mg IV bolus after the third shock. If persistent, a second dose of 150 mg IV bolus can be given. This is the first-line antiarrhythmic in the 2025 AHA ACLS algorithm.[1]
- **Stable, Monomorphic Ventricular Tachycardia:** A loading dose of 150 mg IV over 10 minutes, followed by an infusion.
- **Atrial Fibrillation with Rapid Ventricular Response (Rate Control):** For hemodynamically stable patients when beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers are contraindicated (e.g., borderline blood pressure, severe LV dysfunction). It is not a first-line agent for rhythm control in AF in the acute setting.
- **Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) Refractory to Adenosine and Vagal Maneuvers.**

### Contraindications:

- **Known severe iodine allergy** (amiodarone is 37% iodine by weight).
- **Cardiogenic shock** with a sinus rhythm (it is a negative inotrope and vasodilator).
- **Severe sinus node dysfunction or high-grade AV block** without a functioning pacemaker.
- **Prolonged QT interval (QTc >500 ms)** in a non-code situation.

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- **Cardiac Arrest Bolus:** The 300 mg (6 mL) is drawn up from a glass ampoule and administered undiluted as a rapid IV bolus. Do not mix it with other drugs in the same syringe.
- **Stable Tachycardia Infusion:** A 150 mg loading dose is diluted in 100 mL of D5W (dextrose 5% in water) and infused over 10 minutes. Do not use normal saline; amiodarone is incompatible with saline and precipitates. This is a critical pharmacy point.
- **Maintenance Infusion:** After the bolus, a continuous infusion of 1 mg/min is run for 6 hours (360 mg in 360 mL of D5W at 60 mL/hr), followed by 0.5 mg/min for 18 hours. The drug must be administered through a central venous catheter if the infusion will last >24 hours, as it is a profound venous sclerosant and causes severe, irreversible chemical phlebitis in a peripheral vein.

### Monitoring and Key Points of Attention:

- **Hypotension:** The IV bolus in a code will cause profound vasodilation and hypotension immediately after administration, which is one reason it is given after the third shock and after adrenaline. In a patient with a blood pressure, infuse it slowly over 10 minutes.
- **QT Prolongation:** The ECG QT interval will prolong. A QTc >500 ms is a red flag for torsades de pointes, and the amiodarone must be stopped or the dose reduced.
- **Drug Interactions:** Amiodarone is a potent inhibitor of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein. It will significantly increase the serum concentration of warfarin (up to doubling the INR), digoxin, and rivaroxaban. The warfarin dose must be empirically reduced by 30–50% when amiodarone is started, and the INR must be checked within 3–5 days.
- **Long-Term Toxicity:** Pulmonary fibrosis, thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism or thyrotoxicosis due to the massive iodine load), slate-gray skin discoloration, corneal microdeposits, and hepatotoxicity. These are for chronic use, but you must be aware of them when continuing the drug beyond the acute event.

## 3. Adenosine

**Mechanism of Action:**

An endogenous purine nucleoside. It binds to A1 adenosine receptors on the surface of atrial and AV nodal myocytes. This receptor activates an inhibitory G-protein, which opens the inward, rectifying potassium channel (IKAdo), causing a potassium efflux and a rapid hyperpolarization of the cell membrane. This transiently suppresses the calcium current and slows or completely blocks AV nodal conduction. The half-life is 5–10 seconds.[2]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Acute Termination of Paroxysmal Supraventricular Tachycardia (PSVT, including AVNRT and AVRT):** The first-line diagnostic and therapeutic agent. It will terminate 90% of AV nodal-dependent re-entrant tachycardias.
- **Diagnostic Use in Wide-Complex Tachycardia of Unknown Origin:** It will transiently block the AV node. If the rhythm is atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter with a bypass tract (WPW), the underlying pre-excited rhythm is unmasked on the ECG during the AV block, providing critical diagnostic information.

### Contraindications:

- **Active, Severe Bronchospasm or Asthma:** The A2B and A3 adenosine receptors on bronchial mast cells can be activated, causing an acute, severe, and prolonged bronchospasm in asthmatics. This is a relative but very strong contraindication.
- **Heart Transplant Recipients:** A denervated transplanted heart has a profound, 10- to 20-fold hypersensitivity to adenosine, and a standard dose will cause prolonged, dangerous asystole. The dose must be reduced to 1–2 mg initially, given only by a specialist.
- **Known, Non-Compensated Sinus Node Disease or High-Grade AV Block** without a pacemaker.
- **Dipylidamole Use:** Dipylidamole blocks the cellular reuptake of adenosine, massively potentiating its effect. A standard dose of adenosine in a patient on dipylidamole can cause prolonged, life-threatening asystole.

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- **The "Two-Syringe, Rapid IV Push Technique":** Draw 6 mg of adenosine into a 3 mL syringe. Attach this syringe and a separate 20 mL normal saline flush syringe to the nearest, most proximal, large-bore IV cannula port (antecubital fossa is best).
- **Inject the adenosine as a rapid, single-bore bolus, and immediately follow it with the 20 mL saline flush**, holding the arm elevated. This is a bolus chase technique. The goal is to deliver the entire dose as a single, concentrated column of drug into the central circulation. If given through a slow IV line in the hand, the drug is metabolized before it ever reaches the heart, and it will have no effect. This is the single most common error in adenosine administration.
- The initial dose is 6 mg. If there is no effect after 1–2 minutes, a second dose of 12 mg is administered. A third dose of 12 mg can be given.

### Monitoring and Key Points of Attention:

- **Warn the Patient:** Before pushing the drug, you must say: "You are about to feel a very strange and frightening sensation. Your chest will feel tight, you will feel like you cannot breathe, and you may feel a sense of impending doom. This will last about 20 seconds, and then it will completely go away. Do not panic. I will be right here." If you do not say this, the patient will panic, the catecholamine surge will re-initiate the SVT, and your entire intervention will be wasted.
- **Rhythm Strip Monitoring:** A continuous 12-lead ECG rhythm strip must be running during and after the injection. The characteristic response is: a brief period of sinus bradycardia, sinus arrest, or high-grade AV block (asystole for up to 3–6 seconds), during which the P-waves are seen without QRS complexes. Then the SVT terminates and is replaced by a normal sinus rhythm. The period of asystole is the desired, therapeutic effect.
- **Atrial Fibrillation Induction:** Adenosine can transiently induce atrial fibrillation in up to 12% of patients. This is usually brief and self-terminating. However, in a patient with an accessory pathway (WPW), the induced AF can conduct rapidly down the bypass tract and degenerate into ventricular fibrillation. This is a rare but catastrophic event. A defibrillator must be at the bedside.

## 4. Atropine

**Mechanism of Action:**

A competitive, reversible antagonist of acetylcholine at peripheral muscarinic receptors (M2 receptors on the sinoatrial and AV nodes). It blocks the vagal, parasympathetic tone on the heart, increasing the rate of spontaneous phase 4 depolarization in the sinus node and accelerating AV nodal conduction.[1]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Symptomatic Sinus Bradycardia with Hypotension:** 0.5 mg IV bolus, repeated every 3–5 minutes up to a maximum total dose of 3 mg. The 2025 AHA guidelines have significantly downgraded the role of atropine. It is no longer recommended for routine use in asystole or PEA cardiac arrest; the evidence shows no benefit.[1]
- **Organophosphate Poisoning:** Massive doses of atropine (2–5 mg IV every 5 minutes) are used to reverse the life-threatening muscarinic effects (bradycardia, bronchospasm, bronchorrhea).

### Contraindications:

- There is no true contraindication in an emergency. However, it is ineffective and potentially harmful in bradycardia caused by a block distal to the AV node (Mobitz II second-degree AV block, third-degree AV block with a wide QRS escape rhythm). Atropine will increase the sinus rate but will not improve the conduction through the diseased His-Purkinje system, potentially worsening the block.

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- The pre-filled syringe is 1 mg in 10 mL (0.1 mg/mL). Give it as a rapid IV bolus. The minimum dose is 0.5 mg. Doses <0.5 mg can paradoxically cause bradycardia due to a central vagotonic effect.

### Monitoring:

- The target is a heart rate >60 bpm and resolution of hypotension. Monitor for anticholinergic side effects: dry mouth, mydriasis, and confusion.

## 5. Lidocaine

**Mechanism of Action:**

A class Ib antiarrhythmic. It blocks the fast inward sodium channels (INa) in cardiac Purkinje fibers and ventricular muscle. It binds preferentially to open or inactivated sodium channels, and its effect is most pronounced in ischemic, depolarized tissue. It shortens the action potential duration and the effective refractory period in the Purkinje fibers, which helps to suppress ventricular ectopy and re-entrant ventricular arrhythmias in the setting of acute myocardial ischemia.[1]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Refractory Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia/Ventricular Fibrillation:** An alternative to amiodarone in the 2025 AHA ACLS algorithm. The dose is 1–1.5 mg/kg IV bolus, followed by a second dose of 0.5–0.75 mg/kg if persistent. The maximum total dose over the first hour is 3 mg/kg.[1]
- **Stable Monomorphic Ventricular Tachycardia:** When amiodarone is not available or contraindicated.

### Contraindications:

- **High-Grade AV Block without a Pacemaker:** Lidocaine can suppress a ventricular escape rhythm.
- **Stokes-Adams Attacks.**
- **Known Hypersensitivity to Amide Local Anesthetics.**

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- A pre-filled 100 mg in 5 mL syringe is used for the bolus. For an infusion, 1 gram of lidocaine in 500 mL of D5W (2 mg/mL) is run at 1–4 mg/min.

### Monitoring:

- **Neurological Toxicity:** The earliest signs are perioral numbness, tinnitus, metallic taste, confusion, and dysarthria. This progresses to seizures and coma. This is a classic, predictable, and dose-dependent adverse effect. The infusion must be stopped immediately at the first sign of neurotoxicity.
- **Cardiac Toxicity:** Sinus arrest, AV block, and myocardial depression at high doses.

## 6. Calcium Gluconate (or Calcium Chloride)

**Mechanism of Action:**

Calcium ions directly antagonize the depolarizing effect of potassium on the resting membrane potential of cardiac myocytes. In severe hyperkalemia, the extracellular potassium concentration is so high that the resting membrane potential rises closer to the threshold potential, causing depolarization, slow conduction, and eventual inexcitability. Intravenous calcium does not lower the serum potassium level. It rapidly shifts the threshold potential to a more negative value, restoring a normal electrical gradient and stabilizing the myocardium. This effect occurs within 1–3 minutes and lasts for 30–60 minutes.[3]

### Clinical Uses:

- **Severe Hyperkalemia with ECG Changes (Peaked T-waves, Loss of P-waves, Widened QRS):** 10–20 mL of 10% calcium gluconate (1–2 grams) IV over 2–3 minutes. This is the first and most critical step. It is indicated only when the ECG is abnormal or the potassium is >6.5 mmol/L. It protects the heart while the potassium is shifted and eliminated. This is a seconds-to-minutes emergency.
- **Calcium Channel Blocker Overdose:** High-dose IV calcium chloride (or gluconate) is used to overcome the competitive blockade of the calcium channel. The dose is 2 grams of calcium gluconate IV, followed by an infusion.

### Contraindications:

- **Digoxin Toxicity:** The combination of IV calcium and digoxin is classically described as causing a non-recoverable, "stone heart" asystole. The in vivo evidence for this is weak and based on older case reports; modern expert opinion is that if the patient is in peri-arrest from severe hyperkalemia, you give the calcium, even if they are on digoxin. However, it should be given as a slow infusion, not a rapid bolus, and under continuous cardiac monitoring.

### How to Prepare and Administer:

- Calcium gluconate is given through a large-bore IV. Calcium chloride is a vesicant and causes severe tissue necrosis if it extravasates; it must be administered through a central venous catheter.
- **Do not mix calcium with sodium bicarbonate in the same IV line.** They will combine and form a solid, insoluble calcium carbonate precipitate, which will clog the IV tubing completely.

### Monitoring:

- The effect is seen immediately on the cardiac monitor. The peaked T-waves flatten, the QRS narrows, and the P-waves reappear if they were lost. The effect wears off in 30–60 minutes, and the potassium level is unchanged, so definitive potassium-lowering therapy (insulin + dextrose, salbutamol, dialysis) must follow immediately.

**Part Two: Chronic Cardiac Drugs Found on the Patient's Medication Administration Record (The "Lizom") and How to Adjust Them**

A hospitalized cardiac patient will typically be on several or all of the following drug classes. Your task in the acute setting is to decide which to hold, which to continue, and which to add.

## 1. Beta-Blockers (e.g., Bisoprolol, Carvedilol, Metoprolol Succinate)

**Mechanism:** Block beta-1 adrenergic receptors in the heart. This reduces heart rate, myocardial contractility (negative inotrope), and renin release from the juxtaglomerular cells. In the chronic setting, this reverses pathological ventricular remodeling, reduces myocardial oxygen demand, and is a pillar of mortality reduction in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and post-myocardial infarction.[4]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Acute Decompensated Heart Failure with Hypotension or Cardiogenic Shock:** Hold the beta-blocker. The negative inotropic effect is acutely harmful in a failing, hypoperfused heart. Restart at a low dose only when the patient is warm, euvolemic, and off inotropes/vasopressors for at least 24–48 hours.[4]
- **Acute Pulmonary Edema (Hypertensive, Well-Perfused):** Continue the beta-blocker. The heart rate control is beneficial to reduce myocardial oxygen demand. Do not hold it.
- **Bradycardia or High-Grade AV Block:** Hold the beta-blocker immediately. This is the most common cause of symptomatic bradycardia on the ward.
- **STEMI:** An oral beta-blocker (metoprolol tartrate 25–50 mg PO) should be initiated within the first 24 hours, provided there are no signs of heart failure, hypotension, bradycardia, or cardiogenic shock. This is a Class I recommendation.[5]

## 2. ACE Inhibitors (e.g., Ramipril, Lisinopril) / ARBs (e.g., Valsartan) / ARNI (Sacubitril/Valsartan)

**Mechanism:** ACE-I blocks the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. ARBs block the angiotensin II type 1 receptor. ARNI is an ARB combined with a neprilysin inhibitor, which increases endogenous natriuretic peptides. The net effect is arterial and venous vasodilation (reducing preload and afterload), reduced aldosterone secretion, and inhibition of pathological cardiac fibrosis. These are pillars of mortality reduction in HFrEF.[4]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Acute Kidney Injury (Creatinine Rise >30% from Baseline) or Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5 mmol/L):** Hold the ACE-I/ARB/ARNI immediately. The drug is impairing the kidney's ability to maintain the glomerular filtration pressure in a hypoperfused state (efferent arteriole vasodilation). Recheck the renal function and potassium daily. Restart at a lower dose when the creatinine returns to baseline and the potassium is below 5.0 mmol/L.[4]
- **Hypotension or Cardiogenic Shock:** Hold the drug.
- **Post-STEMI:** An ACE-I (or ARB if intolerant) should be initiated within the first 24 hours in all patients with an anterior MI, LVEF ≤40%, heart failure, or diabetes, unless contraindicated by hypotension or renal failure.[5]

## 3. Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs) (e.g., Spironolactone, Eplerenone)

**Mechanism:** Block the aldosterone receptor in the distal tubule and collecting duct, promoting natriuresis and potassium retention. In the heart, they inhibit aldosterone-mediated cardiac fibrosis and pathological remodeling. They reduce mortality in HFrEF and post-STEMI with LV dysfunction.[4]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.0 mmol/L):** This is the most common reason to hold an MRA. Hold the drug immediately. Recheck the potassium daily.
- **Acute Kidney Injury (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m² or Creatinine >2.5 mg/dL in men or >2.0 mg/dL in women):** The MRA is contraindicated. It must be stopped and only restarted if the renal function recovers to a safe level.[4]

## 4. Digoxin

**Mechanism:** A cardiac glycoside with two main effects. (1) It inhibits the myocardial Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump. The resulting increase in intracellular sodium reduces the driving force for the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, leading to an increase in intracellular calcium and a positive inotropic effect. (2) It increases vagal (parasympathetic) tone on the AV node, which slows AV nodal conduction and prolongs the refractory period. This is the primary effect used for rate control in atrial fibrillation.[6]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Bradycardia or High-Grade AV Block:** Hold digoxin. This is a classic sign of digoxin toxicity.
- **Nausea, Vomiting, Confusion, or Yellow-Vision (Xanthopsia):** These are the classical, non-cardiac symptoms of digoxin toxicity. Hold the drug, check a serum digoxin level, and an ECG.
- **Acute Kidney Injury:** Digoxin is renally cleared. If the creatinine doubles, the digoxin level will double. The dose must be empirically reduced by 50% or held, and a trough serum digoxin level must be checked.
- **Hypokalemia:** A low potassium level potentiates digoxin binding to the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump and dramatically increases the risk of digoxin toxicity, even at a "normal" serum digoxin level. The potassium must be aggressively repleted to >4.0 mmol/L.[6]

## 5. Loop Diuretics (Furosemide, Torsemide, Bumetanide)

**Mechanism:** Inhibit the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporter on the luminal membrane of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, blocking the reabsorption of sodium, chloride, and potassium and abolishing the countercurrent multiplier mechanism. This causes a massive, dose-dependent natriuresis and diuresis.[4]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Acute Decompensated Heart Failure with Volume Overload:** The dose of IV furosemide must be at least double the patient's chronic oral daily dose, given as an IV bolus. A patient on 80 mg PO BID of furosemide at home needs an initial IV dose of 160–200 mg. This is a common error: under-dosing the diuretic in the acute setting. The goal is a urine output of >1–2 liters in the first 6 hours. If the patient does not respond, the dose is doubled every 1–2 hours until a response is achieved. A furosemide infusion (10–20 mg/hour) is often more effective and avoids the post-bolus sodium retention rebound.[4]
- **Hypovolemia or Pre-Renal AKI:** The diuretic must be held. A falling creatinine, a rising BUN:creatinine ratio, a flat JVP, and orthostatic hypotension are the signs that the patient is over-diuresed and volume-depleted.

## 6. Antiplatelet Agents

- **Aspirin:** Continue lifelong in all patients with coronary artery disease, peripheral arterial disease, and cerebrovascular disease. Dose is 75–100 mg daily. In the acute setting, hold only if there is active, life-threatening bleeding or a platelet count <20,000/µL. In a post-cardiac arrest STEMI or NSTEMI, give a loading dose of 150–300 mg chewed if not already given.
- **P2Y12 Inhibitors (Clopidogrel, Ticagrelor, Prasugrel):** The duration of DAPT is determined by the indication. After an ACS with a drug-eluting stent, DAPT is typically continued for 6–12 months. Premature cessation of the P2Y12 inhibitor within the first month (for a drug-eluting stent) or the first 3 months (for a bare-metal stent) carries a very high risk of acute, catastrophic in-stent thrombosis and a 50–70% mortality rate. This is a cardinal rule of cardiology: **Never stop the P2Y12 inhibitor in a patient with a recent stent without speaking to the interventional cardiologist who placed the stent.** The only exception is life-threatening, uncontrolled bleeding.

## 7. Anticoagulants (Warfarin, Apixaban, Rivaroxaban, etc.)

**Mechanism:** Warfarin inhibits the vitamin K-dependent synthesis of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. DOACs directly inhibit factor Xa (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) or thrombin (dabigatran). They are indicated for stroke prevention in non-valvular atrial fibrillation (CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥2 in men, ≥3 in women), and for the treatment and secondary prevention of venous thromboembolism.[7]

### How to Adjust in the Acute Setting:

- **Active Major Bleeding or Emergency Surgery:** The anticoagulant must be reversed. For warfarin, give 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC, Kcentra) and IV vitamin K 10 mg. For apixaban or rivaroxaban, andexanet alfa (Andexxa) is the specific reversal agent. For dabigatran, idarucizumab (Praxbind) is the specific reversal agent.
- **Acute Ischemic Stroke in a Patient with AF:** Do not reflexively stop the anticoagulant. The patient is on it for a reason (to prevent a cardioembolic stroke). A multidisciplinary decision is made to either restart the anticoagulant early (within 1–3 days for a small stroke), delay it (for a large stroke with a risk of hemorrhagic transformation), or place a left atrial appendage occlusion device. This is a specialist decision.
- **Acute Kidney Injury (DOACs):** All DOACs are renally cleared to varying degrees. In acute kidney injury with a CrCl <30 mL/min (for rivaroxaban) or <25 mL/min (for apixaban), the drug must be held or the dose must be reduced. If the CrCl falls below 15 mL/min, a switch to warfarin (which is hepatically cleared) is generally indicated.[7]
