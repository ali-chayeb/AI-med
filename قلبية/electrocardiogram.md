## First: Definition, Classification & Technical Principle

**Definition:** The electrocardiogram is a non-invasive, transthoracic graphical recording of the summed electrical activity of the heart, measured from the body surface. It displays the voltage (in millivolts) on the vertical axis against time (in seconds) on the horizontal axis. It captures the sequence of depolarization and repolarization of atrial and ventricular myocardium during each cardiac cycle. The ECG is the gold standard first-line investigation for the evaluation of cardiac rhythm, conduction abnormalities, myocardial ischemia and infarction, chamber enlargement, electrolyte disturbances, and the effects of cardioactive drugs.[1]

**Classification:** The ECG is a physiological, non-invasive, bioelectrical investigation. It is a surface recording of the cardiac electrical field, classified as a graphical vectorcardiographic scalar display. Standard clinical ECG consists of 12 leads, generated from 10 electrodes placed on the limbs and chest. The 12 leads are subdivided into six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) that record electrical activity in the frontal plane, and six precordial leads (V1 through V6) that record electrical activity in the horizontal plane. A rhythm strip (typically lead II or V1) provides a continuous recording of at least 10 seconds for rhythm analysis.[1,2]

### Technical Principle:

The ECG is based on the dipole theory of cardiac electrical activity. The heart acts as a time-varying electrical dipole within a volume conductor (the thorax). The fundamental physiological events are the depolarization and repolarization of atrial and ventricular myocytes, which produce extracellular voltage differences.[1,3]

- **Cellular Electrophysiology:** In the resting cardiac myocyte, the intracellular potential is approximately –90 mV relative to the extracellular space (phase 4 of the action potential), maintained by the Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase pump and the inwardly rectifying K⁺ current (IK1). Depolarization (phase 0) occurs when voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, causing a rapid influx of Na⁺ ions, shifting the membrane potential to approximately +20 mV. This generates a moving wavefront of positive charge inside the cell, with a corresponding negative wavefront on the outside. The ECG electrode detects this advancing wave of extracellular negativity. By convention, a wave of depolarization moving toward a positive electrode produces a positive (upward) deflection on the ECG. A wave of depolarization moving away from the positive electrode produces a negative (downward) deflection. Repolarization (phases 1-3) involves K⁺ efflux restoring the resting potential. Because repolarization progresses from epicardium to endocardium (opposite to depolarization), the T wave is normally upright in the same leads as the QRS complex.[1,3]
- **The Cardiac Dipole and Vectorcardiography:** At any instant, the summed electrical activity of millions of myocytes can be represented as a single dipole vector with a magnitude and direction. The standard ECG displays this vector on two simultaneous planes. Each lead is a pair of electrodes; the limb leads record the projection of the cardiac vector onto the frontal plane, and the chest leads record its projection onto the horizontal plane. The vector changes in magnitude and direction throughout the cardiac cycle, and the ECG tracing is the scalar amplitude of this vector over time as seen by each lead.[3]

### Lead System (Einthoven, Goldberger, Wilson):

  - **Bipolar Limb Leads (Einthoven Triangle, Frontal Plane):** Lead I records the potential difference between the left arm (positive) and right arm (negative). Lead II: left leg (positive) and right arm (negative). Lead III: left leg (positive) and left arm (negative). The algebraic relationship is Einthoven's Law: Lead II = Lead I + Lead III at any instant. The axis of the heart is calculated from leads I and aVF.[1]
  - **Augmented Unipolar Limb Leads (Goldberger, Frontal Plane):** Leads aVR, aVL, and aVF are derived by comparing one limb electrode against the averaged potential of the other two. This increases the signal amplitude by 50%. They record from the right shoulder (aVR, –150°), left shoulder (aVL, –30°), and left foot (aVF, +90°). aVR is conventionally inverted; ST-elevation in aVR (>1 mm) is a marker of severe left main or triple-vessel disease.[1,2]
  - **Unipolar Precordial Leads (Wilson, Horizontal Plane):** Leads V1 through V6 are measured against the Wilson Central Terminal, which averages the potentials of the three limb electrodes to create a theoretical zero reference. They are placed at precise anatomical positions: V1: 4th intercostal space, right sternal border. V2: 4th intercostal space, left sternal border. V3: midway between V2 and V4. V4: 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line. V5: anterior axillary line, same horizontal level as V4. V6: midaxillary line, same horizontal level as V4. Leads V1-V2 view the right ventricle and septum; V3-V4 view the anterior left ventricle; V5-V6 view the lateral left ventricle.[1]
- **Signal Processing and Display:** The ECG machine amplifies the microvolt-level skin signals, filters out noise (muscle artifact, baseline wander, power-line interference at 50/60 Hz), and displays or prints the tracing. Standard ECG paper speed is 25 mm/second. At this speed, one small square (1 mm) = 0.04 seconds (40 ms) and one large square (5 mm) = 0.20 seconds (200 ms). Standard calibration: a 1 mV signal produces a 10 mm vertical deflection. If the QRS is very large (left ventricular hypertrophy) or very small (obesity, pericardial effusion), the calibration may be set to half-standard (1 mV = 5 mm) or double-standard (1 mV = 20 mm).[1]

### What is Needed to Perform the Investigation:

- **Equipment:** 12-lead ECG machine with patient cable, 10 disposable silver/silver chloride adhesive electrodes, skin preparation materials (alcohol wipes, abrasive pad or fine sandpaper, clippers for hair removal), ECG paper.
- **Patient Preparation:** The patient must remove clothing from the upper body; a gown is provided. The skin at electrode sites must be clean, dry, and free of oils. Excessive chest hair should be clipped (not shaved, to avoid microabrasions that create electrical noise). The skin is gently abraded with a dry gauze or fine sandpaper to remove the superficial stratum corneum and reduce skin impedance. Alcohol wipes are used to clean the skin but must be allowed to dry completely before electrode application (wet alcohol creates a high-impedance circuit and poor-quality tracing). Electrodes must be fresh, within their expiration date, with conductive gel intact.[1,4]
- **Patient Positioning:** The patient must lie supine with arms at the sides, legs uncrossed, and fully relaxed. The head should rest on a single pillow. Speaking and movement during the recording cause muscle artifact. A standard 10-second recording is obtained during quiet respiration. For rhythm analysis, a longer rhythm strip (30–60 seconds) may be printed.
- **Correct Electrode Placement:** The limb electrodes must be placed on the limbs, not the torso, for standard diagnostic ECG. Placement on the torso (Mason-Likar modification) alters the frontal plane axis and QRS amplitude and is acceptable only for continuous monitoring (telemetry, Holter, exercise stress testing), not for the standard diagnostic 12-lead ECG. The 10 electrodes are: right arm (RA), left arm (LA), right leg (RL, ground), left leg (LL), and V1 through V6. Misplacement of V1 and V2 in the 2nd or 3rd intercostal space (too high) is the most common technical error and produces a spurious rSR' pattern in V1 mimicking incomplete right bundle branch block, and can mask anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or cause a false diagnosis of Brugada pattern. The electrodes must be placed precisely at the sternal border in the 4th intercostal space (the 4th ICS is found by palpating the sternal angle of Louis at the 2nd ICS, then counting down two spaces).[4,5]

## Second: Indications (Why & When to Order)

### Screening Indications:

- **Pre-operative evaluation:** Screening ECG is indicated in asymptomatic patients scheduled for high-risk surgery (intra-thoracic, intra-abdominal, major vascular, major orthopedic, neurosurgery), or in patients with any cardiovascular risk factor (age >65, hypertension, diabetes, known coronary artery disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease) undergoing intermediate-risk surgery. The ECG provides a baseline for perioperative cardiac ischemia and arrhythmia monitoring. Routine screening ECG in asymptomatic, low-risk patients undergoing low-risk surgery is not recommended by the 2024 ACC/AHA Perioperative Guideline.[6]
- **Pre-participation sports screening:** A resting 12-lead ECG is recommended as part of the pre-participation cardiovascular screening for competitive athletes, per the 2017 AHA/ACC and 2021 ESC guidelines on sports cardiology. The goal is to detect hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, and other conditions associated with sudden cardiac death in young athletes. The ECG is interpreted according to the International Consensus Criteria for Athlete's ECG, which distinguishes physiological (training-related) adaptations from pathological findings.[7]
- **Baseline ECG before initiating QT-prolonging medications:** Screening ECG is indicated before starting drugs with a known high risk of Torsades de Pointes (e.g., sotalol, dofetilide, amiodarone, methadone, haloperidol, azithromycin, levofloxacin, many antipsychotics and antiemetics) in patients with risk factors (older age, female sex, electrolyte disturbances, structural heart disease, congenital long QT syndrome).
- **Screening for silent coronary artery disease:** In asymptomatic adults, the USPSTF 2018 recommends against screening with resting or exercise ECG in adults at low risk of CVD events. The ECG has poor sensitivity and specificity for silent ischemia in low-risk populations and generates a high rate of false-positive findings leading to unnecessary downstream testing.

### Diagnostic Indications:

- **Chest pain or anginal equivalent:** The 12-lead ECG is the single most important initial diagnostic test and must be obtained and interpreted within 10 minutes of medical contact for any patient presenting with acute chest pain, per the 2025 AHA/ACC Guideline for the Management of Acute Coronary Syndromes. The ECG diagnoses ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) versus non-ST-elevation ACS (NSTEMI/Unstable Angina) and directs reperfusion strategy. The ECG in acute chest pain answers the binary clinical question: "Is there ST-segment elevation or new left bundle branch block that meets STEMI criteria?"[8]
- **Palpitations, syncope, or presyncope:** The ECG identifies tachyarrhythmias, bradyarrhythmias, pre-excitation (WPW), Brugada pattern, long or short QT syndrome, epsilon waves of ARVC, and evidence of structural heart disease predisposing to arrhythmia (left ventricular hypertrophy, Q waves of prior MI).
- **Unexplained dyspnea:** The ECG detects left ventricular hypertrophy (hypertensive heart disease, aortic stenosis), atrial enlargement (mitral valve disease, cardiomyopathy), Q waves of prior silent MI, and low QRS voltage (pericardial effusion, constrictive pericarditis), all of which can cause dyspnea.
- **Suspected acute pericarditis:** The ECG shows diffuse, concave-upward ST-segment elevation in multiple leads with PR-segment depression, evolving through four classical stages.
- **Suspected electrolyte disturbances:** The ECG is the most rapid diagnostic test for life-threatening hyperkalemia (peaked T waves, loss of P waves, sine wave pattern) and hypokalemia (U waves, ST depression, T-wave flattening). The ECG is more immediately actionable than the serum potassium level in cardiac arrest.
- **Suspected drug toxicity:** The ECG diagnoses tricyclic antidepressant overdose (wide QRS, prominent R wave in aVR, terminal right-axis deviation), digoxin toxicity (scooped ST depression, "Salvador Dalí mustache," atrial tachycardia with block), and sodium channel blocker toxicity (QRS widening).
- **Diagnosis of arrhythmias:** The gold standard for atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, heart blocks, and bundle branch blocks.

### Monitoring Indications:

- **Continuous ST-segment monitoring:** In patients with acute coronary syndrome or after cardiac surgery, continuous 12-lead ST-segment monitoring or telemetry monitoring is used to detect recurrent ischemia.
- **QTc interval monitoring during antiarrhythmic drug loading:** During initiation of sotalol, dofetilide, or ibutilide, the QTc must be monitored every 2–4 hours, and the drug withheld if QTc exceeds 500 ms or increases by >60 ms from baseline.
- **Monitoring during electrolyte correction:** In severe hyperkalemia or hypokalemia, the ECG must be monitored continuously until the serum potassium normalizes, to detect resolution of or deterioration in cardiac electrical stability.
- **Post-cardiac arrest:** Continuous ECG monitoring for 72 hours to detect re-arrest and to identify an underlying ischemic or arrhythmogenic cause.
- **During targeted temperature management:** The ECG is monitored for Osborn waves (J waves), which appear at core temperatures <32°C and are benign, as well as for prolonged QTc predisposing to ventricular arrhythmia.

### Contraindications and Risks:

- **Absolute contraindications:** None. The ECG is entirely non-invasive, painless, and without ionizing radiation.

### Relative cautions:

  - Severe skin breakdown, burns, or epidermolysis bullosa over the electrode placement sites: the adhesive may cause skin shear. Modified lead placement or non-adhesive electrodes may be used.
  - Allergy to electrode adhesive gel: rare; hypoallergenic electrodes are available.
- **Procedural risks:** The single most important "risk" of an ECG is not a physical complication but a cognitive error: misinterpretation by the clinician. A missed STEMI, a Brugada pattern misinterpreted as benign early repolarization, a long QT interval dismissed as normal, or a paced rhythm misinterpreted as a native rhythm with left bundle branch block — each of these interpretive errors can cause patient death. The standard for clinical competence is systematic, unhurried interpretation.[4]
- **Radiation exposure:** None.

### Pre-test Requirements:

- Patient must be supine and relaxed for at least 2–3 minutes before recording to allow heart rate to stabilize. A prior ECG, if available, must be obtained for comparison; the diagnostic yield of an ECG increases by 30–50% when compared to a previous tracing. The patient's medications (especially digoxin, antiarrhythmics, and QT-prolonging drugs), serum potassium, and calcium levels should be known to the interpreter.
- Electrode sites must be prepared as described above. The patient must be instructed not to move, speak, or shiver during the 10-second recording.

### The Clinical Question This Investigation Answers:

The ECG answers a set of five binary questions, in sequence: (1) What is the rate and is the rhythm sinus or non-sinus? (2) Are there ischemic ST-T changes meeting STEMI criteria? (3) Is there a conduction abnormality (bundle branch block, heart block, pre-excitation)? (4) Is there evidence of chamber enlargement or hypertrophy? (5) Are there metabolic or toxic effects (electrolyte abnormality, drug toxicity)? In the acute setting, the first two questions take absolute priority.

## Third: Systematic interpretation

### The reading sequence, stated once as a checklist[E]

Patient identifiers and calibration → **rate** → **rhythm** → **axis** → **P wave** → **PR interval** → **QRS complex (duration and morphology)** → **Q waves** → **ST segment** → **T wave** → **QT interval** → **chamber hypertrophy criteria** → **comparison with any previous ECG**. Comparison with a prior ECG is the single most valuable and most frequently skipped step — a "new" abnormality that is actually old and stable changes the entire clinical urgency.

Below, each step is expanded with its own differential diagnosis and the **mechanistic reason** for each entry — the "why," as requested — rather than a bare label list.

### Step 1 — Rate

**Method** [E] — count large squares between consecutive R waves (300/number of large squares) for a regular rhythm, or count QRS complexes over 6 seconds ×10 for an irregular one.

### Abnormality: Bradycardia (<60/min)

| **Cause** | **Why it produces bradycardia** |
|---|---|
| Sinus bradycardia (physiological) | High vagal tone in athletes and during sleep suppresses sinoatrial node automaticity directly. [E] |
| Sick sinus syndrome | Degenerative fibrosis of the sinoatrial node reduces the rate of spontaneous pacemaker depolarisation. [E] |
| Hypothyroidism | Reduced beta-adrenergic receptor density and sensitivity blunts sympathetic drive to the SA node. [E] |
| Hypothermia | Direct slowing of ion channel kinetics reduces the rate of pacemaker cell depolarisation. [E] |
| Raised intracranial pressure (Cushing reflex) | Baroreceptor-mediated reflex vagal surge in response to hypertension from cerebral ischaemia. [E] |
| Drugs (beta-blockers, digoxin, non-dihydropyridine CCBs) | Direct pharmacological suppression of SA node automaticity or AV conduction slowing misread as sinus bradycardia if AV block coexists. [E] |
| High-grade AV block | Escape rhythm rate is intrinsically slower than sinus rate because subsidiary pacemakers (junctional, ventricular) have slower intrinsic automaticity than the SA node. [E] |
| Hyperkalaemia | Elevated extracellular potassium reduces the resting membrane potential gradient, slowing conduction and pacemaker cell depolarisation. [E] |

### Abnormality: Tachycardia (>100/min)

| **Cause** | **Why it produces tachycardia** |
|---|---|
| Sinus tachycardia (physiological/secondary) | Increased sympathetic tone or reduced vagal tone accelerates SA node depolarisation — always ask what is driving it (fever, pain, hypovolaemia, anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, PE). [E] |
| Atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response | Chaotic atrial activity bombards the AV node with impulses far faster than it can filter, producing a rapid, irregular ventricular rate. [E] |
| Supraventricular tachycardia (AVNRT/AVRT) | A re-entrant circuit within or around the AV node continuously re-excites the atria and ventricles without needing the SA node at all. [E] |
| Ventricular tachycardia | A re-entrant circuit or triggered focus within ventricular myocardium bypasses the normal His-Purkinje activation sequence entirely, producing a rapid wide-complex rhythm. [E] |
| Thyrotoxicosis | Excess thyroid hormone upregulates beta-adrenergic receptor density and sensitivity in the SA node. [E] |
| Hypovolaemia/anaemia/hypoxia | Reflex sympathetic activation compensates for reduced effective circulating volume or oxygen-carrying capacity. [E] |

### Step 2 — Rhythm

**Method** [E] — assess regularity of the R-R interval, and confirm every QRS is preceded by a P wave with a constant PR interval (sinus rhythm) or not.

### Abnormality: Irregularly irregular rhythm

| **Cause** | **Why the rhythm is irregularly irregular** |
|---|---|
| Atrial fibrillation | Multiple chaotic re-entrant wavelets in the atria bombard the AV node at random intervals; the node conducts unpredictably, so no two R-R intervals relate to each other. [E] |
| Multifocal atrial tachycardia | Multiple competing atrial ectopic foci fire at different rates and are conducted at variable AV nodal timing, producing varying P-wave morphology and irregular R-R intervals — classically in severe COPD from hypoxia-driven atrial irritability. [E] |
| Frequent multifocal ectopy | Ectopic beats arising from different foci interrupt the underlying rhythm unpredictably. [E] |

### Abnormality: Regularly irregular rhythm

| **Cause** | **Why the rhythm is regularly irregular** |
|---|---|
| Second-degree AV block, fixed ratio (e.g. 2:1, 3:1) | A fixed proportion of atrial impulses fail to conduct through the AV node in a repeating pattern, producing a predictable, patterned irregularity. [E] |
| Bigeminy/trigeminy (fixed-coupling ectopy) | An ectopic focus fires at a fixed interval after each sinus beat (fixed coupling), producing a repeating short-long pattern. [E] |
| Sinus arrhythmia | Respiration modulates vagal tone on the SA node — inspiration reduces vagal tone and speeds the rate, expiration does the reverse — producing a cyclical, respiration-linked variation. [E] |

### Step 3 — Axis

**Method** [E] — assess the net QRS deflection in leads I and aVF. Both positive = normal axis (−30° to +90°). I positive, aVF negative = possible left axis deviation (confirm with lead II negative). I negative, aVF positive = right axis deviation. Both negative = extreme axis deviation ("no man's land").

### Abnormality: Left axis deviation

| **Cause** | **Why it shifts the axis left** |
|---|---|
| Left anterior fascicular block | Loss of the normal early inferior-then-superior activation sequence of the left ventricle delays activation of the anterior/superior LV wall, shifting the mean vector superiorly and leftward. [E] |
| Left ventricular hypertrophy | The hypertrophied, thicker left ventricular free wall generates a disproportionately large electrical vector directed toward it, pulling the mean axis leftward. [E] |
| Inferior myocardial infarction (old) | Loss of viable myocardium in the inferior wall removes its normal contribution to the vector, leaving the remaining superior forces relatively unopposed. [E] |
| Some cases of ventricular pre-excitation (WPW) | An accessory pathway inserting in specific locations produces an abnormal initial activation vector that can mimic axis deviation. [L] |

### Abnormality: Right axis deviation

| **Cause** | **Why it shifts the axis right** |
|---|---|
| Right ventricular hypertrophy | The hypertrophied right ventricle generates a disproportionately large rightward-directed vector that overcomes the normally dominant leftward LV forces. [E] |
| Left posterior fascicular block (rare, diagnosis of exclusion) | Delayed activation of the posterior/inferior LV wall shifts the vector inferiorly and rightward; other causes of right axis deviation must be excluded first. [E] |
| Acute right heart strain (e.g. massive pulmonary embolism) | Acute right ventricular dilatation from abrupt afterload increase shifts the electrical vector rightward before structural hypertrophy has time to develop. [E] |
| Normal in children and thin, tall adults | The heart's anatomical position is more vertical, physiologically directing the mean vector rightward without any pathology. [E] |
| Dextrocardia | The heart's mirror-image anatomical position produces a rightward vector as a direct anatomical consequence, not a pathological one — check for a positive P wave in aVR and progressive R-wave loss (rather than growth) across the precordium to confirm. [E] |

### Step 4 — P wave

**Method** [E] — assess morphology (best seen in lead II and V1), amplitude, and duration.

### Abnormality: Tall, peaked P wave ("P pulmonale") — amplitude >2.5 mm in lead II

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Right atrial enlargement | An enlarged right atrium takes longer to depolarise and generates a larger initial vector, producing a taller P wave in the leads viewing that initial rightward, anterior force (II, V1). [E] |
| Chronic pulmonary disease / pulmonary hypertension | Chronically elevated right-sided pressures cause right atrial hypertrophy as a direct haemodynamic consequence. [E] |

### Abnormality: Broad, notched P wave ("P mitrale") — duration >120 ms, notched in lead II

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Left atrial enlargement | An enlarged left atrium takes longer to complete depolarisation; because the left atrium depolarises slightly after the right, the terminal (left atrial) portion of the P wave is prolonged and often notched, classically from mitral valve disease. [E] |

### Abnormality: Absent P waves

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Atrial fibrillation | Chaotic, disorganised atrial electrical activity produces no single coordinated depolarisation wavefront, so no discrete P wave can form — only an undulating fibrillatory baseline. [E] |
| Sinoatrial arrest with junctional escape | Failure of SA node impulse generation removes the P wave; a junctional pacemaker takes over, and its impulse may not conduct retrogradely to the atria in a way that produces a visible P wave before the QRS. [E] |
| Hyperkalaemia (severe) | Elevated extracellular potassium reduces atrial myocardial excitability and conduction velocity progressively until the P wave flattens and disappears — a late, ominous sign preceding a "sine wave" pattern. [E] |

### Abnormality: P wave morphology varies beat to beat

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Multifocal atrial tachycardia / wandering atrial pacemaker | Depolarisation originates from different atrial foci on different beats, and each focus produces a P wave with a different vector and therefore a different surface morphology. [E] |

### Step 5 — PR interval

**Method** [E] — measured from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex; normal range 120–200 ms.

### Abnormality: Short PR interval (<120 ms)

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Wolff-Parkinson-White (ventricular pre-excitation) | An accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) conducts directly from atrium to ventricle, bypassing the normal decremental conduction delay of the AV node, so ventricular activation begins earlier than normal — producing both a short PR and, classically, a slurred upstroke (delta wave). [E] |
| Junctional rhythm | The pacemaker arises at or near the AV node itself, so there is little to no distance and delay for the impulse to travel before reaching the ventricles; the P wave (if visible) may be inverted or buried in the QRS. [E] |

### Abnormality: Prolonged PR interval (>200 ms) — first-degree AV block

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Increased vagal tone (including in fit athletes) | Vagal input to the AV node slows conduction velocity through the node itself — a physiological, reversible delay. [E] |
| AV nodal disease (fibrosis, ischaemia) | Structural degeneration of the AV node's conducting tissue slows impulse propagation through it. [E] |
| Drugs (beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs, digoxin) | Direct pharmacological slowing of AV nodal conduction velocity. [E] |
| Hyperkalaemia | Reduced excitability of AV nodal tissue slows conduction through it, in the same way it slows atrial and ventricular conduction. [E] |

**Abnormality: Progressively lengthening PR interval, eventually a dropped QRS — Mobitz I (second-degree AV block)**

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| AV nodal fatigue/decremental conduction | Each successive impulse finds the AV node still partially refractory from the previous one, so conduction takes progressively longer until one impulse fails to conduct at all, after which the node recovers and the cycle restarts. [E] Usually benign, often vagally mediated. |

**Abnormality: Constant PR interval with an unexpected dropped QRS — Mobitz II (second-degree AV block)**

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Disease below the AV node (His bundle or bundle branches) | The block occurs distal to the AV node itself, in tissue that conducts in an all-or-nothing fashion rather than decrementally, so there is no warning lengthening beforehand — impulses either conduct normally or fail completely. This is why Mobitz II is considered more dangerous: it can progress abruptly to complete heart block. [E] |

**Abnormality: No relationship between P waves and QRS complexes — third-degree (complete) AV block**

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Complete failure of AV conduction | No atrial impulse reaches the ventricles at all; the ventricles are driven by an independent subsidiary (junctional or ventricular) escape pacemaker, so atrial and ventricular rates are dissociated and unrelated. [E] |

### Step 6 — QRS complex: duration and morphology

**Method** [E] — measure duration (normal <120 ms); assess morphology for bundle branch block patterns, especially in V1 and V6.

**Abnormality: Widened QRS (>120 ms) with RSR' pattern in V1 and a broad S wave in V6 — right bundle branch block**

| **Why** |
|---|
| The right bundle branch fails to conduct, so the right ventricle is activated late, via slow cell-to-cell spread from the already-depolarised left ventricle rather than via the fast conducting system — producing a delayed, broad terminal rightward force seen as the second R wave (R') in V1 and the broad terminal S wave in V6. [E] |

**Abnormality: Widened QRS with a broad, notched R wave in V6 and a deep S wave in V1 — left bundle branch block**

| **Why** |
|---|
| The left bundle branch fails to conduct, so the left ventricle — the dominant electrical contributor — is activated late via slow transseptal spread from the right ventricle, producing a broad, delayed, notched R wave in the leads facing the left ventricle and abolishing the normal small initial septal Q waves in the lateral leads. [E] New LBBB in the context of ischaemic symptoms is managed per the current ACS guideline as a high-risk equivalent in the appropriate clinical context [8]. [G] |

### Abnormality: Widened, bizarre QRS with AV dissociation — ventricular tachycardia

| **Why** |
|---|
| Ventricular myocardium is activated by a re-entrant circuit or ectopic focus within the ventricle itself, spreading slowly cell-to-cell rather than via the fast His-Purkinje system, producing a wide, slurred, often bizarre QRS morphology unrelated to any preceding P wave. [E] |

### Abnormality: Widened QRS with a short PR and a slurred initial upstroke (delta wave)

| **Why** |
|---|
| Wolff-Parkinson-White — the accessory pathway pre-excites part of the ventricle directly, fusing with the normally conducted impulse arriving slightly later via the AV node; the fusion produces the characteristic slurred delta wave and a widened QRS, even though overall conduction is, paradoxically, *faster* to begin. [E] |

**Abnormality: Low-voltage QRS (amplitude <5 mm in all limb leads or <10 mm in all precordial leads)**

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Pericardial effusion | Fluid between the heart and the recording electrodes acts as an insulator, attenuating the electrical signal reaching the body surface. [E] |
| Obesity/emphysema | Increased distance (adipose tissue) or air (hyperinflated lungs, a poor electrical conductor) between the heart and the electrodes attenuates the signal. [E] |
| Infiltrative cardiomyopathy (amyloidosis) | Amyloid deposition replaces functioning myocardium with electrically inert material, reducing the net voltage generated — the classic and high-yield discriminator from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, which shows *increased* voltage despite similar wall thickness on imaging. [E] |
| Hypothyroidism | Myxoedematous infiltration of the myocardium and associated pericardial effusion both reduce the transmitted voltage. [E] |

### Step 7 — Q waves

**Method** [E] — a Q wave is pathological if it is ≥40 ms (one small square) in duration, or ≥25% of the height of the following R wave in that lead, and present in leads that do not normally show one (small septal Q waves in I, aVL, V5, V6 are normal and reflect normal left-to-right septal depolarisation).

### Abnormality: Pathological Q waves

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Prior transmural myocardial infarction | Full-thickness myocardial necrosis leaves electrically inert scar tissue that generates no depolarisation vector of its own; the recording electrode over that territory instead "sees through" the dead tissue to the opposite, healthy ventricular wall, recording that wall's vector moving *away* from it — producing a deep negative deflection (Q wave) that persists indefinitely as a permanent marker of prior infarction. [E] |
| Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy | Asymmetric septal hypertrophy alters the normal septal depolarisation vector, producing deep, narrow "pseudo-infarct" Q waves, classically in the inferolateral leads, without any actual infarction. [E] |
| Wolff-Parkinson-White | Pre-excitation via certain accessory pathway locations can produce a negative initial deflection that mimics a pathological Q wave — a recognised pseudo-infarct pattern. [L] |
| Left bundle branch block | Loss of normal septal activation (which normally proceeds left-to-right) can abolish or distort the normal small septal q waves and occasionally mimic pathological Q waves elsewhere — one reason LBBB is said to "hide" infarction on ECG. [E] |

### Step 8 — ST segment

**Method** [E] — assessed at the J point (junction of QRS and ST segment), relative to the isoelectric baseline (measured from the preceding PR segment).

::: **Abnormality: ST elevation** — the highest-stakes finding on the entire tracing

**Governing criteria** [G] — ST-segment elevation at the J point should be present in **2 or more contiguous leads**, exceeding **0.2 mV (2 mm) in leads V1–V3** and **0.1 mV (1 mm) in all other leads** [4,5]. This is the operational threshold for the STEMI ECG pattern.

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| ST-elevation myocardial infarction | Complete or near-complete coronary occlusion produces transmural ischaemia; the injured, depolarised-but-not-repolarising myocardium creates a persistent voltage gradient during the ST segment (the "injury current"), recorded as elevation in the leads overlying that territory and as reciprocal depression in the electrically opposite leads. [E] |
| Coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina) | Transient, severe epicardial spasm produces transmural ischaemia identical in ECG appearance to atherothrombotic occlusion, but it resolves spontaneously as the spasm relieves — distinguished by resolution of both pain and ST elevation together, often at rest. [E] |
| Pericarditis | Diffuse subepicardial inflammation produces a widespread injury current not confined to a single coronary territory, giving diffuse, concave ST elevation across multiple territories with associated PR depression, rather than the localised, convex elevation of infarction. [E] |
| Left ventricular aneurysm | A prior transmural infarction leaves a thin, dyskinetic, scarred wall segment; the persistent electrical abnormality at its border produces persistent ST elevation that can be mistaken for acute injury — distinguished by a stable, unchanged pattern on serial ECGs, often with associated deep Q waves. [E] |
| Benign early repolarisation | A normal variant in which the terminal portion of depolarisation and the beginning of repolarisation overlap slightly, producing mild concave ST elevation, classically in young, healthy individuals with a notch or slur at the J point — a diagnosis of exclusion. [E] |
| Brugada pattern | An inherited sodium-channelopathy alters the balance of ionic currents during early repolarisation in the right ventricular outflow tract, producing a characteristic coved ST elevation in V1–V2 with a specific risk of ventricular arrhythmia and sudden death. [E] |
| Hyperkalaemia (severe) | Markedly elevated extracellular potassium can produce a localised ST-elevation pattern mimicking infarction ("hyperkalaemic STEMI mimic"), through altered resting membrane potential and repolarisation kinetics — resolves with correction of potassium. [L] |
| Left ventricular hypertrophy / left bundle branch block | Secondary repolarisation abnormalities from the abnormal depolarisation sequence itself can produce ST changes (elevation in some leads, depression in others) that mimic ischaemia — this is precisely why LBBB "hides" infarction and requires dedicated criteria (e.g. Sgarbossa) rather than the standard threshold. [E] |
| ::: |  |

::: **Abnormality: ST depression**

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Subendocardial ischaemia (NSTEMI/unstable angina) | The subendocardium is the last-perfused, highest-pressure, most metabolically demanding layer of the ventricular wall (Part 1 of this project's cardiology review, item 1); when supply is reduced but not abolished, ischaemia there — without full-thickness injury — produces horizontal or downsloping ST depression rather than elevation, because the injury current vector points inward rather than outward. [E] |
| Reciprocal change to ST elevation elsewhere | The same injury current that elevates the ST segment in leads overlying the infarct is recorded as depression in the electrically opposite leads, purely as a consequence of viewing the same vector from the opposite direction. [E] |
| Digoxin effect ("digoxin scoop") | Digoxin's inhibition of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump alters the repolarisation phase of the action potential, producing a characteristic sagging, scooped ST depression that reflects drug effect, not ischaemia, and does not correlate with toxicity. [E] |
| Left ventricular hypertrophy ("strain pattern") | Prolonged, asynchronous repolarisation of the hypertrophied ventricular wall produces downsloping ST depression with T-wave inversion, classically in the leads with the tallest R waves (I, aVL, V5, V6). [E] |
| Hypokalaemia | Reduced extracellular potassium prolongs the repolarisation phase, which can manifest as ST depression together with T-wave flattening and U-wave prominence (below). [E] |
| ::: |  |

### Step 9 — T wave

**Method** [E] — assess amplitude, polarity, and symmetry; normally concordant with the QRS in most leads.

### Abnormality: T-wave inversion

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Non-ST-elevation ischaemia (NSTEMI/unstable angina) | Ischaemia delays and alters the normal sequence of repolarisation, and when subendocardial repolarisation is disproportionately affected, the normal repolarisation vector reverses direction, inverting the T wave in the leads overlying that territory. [E] |
| Evolving/resolving myocardial infarction | As an infarct evolves over hours to days, ST elevation resolves and deep, symmetrical T-wave inversion develops as the myocardium's repolarisation properties reorganise around the scar — the expected temporal evolution, useful for dating an infarct clinically. [E] |
| Wellens syndrome | Deep, symmetric T-wave inversion in V2–V3 reflects a critical, recently reperfused (often spontaneously) stenosis of the proximal LAD — a warning pattern of impending large anterior infarction if untreated, seen in a pain-free interval. [E] |
| Pulmonary embolism (right heart strain) | Acute right ventricular pressure overload alters right ventricular repolarisation, producing T-wave inversion classically in the right precordial and inferior leads (V1–V3, III) — part of the S1Q3T3 pattern family. [E] |
| Raised intracranial pressure / subarachnoid haemorrhage | Massive catecholamine surge from the CNS insult produces diffuse, often bizarre, deep T-wave inversion ("cerebral T waves") through direct myocardial catecholamine toxicity rather than coronary disease. [E] |
| Normal juvenile pattern / persistent juvenile T-wave pattern | A physiological variant in which T-wave inversion persists in the right precordial leads (V1–V3) beyond childhood without any pathological substrate. [E] |

### Abnormality: Tall, peaked T waves

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Hyperkalaemia (early) | Elevated extracellular potassium narrows the difference between resting and threshold potential and accelerates the terminal phase of repolarisation, producing tall, narrow, "tented" T waves — typically the earliest ECG sign of hyperkalaemia, preceding P-wave flattening and QRS widening as potassium rises further. [E] |
| Hyperacute phase of STEMI | In the earliest minutes of transmural ischaemia, before ST elevation fully develops, local hyperkalaemia-like ionic shifts and altered repolarisation produce tall, broad-based T waves — a transient early clue that precedes the classic ST-elevation pattern. [E] |

### Abnormality: Flattened T waves with prominent U waves

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Hypokalaemia | Reduced extracellular potassium prolongs the repolarisation phase of the action potential, flattening the T wave and unmasking or exaggerating the U wave (a small deflection reflecting late repolarisation of specific myocardial regions, normally overshadowed). [E] |

### Step 10 — QT interval

**Method** [E] — measured from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave, then corrected for heart rate (QTc), most commonly by Bazett's formula: QTc = QT / √(R-R interval in seconds). Normal QTc is broadly under 450 ms in men and 460 ms in women, though exact cut-offs are formula- and population-dependent and were not further sourced here. [?] for the precise numeric thresholds beyond this general range.

### Abnormality: Prolonged QT

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Congenital long QT syndrome | Inherited mutations in cardiac potassium or sodium channel genes slow the repolarisation current, prolonging the action potential duration and, on the surface ECG, the QT interval — creating a substrate for triggered activity (torsades de pointes). [E] |
| QT-prolonging drugs (many antiarrhythmics, some antipsychotics, some antibiotics) | Pharmacological blockade of the rapid delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) slows the terminal phase of repolarisation, prolonging the QT interval in a dose-dependent, largely predictable way. [E] |
| Hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia | Each alters the ionic currents underlying the repolarisation phase of the ventricular action potential, extending its duration and therefore the surface QT interval. [E] |
| Hypothermia | Slowed ion channel kinetics at low temperature prolong every phase of the action potential, including repolarisation. [E] |

### Abnormality: Shortened QT

| **Cause** | **Why** |
|---|---|
| Hypercalcaemia | Elevated extracellular calcium accelerates the plateau phase of the action potential, shortening its overall duration and therefore the QT interval. [E] |
| Digoxin effect | Enhanced potassium efflux from digoxin's effect on repolarisation shortens the action potential duration, in addition to producing the characteristic ST scooping described above. [E] |
| Congenital short QT syndrome | Gain-of-function mutations in cardiac potassium channels accelerate repolarisation, shortening the action potential and creating a substrate for re-entrant ventricular arrhythmia. [E] |

### Step 11 — Chamber hypertrophy criteria

**Left ventricular hypertrophy** [E]

- **Sokolow-Lyon criterion:** S wave in V1 + R wave in V5 or V6 > 35 mm.
- **Cornell criterion:** R wave in aVL + S wave in V3 > 28 mm (men) or > 20 mm (women).
- *Why these work:* a thicker LV free wall generates a larger depolarisation vector, increasing the amplitude of deflections recorded by leads that view that vector directly (V5/V6, aVL) and, via the reciprocal relationship, deepening the S wave in the electrically opposite lead (V1/V3).
- **Comparative accuracy** [E] — traditional criteria (Sokolow-Lyon, Cornell) have relatively low sensitivity against echocardiographic LVH as the reference standard; the newer Peguero-Lo Presti criterion has shown improved diagnostic performance in some comparative studies, though this remains an area of ongoing refinement rather than settled consensus. [L]
- **Associated "strain" repolarisation changes** (ST depression, T-wave inversion in the leads with tallest R waves) reflect the same disproportionate, asynchronous repolarisation described under Step 8.

**Right ventricular hypertrophy** [E]

- Dominant R wave in V1 (R > S), right axis deviation, and deep S waves persisting through the lateral precordial leads (V5, V6) — reported criteria include an S wave amplitude >10 mm in V5 and >3 mm in V6 [7].
- *Why:* an enlarged right ventricle generates a rightward, anterior vector that competes with and can exceed the normally dominant leftward LV vector, reversing the normal R/S progression across the precordium.

## Fourth: Expected Findings in Common Diseases

### Ischemic Heart Disease / Acute Coronary Syndromes

### Acute Anterior STEMI (LAD Occlusion):

  - Typical finding: ST-segment elevation ≥2 mm in V1-V4, often with hyperacute (tall, symmetric) T waves initially, followed by Q-wave development within hours. Reciprocal ST depression in inferior leads (II, III, aVF) may be present. The R waves in V1-V4 may be diminished or lost (Q waves).
  - Pattern recognition: Progressive ST elevation from V1 to V4 + reciprocal inferior ST depression. The most lethal STEMI territory due to large myocardial mass at risk. Conduction defects (new right bundle branch block, left anterior fascicular block) indicate proximal LAD occlusion and a large infarct.

### Acute Inferior STEMI (RCA or LCx Occlusion):

  - Typical finding: ST-segment elevation ≥1 mm in leads II, III, aVF. Reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL (highly specific). If ST elevation in III > II, and ST depression in aVL > I, the culprit is the right coronary artery (RCA). If ST elevation in II > III, the culprit is the left circumflex (LCx).
  - Pattern recognition: Inferior ST elevation + reciprocal aVL ST depression. A right-sided ECG (V4R) is mandatory: ST elevation ≥1 mm in V4R indicates right ventricular infarction, which is a contraindication to nitroglycerin (preload-dependent). Posterior extension: ST depression in V1-V3 with tall R waves (posterior leads V7-V9 show ST elevation).

### Wellens Syndrome (Critical Proximal LAD Stenosis, "Widowmaker"):

  - Typical finding: Deeply inverted or biphasic T waves in V2-V3 (Type A: biphasic T waves with initial positivity and terminal deep negativity; Type B: deeply, symmetrically inverted T waves). No significant ST elevation. The patient is often pain-free at the time of the ECG. Minimal or no troponin elevation.
  - Pattern recognition: T-wave inversion in V2-V3 in a patient with recent chest pain, with preserved R waves. This is the most dangerous ECG in emergency medicine because it looks "non-urgent" but represents critical LAD stenosis with a high risk of imminent anterior STEMI and death if not recognized. The patient requires urgent coronary angiography within 24 hours, not a stress test.

### De Winter T Waves (Proximal LAD Occlusion, STEMI Equivalent):

  - Typical finding: Up-sloping ST depression at the J point with tall, peaked, symmetric T waves in V2-V6. No ST elevation. This is a hyperacute STEMI equivalent indicating acute total LAD occlusion, requiring emergent reperfusion.
  - Pattern recognition: J-point ST depression + tall T waves + precordial leads + acute chest pain = activate cath lab.

### NSTEMI / Unstable Angina (Subendocardial Ischemia):

  - Typical finding: Horizontal or downsloping ST depression ≥0.5 mm in two contiguous leads. The leads with ST depression localize the region of ischemia. T waves may be inverted, flat, or normal. Q waves are absent (unless the patient has a prior MI).
  - Pattern recognition: Diffuse ST depression in many leads (V4-V6, I, II, aVL) with ST elevation in aVR >1 mm indicates left main or severe triple-vessel disease (global subendocardial ischemia).

### Arrhythmias and Conduction Disorders

### Atrial Fibrillation:

  - Typical finding: Absent P waves, chaotic fibrillatory baseline (fine or coarse), irregularly irregular R-R intervals. QRS is narrow unless underlying bundle branch block or rate-related aberrancy (Ashman phenomenon: a long R-R interval followed by a short R-R interval produces a wide, RBBB-morphology QRS because the right bundle has a longer refractory period).
  - Pattern recognition: Irregularly irregular + no P waves.

### Atrial Flutter:

  - Typical finding: Sawtooth flutter waves at 250–350 bpm, best seen in leads II, III, aVF. Classic 2:1 AV block produces a ventricular rate of 150 bpm. The flutter waves march through the QRS complexes. With 2:1 block, one flutter wave is hidden in the QRS, and one is hidden in the T wave; carotid sinus massage or adenosine slows AV conduction and reveals the flutter waves.
  - Pattern recognition: Narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 bpm: always suspect atrial flutter until proven otherwise.

### Ventricular Tachycardia (Monomorphic VT):

  - Typical finding: Wide QRS tachycardia (>120 ms) at 100–250 bpm, regular. The Brugada algorithm is the standard for distinguishing VT from SVT with aberrancy: (1) Absence of RS complex in all precordial leads? Yes → VT. (2) Longest R-S interval >100 ms in any precordial lead? Yes → VT. (3) AV dissociation? Yes → VT. (4) Morphology criteria for VT in V1-V2 and V6? If yes → VT. AV dissociation (P waves marching through QRS at a different rate) is the single most specific sign. Fusion beats and capture beats are pathognomonic for VT.
  - Pattern recognition: Wide QRS + regular + extreme axis deviation (northwest) + AV dissociation = VT until proven otherwise. In older patients with structural heart disease, >90% of wide-complex tachycardias are VT.

### Third-Degree (Complete) AV Block:

  - Typical finding: P waves and QRS complexes are completely dissociated; the P-P interval and R-R interval are regular but at different rates (atrial rate > ventricular rate). The escape rhythm is junctional (narrow QRS, rate 40–60) if the block is at the AV node, or ventricular (wide QRS, rate 20–40) if the block is infranodal.
  - Pattern recognition: P waves marching through the QRS at a faster rate + constant, regular ventricular escape rhythm.

### Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Pre-Excitation:

  - Typical finding: Short PR interval (<120 ms), wide QRS (>120 ms) with a slurred upstroke (delta wave) at the QRS onset, and secondary ST-T changes opposite to the delta wave and QRS vector. The delta wave represents early ventricular activation via the accessory pathway.
  - Pattern recognition: Short PR + delta wave + wide QRS. WPW type A (positive delta in V1-V2, tall R, mimics RVH, posterior MI). WPW type B (negative delta in V1-V2, mimics LBBB, anterior MI). Atrial fibrillation with WPW is a life-threatening emergency: the accessory pathway has a short refractory period, and rapid AF conduction can degenerate to ventricular fibrillation. The ECG shows an irregularly irregular, extremely rapid (>200 bpm), wide QRS tachycardia with varying QRS morphology. Do not give AV nodal blockers (adenosine, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin); they can accelerate accessory pathway conduction.

### Structural Heart Disease

### Left Ventricular Hypertrophy:

  - Typical finding: Voltage criteria (Sokolow-Lyon: S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 >35 mm; Cornell: R in aVL + S in V3 >28 mm in men, >20 mm in women). ST depression and T-wave inversion in the lateral leads with tall R waves (the "LV strain pattern," representing subendocardial ischemia from a thick ventricle). Left axis deviation. Left atrial enlargement (P mitrale). Poor R-wave progression.
  - Pattern recognition: High voltage + strain pattern + LAE + left axis.

### Acute Pericarditis:

  - Typical finding: Diffuse, concave-upward ST-segment elevation in most leads (I, II, III, aVF, V2-V6) with reciprocal ST depression only in aVR and sometimes V1. PR-segment depression (often the earliest finding, representing atrial injury). The ST elevation evolves through four stages: Stage I (diffuse ST elevation + PR depression), Stage II (ST segments normalize, T waves flatten), Stage III (T-wave inversion), Stage IV (normalization over weeks to months). Unlike STEMI, the ST elevation in pericarditis is concave upward, does not respect coronary territories, has no reciprocal changes except aVR, and Q waves do not develop.
  - Pattern recognition: Diffuse concave ST elevation + PR depression + no reciprocal changes (except aVR) + no Q waves = pericarditis. A friction rub on auscultation confirms.

### Pulmonary Embolism (Massive / Submassive):

  - Typical finding: Sinus tachycardia (most common finding). The classic S1Q3T3 pattern (S wave in lead I, Q wave and T-wave inversion in lead III) is present in only 15–25% of patients with PE. More commonly: T-wave inversion in V1-V4 (anterior precordial leads), incomplete or complete RBBB, right axis deviation, and evidence of right ventricular strain. The ECG may be entirely normal in up to 20% of PE cases.
  - Pattern recognition: The ECG's primary role in PE is to exclude acute STEMI (which PE can mimic) and to assess for right ventricular strain, which indicates a higher-risk PE. T-wave inversion in both anterior (V1-V4) and inferior (II, III, aVF) leads is highly specific for RV strain from submassive PE.

### Electrolyte and Metabolic Disorders

### Hyperkalemia:

  - Typical finding: A predictable progression, often correlating with serum K⁺: (1) Peaked, tall, narrow-based, symmetric T waves (best in V2-V4, K⁺ 5.5–6.5 mmol/L). (2) Loss of P waves (K⁺ 6.5–7.5): the atria are paralyzed, but sinus node continues to fire and conducts via specialized internodal tracts without atrial depolarization, producing a "sinoventricular rhythm" with a wide QRS. (3) Widening of the QRS (K⁺ 7.5–8.0). (4) Sine wave pattern (K⁺ >8.0–9.0): a sinusoidal, wide, undulating pattern that degenerates to ventricular fibrillation or asystole. This is a pre-terminal rhythm.
  - Pattern recognition: Peaked T waves > other causes of tall T waves (STEMI hyperacute T wave, normal variant) by symmetry, narrow base, and presence in all leads.

### Hypokalemia:

  - Typical finding: ST-segment depression, T-wave flattening or inversion, prominent U waves (U-wave amplitude > T-wave amplitude in the same lead is diagnostic of severe hypokalemia), prolonged QU interval (QT interval appears prolonged when the U wave is included, but true QTU prolongation is present). Ventricular ectopy, atrial tachycardia, and Torsades de Pointes may occur.
  - Pattern recognition: U wave > T wave + ST depression + PVCs.

### Hypocalcemia:

  - Typical finding: Prolonged QT interval, specifically the ST segment is lengthened (the T wave onset is delayed). The T wave itself is normal duration. Hypocalcemia prolongs phase 2 of the action potential (calcium influx), lengthening the ST segment without affecting the QRS or T-wave duration. Severe hypocalcemia can cause Torsades de Pointes.
  - Pattern recognition: Long ST segment with normal T wave + Chvostek or Trousseau sign.

### Hypercalcemia:

  - Typical finding: Shortened QT interval, specifically the ST segment is abbreviated or absent. The QRS immediately transitions to the T wave. The T wave is normal. Short QT is also seen in digoxin effect, hyperthermia, and congenital short QT syndrome.
  - Pattern recognition: Short QT with a normal QRS and T wave; compare to prior ECG.

## Fifth: Differential Diagnoses by ECG Pattern

The ECG is not interpreted by looking at a list of symptoms and generating a differential. Rather, the ECG is a pattern recognition test in which the abnormal finding is the "complaint." The clinician must look at the ECG and answer: "What is the rhythm? Is there ST elevation? Is the QRS wide or narrow? Is the QT long?" This section provides the differential diagnosis for each major ECG pattern.[1,2,10]

### 1. ST-Segment Elevation (What Are the Causes of ST Elevation on ECG?)

- **Acute STEMI (coronary occlusion):** Convex-upward ("tombstone") ST elevation in a coronary territory with reciprocal ST depression. Q waves evolve. The patient has acute chest pain. This is the first diagnosis that must be excluded. Time is myocardium.
- **Coronary Vasospasm (Prinzmetal Angina):** Transient, episodic ST elevation during rest, resolving with nitroglycerin or calcium channel blockers. Typically young, female, with no atherosclerotic risk factors. ST elevation is dramatic but resolves without Q waves if treated promptly.
- **Pericarditis:** Diffuse, concave-up ST elevation + PR depression + no reciprocal changes except aVR. The ST changes are widespread, not respecting coronary territories.
- **Benign Early Repolarization (BER):** Concave-up ST elevation, most prominent in V2-V5. J-point notching or slurring. No reciprocal ST depression. ST/T ratio in V6 <0.25. Most common in young, athletic, African-American males. Does not evolve; ST segments remain stable over time.
- **Left Ventricular Hypertrophy:** ST elevation in V1-V3 with deep S waves (discordant ST-T changes). Deep S waves + ST elevation in the right precordial leads is the LVH pattern.
- **Left Bundle Branch Block:** ST elevation discordant to the deep S waves in V1-V3. Using the original Sgarbossa criteria: Concordant ST elevation ≥1 mm in any lead is highly specific for acute MI (score 5). Discordant ST elevation ≥5 mm in any lead is a marker of increased specificity for MI (score 2). ST depression ≥1 mm in V1-V3 is concordant with the QRS and indicates acute MI (score 3). A total score ≥3 has >90% specificity for MI.
- **Hyperkalemia:** Peaked T waves with ST elevation in V1-V3 mimicking STEMI. The T wave is tall, peaked, narrow, and symmetric, seen in multiple leads. The history and serum potassium confirm.
- **Brugada Syndrome:** Type 1: Coved ST elevation ≥2 mm in V1-V2 (or V3), with a negative T wave. Type 2: Saddleback ST elevation ≥2 mm, with ST segment descending >1 mm before rising again, with a positive or biphasic T wave. The Brugada pattern may be intermittent and unmasked by fever, sodium channel blockers (flecainide, procainamide, ajmaline), cocaine, or tricyclic antidepressants. In the presence of syncope, aborted sudden cardiac death, or family history of sudden death, the type 1 pattern is diagnostic of Brugada syndrome.
- **Ventricular Aneurysm:** Persistent ST elevation weeks to months after an anterior MI (especially in V1-V4), without evolution. The ST elevation is due to the akinetic/dyskinetic aneurysm wall, not acute ischemia. An echocardiogram shows the aneurysm.
- **Acute CNS Event (Neurogenic Stunned Myocardium, "Cerebral T Waves"):** Deep, widespread T-wave inversion, often with prolonged QT, and sometimes ST elevation. Associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke. Catecholamine-mediated myocardial injury (Takotsubo cardiomyopathy).
- **Pulmonary Embolism (Rare):** ST elevation in III and V1, mimicking inferior and anterior STEMI, due to right ventricular strain. Normal coronary arteries on angiography. The clinical context of acute dyspnea and risk factors for PE distinguishes.
- **Hypothermia (Osborn Wave / J Wave):** A positive deflection at the J point, best seen in V3-V6. Can mimic ST elevation. Distinguish by the history and core temperature <32°C.
- **Post-DC Cardioversion:** Transient, marked ST elevation for 1–5 minutes after electrical cardioversion or defibrillation. No clinical significance; resolves spontaneously.

### 2. ST-Segment Depression

- **Myocardial Ischemia (NSTEMI, Unstable Angina):** Horizontal or downsloping ST depression ≥0.5 mm in two contiguous leads. The most common cause in the emergency department. The extent and depth of ST depression correlate with the severity and extent of ischemia.
- **Reciprocal ST Depression in STEMI:** ST depression in leads contralateral to a STEMI (e.g., anterior ST depression in inferior STEMI). This is highly specific for acute transmural infarction and indicates a larger territory at risk.
- **Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (Strain Pattern):** Downsloping ST depression with T-wave inversion in leads with tall R waves (V5-V6, I, aVL). The ST-T vector is opposite to the QRS vector.
- **Digoxin Effect:** Scooped, sagging ST depression ("Salvador Dalí mustache," reverse tick sign) with flattened or biphasic T waves, most prominent in V5-V6. Shortened QT interval. This is a normal effect of therapeutic digoxin and does not indicate toxicity or ischemia. Digoxin toxicity manifests with arrhythmias (atrial tachycardia with block, bidirectional VT, ventricular ectopy).
- **Hypokalemia:** ST depression with prominent U waves and T-wave flattening.
- **Tachycardia:** Rate-related ST depression, especially in patients with subclinical coronary artery disease, resolves when rate slows.
- **Right Ventricular Hypertrophy:** ST depression and T-wave inversion in V1-V3 (right precordial leads) with right axis deviation and tall R waves in V1.

### 3. Wide QRS Complex (>120 ms)

### Bundle Branch Block:

  - LBBB: Wide, notched R wave in leads I, aVL, V5-V6; deep S wave in V1-V3. QRS >120 ms. Discordant ST-T changes (ST depression and T-wave inversion in the lateral leads). Left axis deviation or normal axis. LBBB precludes accurate interpretation of ST-segment changes for ischemia (Sgarbossa criteria are required).
  - RBBB: RSR' pattern in V1-V2 ("rabbit ears"), wide slurred S wave in I, aVL, V5-V6. QRS >120 ms. T-wave inversion in V1-V3 is normal (discordant). RBBB does not obscure STEMI diagnosis; ST elevation in the presence of RBBB is interpreted using standard STEMI criteria.
- **Ventricular Rhythm (VT, Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm, Paced Rhythm):** Wide QRS, AV dissociation, extreme axis deviation, fusion/capture beats. A pacemaker produces a pacing spike before each wide QRS. A biventricular pacemaker (CRT) produces a relatively narrow QRS with a dominant S wave in V1 and dominant R wave in V6, often with a qR pattern in V1.
- **Hyperkalemia:** Wide QRS with a slow, slurred upstroke, progressing to sine wave. No identifiable P waves.
- **Sodium Channel Blocker Toxicity (Tricyclic Antidepressant Overdose, Class IA/IC Antiarrhythmics, Cocaine):** Wide QRS, prominent R wave in aVR >3 mm, terminal right-axis deviation (S wave in I, R wave in aVR). Seizures, coma, and hypotension accompany TCA overdose.
- **Wolff-Parkinson-White Syndrome:** Wide QRS with a delta wave. The QRS is a fusion of normal AV nodal conduction and accessory pathway conduction.
- **Non-Specific Intraventricular Conduction Delay (IVCD):** Wide QRS >110 ms but morphology does not meet criteria for LBBB or RBBB. Common in elderly patients, cardiomyopathy, and severe left ventricular dysfunction.

### 4. Long QT Interval

- **Congenital Long QT Syndrome:** Types 1–3 (LQT1: broad, broad-based T waves; LQT2: bifid, low-amplitude T waves; LQT3: late-onset, peaked T waves with a long isoelectric ST segment). Family history of sudden death, syncope, and sensorineural deafness (Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome). QTc often >500 ms.
- **Electrolyte Disturbances:** Hypocalcemia (prolonged ST segment, normal T wave). Hypokalemia (prominent U wave merges with T wave, appears as prolonged QT, but is actually prolonged QTU). Hypomagnesemia (similar to hypokalemia; often coexists).
- **Drugs (Acquired Long QT, Most Common Cause):** Class IA (quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide) and III (sotalol, dofetilide, ibutilide, amiodarone) antiarrhythmics. Macrolides (erythromycin, clarithromycin, azithromycin), fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), antifungals (fluconazole, ketoconazole), antipsychotics (haloperidol, thioridazine, ziprasidone, quetiapine), antidepressants (citalopram, escitalopram, TCAs), antiemetics (ondansetron, droperidol, domperidone), methadone, ranolazine, and many others. Multiple drugs + electrolyte disturbances + bradycardia multiply the risk of Torsades de Pointes.
- **Myocardial Ischemia / Infarction:** QT prolongation in the leads with ST-T changes. A marker of electrical instability.
- **Bradycardia:** QT interval is rate-dependent; marked bradycardia (<40 bpm) can produce a prolonged QTc even if the absolute QT is normal.
- **Hypothermia:** Prolonged QTc with Osborn (J) waves.
- **CNS Catastrophe (Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, ICH):** Massive QT prolongation with deep T-wave inversion (cerebral T waves).

### 5. Tall R Waves in V1 (R/S Ratio >1)

- **Right Ventricular Hypertrophy:** Right axis deviation, right atrial enlargement, ST depression/T-wave inversion in V1-V3. Deep S wave in V5-V6.
- **Posterior (Posterobasal) Myocardial Infarction:** Tall R in V1-V2 (the reciprocal of a posterior Q wave), upright T waves in V1-V2, ST depression in V1-V2. Often accompanied by inferior STEMI (RCA or LCx occlusion). A posterior ECG (V7-V9) shows ST elevation and Q waves.
- **Wolff-Parkinson-White Type A:** Left-sided accessory pathway. Short PR, delta wave positive in V1, tall R wave in V1.
- **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy:** Prominent septal Q waves, deep T-wave inversions, tall R in V1-V2. LVH voltage elsewhere.
- **Dextrocardia:** Tall R in V1, but with global negativity in lead I (inverted P, QRS, T) and reversed R-wave progression across the precordial leads (R wave decreases from V1 to V6). True dextrocardia shows mirror-image precordial leads.
- **Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy:** Tall R wave in V1 with deep Q waves in I, aVL, V5-V6 (posterolateral fibrosis pattern). Sinus tachycardia.
- **Misplaced Leads (Normal Variant):** V1 and V2 placed too high can produce an R > S in V1.

### If the ECG is NORMAL but Clinical Suspicion Remains High:

- A normal ECG does not exclude ACS. Approximately 1–5% of patients with a normal ECG will have an NSTEMI (detected by elevated troponin), and up to 5% will have unstable angina. Serial ECGs at 15–30 minute intervals and high-sensitivity troponin are mandatory in patients with ongoing chest pain and a normal initial ECG. A completely normal ECG at presentation in a patient with acute chest pain reduces the likelihood of STEMI to near zero, and the likelihood of NSTEMI to <5%.
- A normal ECG does not exclude pulmonary embolism, pericarditis (Stage II or III may show only T-wave inversion), intermittent Brugada pattern (fever or drug challenge may be required to unmask), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, or intermittent long QT syndrome (the QTc may normalize between episodes).
- A normal ECG does not exclude left ventricular hypertrophy (low sensitivity), right ventricular hypertrophy, or significant coronary artery disease (CAD may be extensive yet produce a normal resting ECG if there is no prior MI and no ongoing ischemia).
- A normal QTc on a single ECG does not exclude long QT syndrome (concealed LQTS, up to 30% of carriers). If suspicion is high (syncope during exertion, family history of sudden death), perform a 12-lead ambulatory Holter monitor, an exercise stress test (paradoxical QT prolongation with exercise is characteristic of LQT1), and refer for genetic testing.

## Sixth: Factors Affecting the Result (Confounders)

### Physiological Factors:

- **Age:** Heart rate decreases slightly with age. PR interval and QRS duration increase slightly but remain within normal limits. QTc interval is age-dependent; it lengthens slightly with advancing age. T waves flatten with age. R-wave amplitude (voltage) decreases with age, especially in the precordial leads. The normal axis shifts leftward with age due to increased LV mass and changes in thoracic geometry. Isolated premature atrial and ventricular complexes increase in frequency with age and are benign in the absence of structural heart disease.[1]
- **Sex:** Women have a higher resting heart rate, a shorter PR interval, a shorter QRS duration, and a longer QTc (normal <460 ms vs. <440 ms in men). Women have lower QRS voltage, particularly in the precordial leads. ST-segment elevation in the right precordial leads (V1-V3) is less common in women; ≥1.5 mm is the upper limit of normal, while in men <40, up to 2 mm can be normal. J-point elevation is more common in young men. T waves are more often inverted in V2 in women (persistent juvenile T-wave pattern).[1,2]
- **Ethnicity:** African-Caribbean athletes have a significantly higher prevalence of benign ECG patterns: early repolarization (J-point elevation) with convex ST elevation, T-wave inversion in V1-V4 preceded by ST elevation, and increased QRS voltage meeting LVH criteria. These are normal variants in this population and do not represent hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or ischemia. The 2017 International Consensus Criteria for Athlete's ECG specifically address ethnicity to reduce false-positive diagnoses.[7]
- **Body Habitus:** Obesity depresses the diaphragm, shifting the heart horizontally and to the left, causing left axis deviation, low QRS voltage (due to increased distance from the heart to the chest wall and interposed adipose tissue), and poor R-wave progression. In patients with severe obesity, the ECG may show low voltage in all leads, falsely suggesting pericardial effusion. Emphysema / thin body habitus shifts the heart to a vertical position, causing right axis deviation, low voltage in limb leads, and a dominant R wave in V1 (mimicking RVH or posterior MI).
- **Respiration:** Deep inspiration shifts the heart to a vertical position, altering the QRS axis. QRS amplitude varies with respiration (respiratory alternans), which is a normal finding. T-wave amplitude varies slightly. Breath-holding during the recording minimizes these effects.
- **Pregnancy:** In the third trimester, the diaphragm is elevated, shifting the heart horizontally, causing left axis deviation, small Q waves in III and aVF, T-wave inversion in III, and mild ST depression in the inferior leads. These normal changes of pregnancy must not be misdiagnosed as inferior MI or ischemia.
- **Food Intake:** A heavy meal can cause a mild tachycardia, T-wave flattening, and ST depression (postprandial ST-T changes), especially in patients with underlying coronary artery disease or hiatal hernia. A fasting ECG is ideal for baseline comparison.
- **Circadian Rhythm:** QTc is longer during sleep and in the early morning hours, increasing the risk of Torsades de Pointes in susceptible individuals. Heart rate, PR interval, and QRS duration vary slightly with the sleep-wake cycle.
- **Athletic Training:** Highly trained endurance athletes commonly have sinus bradycardia (rates 30–50 bpm), sinus arrhythmia, first-degree AV block, Wenckebach (Mobitz I) second-degree AV block, increased QRS voltage meeting LVH criteria, early repolarization, and juvenile T-wave inversion in V1-V3. These are physiological adaptations to increased vagal tone and increased LV mass and must be distinguished from pathological findings (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, ARVC) using the 2017 International Consensus Criteria.[7]

### Pharmacological Factors:

### Heart Rate Altering Drugs:

  - Beta-blockers: Sinus bradycardia, prolonged PR interval, decreased QTc.
  - Calcium channel blockers (non-dihydropyridine, verapamil, diltiazem): Sinus bradycardia, prolonged PR interval.
  - Digoxin: At therapeutic levels, produces the "digoxin effect" (scooped ST depression, shortened QTc). At toxic levels, causes increased automaticity and decreased AV conduction: atrial tachycardia with block (classic), junctional tachycardia, bidirectional ventricular tachycardia (pathognomonic), ventricular ectopy.
  - Adenosine: Transient sinus bradycardia, high-grade AV block, or sinus arrest lasting a few seconds. Used diagnostically and therapeutically.
  - Atropine: Sinus tachycardia, shortened PR interval.
  - Sympathomimetics (epinephrine, dobutamine, dopamine, albuterol, cocaine, amphetamines): Sinus tachycardia, rarely atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopy.
- **Drugs That Prolong the QT Interval (Risk of Torsades de Pointes):** The list is extensive. The most commonly implicated are sotalol, dofetilide, ibutilide, amiodarone (though TdP is rare with amiodarone due to its multichannel blocking), haloperidol, methadone, azithromycin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, ondansetron, droperidol, and escitalopram. The CredibleMeds database is the authoritative resource for QT-prolonging drugs. The ECG must be obtained before and serially during initiation of high-risk drugs.
- **Drugs That Widen the QRS (Sodium Channel Blockers):** Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline), class IA antiarrhythmics (quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide), class IC antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone), cocaine, diphenhydramine (in overdose), carbamazepine (in overdose). The ECG in TCA overdose shows sinus tachycardia, QRS >100 ms (marker of toxicity, risk of seizure), prominent R wave in aVR >3 mm, and terminal right-axis deviation.
- **Drugs That Alter the ST Segment and T Wave:** Digoxin (scooped ST depression). Sotalol and amiodarone (T-wave flattening or inversion, U waves). Doxorubicin (chemotherapy-related cardiomyopathy, T-wave flattening).

### Electrolyte Shifts Induced by Drugs:

  - Hypokalemia: Loop and thiazide diuretics, amphotericin B, high-dose corticosteroids, insulin, beta-agonists.
  - Hyperkalemia: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, triamterene, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin.
  - Hypocalcemia: Bisphosphonates, cinacalcet, denosumab, furosemide.
  - Hypercalcemia: Thiazide diuretics, lithium, calcium and vitamin D supplements.

**Pathological Confounders (Conditions That Alter the ECG Independently of the Target Diagnosis):**

- **Left Bundle Branch Block:** Renders the ECG uninterpretable for acute STEMI without the Sgarbossa criteria. LBBB alone produces ST elevation in V1-V3 and ST depression in V5-V6 that is a direct consequence of the abnormal depolarization, not ischemia. The modified Sgarbossa criteria (concordant ST elevation, excessively discordant ST elevation, concordant ST depression in V1-V3) must be applied. A new LBBB in a patient with acute chest pain should be treated as a STEMI equivalent only if the Sgarbossa score is ≥3 or there is hemodynamic instability. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline emphasizes that a new LBBB alone, without Sgarbossa criteria, is not an automatic STEMI equivalent.[8]
- **Right Bundle Branch Block:** Does not obscure STEMI diagnosis. The ST elevation of an acute anterior STEMI is visible in V1-V3 regardless of RBBB. The T-wave inversion in V1-V3 seen in RBBB is normal and should not be mistaken for ischemia.
- **Paced Rhythm (Ventricular Pacing):** A pacemaker produces a pacing spike followed by a wide QRS (usually LBBB morphology because the RV is paced). ST-T changes are discordant to the QRS vector. The Sgarbossa criteria apply to paced rhythms as they do to LBBB. A ventricular-paced rhythm obscures the diagnosis of acute MI but does not exclude it.
- **Hyperventilation:** Can cause ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion, mimicking ischemia. These changes resolve with normalization of breathing.
- **Pancreatitis:** Can produce ST elevation and T-wave inversion, likely due to circulating myocardial depressant factors or concurrent electrolyte disturbances. The clinical context of epigastric pain radiating to the back, elevated lipase, and absence of coronary risk factors distinguishes.
- **Severe Anemia:** Sinus tachycardia, low QRS voltage, ST depression, and T-wave flattening. The ECG mimics global subendocardial ischemia.
- **Hypothermia:** Osborn (J) waves, sinus bradycardia, prolonged PR, QRS, and QTc intervals, shivering artifact, and terminal atrial fibrillation degenerating to ventricular fibrillation.

### Analytical and Technical Confounders:

- **Lead misplacement (limb and precordial):** Discussed extensively above. The most common preventable error. Limb lead reversal must be recognized before interpreting axis, P-wave morphology, and ST elevation. Precordial leads placed in the incorrect intercostal space alter R-wave progression and ST-segment amplitude.
- **Inadequate skin preparation:** High skin impedance from oils, hair, or dry stratum corneum causes low-voltage QRS, 60-Hz interference, and baseline wander. The tracing is non-diagnostic.
- **Muscle tremor artifact (Parkinson disease, shivering, patient movement):** Can mimic atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or VT. The artifact is irregular, without the cardiac rhythmicity. The sine qua non is that QRS complexes can be seen marching through the artifact at a regular rate.
- **Broken patient cable or loose electrode:** A lead disconnected from the skin produces a straight flat line (asystole appearance) in that lead, with loss of all cardiac signal.
- **Incorrect paper speed or calibration:** Misinterprets rate, intervals, and voltage. Always check the printed calibration mark.
- **Electromagnetic interference (EMI):** Radiofrequency ablation equipment, MRI, and poorly grounded electrical devices in the ICU can produce noise that distorts the ECG. The ECG during electrocautery is uninterpretable.
- **Reversed arm and leg electrodes:** RA/LL reversal: lead II is flat; lead III and aVF are identical; lead I and aVL are identical. This produces a bizarre "normal" axis pattern.

## Seventh: Follow-Up & Next-Step Investigations

### When the ECG Is Normal but Clinical Suspicion Is High:

- **Acute Chest Pain with Normal ECG:** Repeat the ECG every 15–30 minutes for the first 1–2 hours, or continuously monitor with serial 12-lead ECGs. If the pain is ongoing, obtain a posterior ECG (leads V7-V9) to exclude isolated posterior STEMI and a right-sided ECG (V4R) to exclude right ventricular STEMI. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) at 0 and 2 hours (0h/2h algorithm) or 0 and 1 hour (0h/1h algorithm) per the 2025 AHA/ACC Guideline.[8] If hs-cTn is normal and ECG remains normal, the patient has a >99% negative predictive value for 30-day major adverse cardiac events and can be discharged.
- **Syncope with Normal ECG:** The ECG rules out acute MI, complete heart block, Brugada, WPW, long QT, and ARVC as immediate causes. If the history strongly suggests arrhythmogenic syncope (syncope during exertion, preceded by palpitations, family history of sudden death), order a 24–48-hour Holter monitor, an event recorder, or an implantable loop recorder (for infrequent events). An echocardiogram is indicated to rule out structural heart disease (aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, ARVC). An exercise stress test is indicated if the syncope is exertional.
- **Palpitations with Normal ECG:** Order a 24–48-hour Holter monitor if symptoms are daily, or a 14–30-day external event monitor or mobile cardiac telemetry if symptoms are less frequent. An echocardiogram is indicated to rule out structural heart disease. If the palpitations are associated with severe symptoms (syncope, presyncope), consider an implantable loop recorder.

### What to Order Next If the ECG Is Abnormal but Non-Specific:

- **Isolated ST-T Changes (ST Depression, T-Wave Inversion):** Compare to the most recent prior ECG. If the changes are new, the differential diagnosis is myocardial ischemia, LVH strain, electrolyte disturbance, or drug effect. Obtain serial troponin, echocardiogram (to assess LV function, wall motion abnormalities, and LVH), and serum potassium and calcium. If troponin is negative and ECG changes are persistent, perform a stress test (exercise ECG, stress echocardiography, myocardial perfusion imaging) or coronary CT angiography to evaluate for obstructive coronary artery disease.
- **Low QRS Voltage (<5 mm in limb leads, <10 mm in precordial leads):** The differential is obesity, COPD/emphysema, pericardial effusion, constrictive pericarditis, infiltrative cardiomyopathy (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis), hypothyroidism (myxedema), and severe myocarditis. Order an echocardiogram. If the echo shows a pericardial effusion, consider cardiac tamponade if the patient has dyspnea, tachycardia, and hypotension (Beck's triad: distant heart sounds, elevated JVP, hypotension). Electrical alternans (beat-to-beat variation in QRS amplitude) in the presence of a large pericardial effusion is pathognomonic for cardiac tamponade. If echo shows thick, sparkling myocardium with low voltage, suspect cardiac amyloidosis; proceed with serum/urine protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, and cardiac MRI.
- **Left Axis Deviation (New):** If new compared to a prior ECG, rule out left anterior fascicular block (most common cause) and inferior MI (look for Q waves in II, III, aVF). Echocardiogram to assess for structural heart disease.
- **Prolonged QTc (Incidental Finding):** Review the medication list for QT-prolonging drugs. Check serum potassium, calcium, and magnesium. If QTc >500 ms, discontinue all non-essential QT-prolonging drugs and admit for telemetry monitoring. If QTc is persistently prolonged and no reversible cause is found, consider congenital long QT syndrome. A resting ECG of first-degree relatives, exercise stress test, and epinephrine challenge test may be diagnostic. Refer to cardiology and genetics.

### The Gold Standard Confirmatory Investigation for ECG Findings:

- For STEMI: Coronary angiography is the gold standard for confirming coronary artery occlusion. The ECG is the diagnostic trigger that activates the cath lab; angiography confirms the lesion and provides definitive therapy (PCI). The ECG has a false-positive rate of 10–15% for acute coronary occlusion.
- For arrhythmias: The 12-lead ECG is the gold standard for the diagnosis of the arrhythmia at the moment it is recorded. For paroxysmal arrhythmias, the gold standard is an event monitor, mobile cardiac telemetry, or an implantable loop recorder that captures the rhythm during a symptomatic episode. The implantable loop recorder has a diagnostic yield of 50–80% for unexplained syncope over 2–3 years.
- For ventricular hypertrophy: The gold standard is echocardiography (or cardiac MRI for precise LV mass measurement). The ECG has low sensitivity but high specificity. A diagnosis of LVH on ECG should be confirmed with echocardiography.
- For long QT syndrome: The gold standard is genetic testing. The Schwartz score combines ECG (QTc, T-wave morphology), clinical history (syncope, congenital deafness), and family history to estimate the probability of LQTS. A score ≥3.5 indicates high probability, and genetic testing is indicated.

### Role of Serial/Repeat Testing:

- Acute chest pain: Serial ECGs every 15–30 minutes during active pain, or if the initial ECG is non-diagnostic and pain persists. STEMI is a dynamic process; the initial ECG may be normal in 50% of patients who will eventually develop ST elevation.
- After coronary revascularization: An ECG is obtained immediately post-PCI and at hospital discharge to establish a new baseline. New ST changes post-PCI suggest stent thrombosis, distal embolization, or no-reflow.
- During antiarrhythmic drug loading: ECG before each dose to measure QRS and QTc. A >25% increase in QRS duration or QTc >500 ms mandates dose reduction or discontinuation.
- Post-cardiac arrest: Serial ECGs every 12–24 hours for 72 hours to detect evolving STEMI, which may be masked initially by post-resuscitation ST changes.

### How to Correlate with Other Investigations:

- Correlate the ECG with the **chest X-ray (CXR):** Cardiomegaly + LVH on ECG = hypertensive or valvular heart disease. Pulmonary edema on CXR + STEMI on ECG = acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema from pump failure. Wide mediastinum + inferior STEMI = consider aortic dissection extending into the RCA ostium.
- Correlate the ECG with **echocardiography:** Regional wall motion abnormality corresponding to ECG ST elevation or Q waves confirms myocardial infarction. LV wall thickness >13 mm + LVH by voltage criteria confirms hypertensive heart disease or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Pericardial effusion + low voltage + electrical alternans confirms cardiac tamponade.
- Correlate the ECG with **serum troponin:** Rising/falling troponin + ST elevation = STEMI. Rising/falling troponin + ST depression or T-wave inversion = NSTEMI. Normal troponin + ST elevation = pericarditis, early repolarization, LVH, LBBB, Brugada, hyperkalemia, or old ventricular aneurysm. Normal troponin + diffuse T-wave inversion = pericarditis (Stage III), intracranial hemorrhage, stress cardiomyopathy, or pulmonary embolism.
- Correlate the ECG with **serum electrolytes:** Peaked T waves + serum K⁺ 6.8 mmol/L = hyperkalemia (confirm and treat). U waves + serum K⁺ 2.6 mmol/L = hypokalemia. Prolonged ST segment + serum Ca²⁺ 6.5 mg/dL = hypocalcemia.

## Eighth: Key Guidelines & Evidence

### Authoritative Guidelines:

- The American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) Recommendations for the Standardization and Interpretation of the Electrocardiogram (2007–2009, Parts I–VI), published in Circulation and the Journal of the American College of Cardiology, are the definitive scientific statements on every aspect of ECG nomenclature, measurement, and interpretation criteria. These six documents form the basis of modern ECG teaching and are the gold standard reference for what constitutes a normal and abnormal ECG.[1,2,4]
- The 2025 AHA/ACC Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Coronary Syndromes provides the most current (2025) STEMI criteria, Sgarbossa modified criteria for LBBB and ventricular pacing, and the mandate for a 10-minute door-to-ECG time in patients with suspected ACS.[8]
- The 2017 International Consensus Criteria for Electrocardiographic Interpretation in Athletes, endorsed by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), AHA, and HRS, provides the definitive evidence-based criteria for distinguishing physiological (training-related) ECG changes from pathological findings that require further investigation to exclude cardiomyopathy or channelopathy. These criteria have reduced the false-positive rate of athlete screening from ~15% to <5%.[7]
- The ESC Guidelines for the management of patients with ventricular arrhythmias and the prevention of sudden cardiac death (2022) provide the diagnostic criteria and ECG features for Brugada syndrome, long QT syndrome, short QT syndrome, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, and early repolarization syndrome.[9]

### Landmark Trials and Studies That Established Clinical Utility:

- Einthoven W. "Le télécardiogramme." (1906). Einthoven's invention of the string galvanometer and his description of the P, QRS, and T waves, and the establishment of the Einthoven triangle (leads I, II, III) laid the foundation for clinical electrocardiography. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1924.
- Sgarbossa EB, Pinski SL, Barbagelata A, et al. "Electrocardiographic diagnosis of evolving acute myocardial infarction in the presence of left bundle-branch block." (1996). This landmark study published in the New England Journal of Medicine validated the Sgarbossa criteria for diagnosing acute MI in LBBB. The three criteria: (1) Concordant ST elevation ≥1 mm in any lead (score 5), (2) ST depression ≥1 mm in V1-V3 (score 3), (3) Discordant ST elevation ≥5 mm (score 2). A score ≥3 had a specificity of 90% and a PPV of 88%. The modified Sgarbossa criteria (Smith modification, 2012) improved sensitivity by replacing the 5 mm absolute discordance with a proportion (ST/S ratio >0.25), and this is incorporated in the 2025 guidelines.[8]
- Antzelevitch C, Brugada P, Borggrefe M, et al. "Brugada syndrome: report of the second consensus conference." (2005). This consensus statement published in Circulation defined the Type 1, 2, and 3 Brugada ECG patterns and established the diagnostic criteria: Type 1 (coved ST elevation ≥2 mm in ≥1 right precordial lead V1-V2) plus one of the following: documented ventricular fibrillation, self-terminating polymorphic VT, family history of sudden cardiac death <45 years, coved-type ECG in family members, electrophysiological inducibility, syncope, or nocturnal agonal respiration.
- Moss AJ, Schwartz PJ, Crampton RS, et al. "The long QT syndrome. Prospective longitudinal study of 328 families." (1991). This Circulation publication established the diagnostic criteria (Schwartz score) and demonstrated that QTc >470 ms is the single most powerful predictor of cardiac events in LQTS. The risk of syncope or sudden death increases exponentially with QTc >500 ms.

### Quality Standards:

- The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) document GP36-A (2008) specifies standards for ECG acquisition, including electrode placement, skin preparation, machine bandwidth and frequency response, and digital signal processing. The standard filter settings for diagnostic ECG are 0.05 Hz (high-pass) to 150 Hz (low-pass) for adults.
- The AHA/ACC/HRS Scientific Statement on ECG Standardization (Part 1) specifies that the standard frequency response of an ECG machine must accurately reproduce sinusoid signals from 0.05 to 150 Hz. The low-frequency cutoff eliminates baseline wander; the high-frequency cutoff eliminates muscle artifact and EMI without distorting the QRS or pacing spikes.
- The Joint Commission and the American Heart Association's Mission: Lifeline program mandates a door-to-ECG time of ≤10 minutes for all patients presenting with acute chest pain, and this is a core quality metric for emergency departments and hospitals.
