## First: Symptoms of the Cardiovascular System
### 1. Angina pectoris (ischaemic chest discomfort) [E]

- Definition: retrosternal pressure, tightness, heaviness, squeezing, or burning, provoked by exertion or emotion, relieved within minutes by rest or nitrates. Duration typically minutes, not seconds and not hours.
- Pathogenesis: myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply → subendocardial ischaemia (the subendocardium is the watershed zone, compressed during systole and perfused only in diastole) → anaerobic glycolysis, lactate accumulation, and release of adenosine, bradykinin, and potassium → stimulation of cardiac sympathetic afferent Aδ and C fibres → these travel with sympathetic nerves through the middle and inferior cervical and thoracic ganglia to dorsal horn segments C7–T4 → convergence on the same second-order neurons receiving somatic afferents from chest wall, arm, neck, and jaw → the cortex cannot localise the visceral origin and refers the sensation to the somatic dermatome. This convergence-projection mechanism is why the pain is diffuse (patient uses a palm or fist, not a fingertip) and why radiation is stereotyped.
- Radiation sites and their segmental basis: left arm and ulnar forearm (C8–T1), both arms, jaw and teeth (via cervical convergence), neck and throat, interscapular region, epigastrium (T5–T6 overlap, the origin of "indigestion" presentations of inferior MI).
- Grading: Canadian Cardiovascular Society class I–IV, by the amount of ordinary activity required to provoke it. [E]

### 2. Anginal equivalents [E]

- Definition: ischaemia presenting without pain — exertional dyspnoea, exertional fatigue, epigastric discomfort, nausea, diaphoresis, or sudden unexplained weakness.
- Pathogenesis: ischaemia raises left ventricular end-diastolic pressure before it generates pain, producing dyspnoea as the first symptom; and afferent nociceptive transmission may be blunted.
- Populations in which this dominates: diabetes (cardiac autonomic neuropathy damaging the afferent limb), the elderly, women, chronic kidney disease, post-transplant (denervated heart). Do not require pain before considering ischaemia in these groups. [E]

### 3. Silent (asymptomatic) myocardial ischaemia [E]

- Definition: objectively documented ischaemia — ECG, perfusion, or biomarker — with no perceived symptom at all.
- Pathogenesis: as above, plus higher endogenous opioid tone and shorter or less severe ischaemic episodes that do not reach the perceptual threshold.
- Clinical consequence: the first presentation may be heart failure, arrhythmia, or sudden death rather than pain.

### 4. Acute coronary syndrome pain [E]

- Definition: ischaemic-type discomfort that is new, prolonged (usually beyond 20 minutes), occurs at rest, or is escalating in frequency or severity, and is not abolished by rest or nitrates.
- Pathogenesis: plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombus → abrupt supply reduction rather than demand excess → sustained ischaemia, then necrosis. Accompanying autonomic symptoms (diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, pallor) reflect intense sympathetic discharge and, in inferior infarction, vagal afferent stimulation from the inferior wall (Bezold–Jarisch reflex) producing bradycardia and vomiting.
- The current US management framework is the 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline, which consolidated the previous separate STEMI and NSTEMI documents [3]. [G]

### 5. Pericardial pain [E]

- Definition: sharp, pleuritic retrosternal pain, worse on inspiration and when supine, relieved by sitting forward, often referred to the ridge of the trapezius.
- Pathogenesis: the visceral pericardium and most of the parietal pericardium are insensate; nociception arises from the adjacent parietal pleura and from the parietal pericardium innervated by the **phrenic nerve (C3–C5)**. Trapezius ridge referral is therefore explained by shared C3–C5 innervation with the trapezius, and is the single most discriminating feature of pericardial pain.
- Postural dependence: recumbency increases pericardial and pleural apposition and stretch; sitting forward reduces it.
- Governing document for the European approach is now the 2025 ESC guideline on myocarditis and pericarditis, which replaced the 2015 pericardial diseases guideline and is the first ESC document to cover both conditions together [5]. [G]

### 6. Aortic dissection pain [E]

- Definition: abrupt-onset, maximal-at-onset, tearing or ripping pain, anterior chest for type A, interscapular or back for type B, frequently migrating as the dissection propagates.
- Pathogenesis: intimal tear → blood enters the media under systemic pressure → adventitial stretch and stimulation of adventitial nociceptors and vasa vasorum → pain. Migration reflects the advancing dissection front. Branch-vessel occlusion adds regional pain (renal flank pain, mesenteric abdominal pain, limb pain).
- Discriminator from ACS: instantaneous maximal intensity and migration, versus the crescendo build of ischaemic pain.

### 7. Pleuritic chest pain of pulmonary vascular origin [E]

- Definition: sharp, localised, inspiration-dependent pain.
- Pathogenesis: distal pulmonary embolism causes infarction or haemorrhage abutting the parietal pleura → parietal pleural inflammation → somatic intercostal nociception (hence localisable, unlike visceral cardiac pain).
- Central massive embolism, by contrast, more often produces dyspnoea, syncope, or shock without pleuritic pain, because no pleural surface is involved.
- The current US framework is the 2026 AHA/ACC multisociety acute pulmonary embolism guideline [2]. [G]

### 8. Intermittent claudication [E]

- Definition: cramping, aching, or fatiguing muscular pain in a defined muscle group, reproducibly provoked by a reproducible walking distance, relieved by standing rest within a few minutes, and reproduced again at the same distance.
- Pathogenesis: fixed arterial stenosis limits the exercise-induced rise in flow → oxygen supply–demand mismatch in working muscle → anaerobic metabolism, lactate and adenosine accumulation, stimulation of group III and IV muscle afferents. Contributory mechanisms beyond stenosis alone: endothelial dysfunction, impaired microvascular recruitment, and an acquired ischaemic skeletal myopathy with mitochondrial dysfunction — this is why revascularisation does not always normalise walking distance.
- Localisation maps to the diseased segment: buttock and hip (aortoiliac), thigh (common femoral or aortoiliac), upper two-thirds of calf (superficial femoral), lower calf (popliteal), foot (tibial or peroneal).
- Relief by standing still, without needing to sit or flex the spine, is the discriminator from neurogenic claudication of lumbar canal stenosis.

### 9. Ischaemic rest pain [E]

- Definition: constant burning pain in the forefoot and toes, worse at night and when supine, relieved by dependency — patients sleep in a chair or hang the leg out of bed.
- Pathogenesis: perfusion pressure falls below that required for basal metabolic needs and for nutritive flow to somatic nerves → ischaemic neuritis. Recumbency removes the hydrostatic column that had been augmenting distal perfusion pressure, and sleep reduces sympathetic tone and heart rate; dependency restores gravitational assistance.
- This symptom defines chronic limb-threatening ischaemia and is a limb-loss red flag, not a chronic complaint to be reviewed electively.

### 10. Acute limb ischaemia pain [E]

- Definition: sudden severe limb pain with the classic six features — pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (perishing cold).
- Pathogenesis: abrupt arterial occlusion (embolic, thrombotic, or dissection-related) with no time for collateral development. The sequence of the six features is prognostically ordered: pain and pallor are early and reversible; paraesthesia signals sensory nerve ischaemia; paralysis signals motor nerve and muscle ischaemia and predicts non-salvageable limb.
- Time-critical: the therapeutic window is measured in hours.

### 11. Abdominal and back pain of vascular origin [E]

- Abdominal aortic aneurysm expansion or rupture: deep boring abdominal, flank, or back pain, sometimes with syncope. Pathogenesis — aneurysm wall stretch and adventitial nociception; rupture adds retroperitoneal haematoma and haemorrhagic shock.
- Chronic mesenteric ischaemia: post-prandial epigastric or central abdominal pain beginning 15–60 minutes after eating, food avoidance, and weight loss. Pathogenesis — the post-prandial splanchnic hyperaemic demand cannot be met across stenosed coeliac and mesenteric ostia. [E]
- Acute mesenteric ischaemia: pain grossly out of proportion to abdominal findings early on, because the visceral peritoneum is not yet involved; peritonism appears only after transmural infarction. This delay is the reason the diagnosis is missed.
- Renal infarction: acute flank pain with haematuria, from cardioembolism or dissection.
- Splenic infarction: left upper quadrant pain, often pleuritic and referred to the left shoulder.

### Group 2 — The dyspnoea family

### 12. Exertional dyspnoea [E]

- Definition: awareness of uncomfortable, disproportionate breathing effort for a given workload.
- Pathogenesis in cardiac disease: elevated left atrial and pulmonary capillary pressure → transudation into the interstitium → stimulation of juxtacapillary (J) receptors on unmyelinated vagal C fibres → increased respiratory drive and the sensation of air hunger; plus reduced lung compliance from interstitial fluid, increased work of breathing, respiratory muscle underperfusion in low output states, and ergoreflex activation from underperfused skeletal muscle.
- Graded by NYHA functional class I–IV. [E]

### 13. Orthopnoea [E]

- Definition: dyspnoea appearing within minutes of lying flat, relieved by sitting up; quantified by the number of pillows required.
- Pathogenesis: recumbency translocates roughly a substantial volume of blood from splanchnic and lower-limb reservoirs into the thorax → increased venous return and preload → in a ventricle already on the flat part of its Starling curve, this raises pulmonary capillary pressure rather than output → pulmonary congestion. Simultaneously, diaphragmatic elevation and loss of gravitational gradient reduce functional residual capacity.

### 14. Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea [E]

- Definition: sudden severe breathlessness waking the patient from sleep, typically 1–3 hours after retiring, forcing them to sit or stand at a window, resolving over 15–30 minutes and often with cough.
- Pathogenesis: nocturnal reabsorption of dependent interstitial oedema expands intravascular volume, adding to the postural redistribution of orthopnoea; sleep-related depression of the respiratory centre and reduced sympathetic support allow congestion to accumulate to a threshold before it triggers arousal; the delay before waking explains the characteristic time lag. Alveolar oedema then narrows airways and produces cough and wheeze.

### 15. Bendopnoea

- Definition: dyspnoea occurring within approximately 30 seconds of bending forward, for example to tie shoes. [E]
- Pathogenesis: bending increases intra-abdominal pressure and further augments preload in a ventricle with already elevated filling pressures.
- Evidence status: described in a single-centre cohort and subsequently studied in small series; associated with elevated filling pressures and worse outcome, but the diagnostic performance figures come from limited data and should not be quoted as established. [L]

### 16. Trepopnoea [E]

- Definition: dyspnoea in one lateral decubitus position but not the other.
- Pathogenesis: in unilateral lung disease or large unilateral effusion, lying on the diseased side worsens ventilation–perfusion matching; in severe cardiomegaly, left lateral decubitus can compress the left lung and, in some patients with mitral disease, worsens symptoms.

### 17. Platypnoea–orthodeoxia [E]

- Definition: dyspnoea and arterial desaturation on sitting or standing, relieved by lying down — the inverse of orthopnoea.
- Pathogenesis: an intracardiac shunt, usually a patent foramen ovale, with directional flow that increases in the upright posture (often after right atrial or aortic geometric change, pneumonectomy, or in hepatopulmonary syndrome) → upright posture increases right-to-left shunting of desaturated blood.
- Its value is that it is nearly specific for shunt physiology, so it redirects the whole diagnostic pathway.

### 18. Acute pulmonary oedema (dyspnoea at rest with frothy sputum) [E]

- Definition: severe breathlessness at rest with orthopnoea, agitation, diaphoresis, and pink frothy sputum.
- Pathogenesis: pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds the interstitial and then alveolar barrier capacity → alveolar flooding with plasma; the pink colour is from erythrocytes crossing the disrupted alveolar–capillary membrane, and the froth is surfactant-stabilised plasma agitated by airflow.

### Group 3 — Low-output and systemic hypoperfusion symptoms

### 19. Fatigue and exercise intolerance [E]

- Pathogenesis: inadequate cardiac output reserve; skeletal muscle hypoperfusion with a secondary myopathy (fibre-type shift, reduced oxidative enzymes, mitochondrial dysfunction); deconditioning; anaemia of chronic disease; iron deficiency, which is common in heart failure and independently reduces exercise capacity; and beta-blockade or over-diuresis as iatrogenic contributors.

### 20. Confusion, agitation, or reduced consciousness [E]

- Pathogenesis: cerebral hypoperfusion once mean arterial pressure falls below the cerebral autoregulatory range, which is right-shifted in chronic hypertension — so a hypertensive patient may become confused at a "normal" blood pressure. In the elderly this may be the sole presentation of MI, heart failure, or arrhythmia.

### 21. Oliguria [E]

- Pathogenesis: reduced renal perfusion pressure plus elevated renal venous pressure (congestive kidney injury) plus intense RAAS and sympathetic activation → afferent vasoconstriction and sodium avidity. Renal venous congestion is at least as important as low output in the cardiorenal syndrome, which is why decongestion can improve renal function.

### 22. Cold, mottled, sweaty extremities [E]

- Pathogenesis: sympathetically mediated cutaneous vasoconstriction to defend central pressure, redistributing flow away from skin — a clinical marker of the low-output "cold" haemodynamic profile.

### 23. Diaphoresis [E]

- Pathogenesis: sympathetic cholinergic activation of eccrine sweat glands during acute ischaemia, pulmonary oedema, or shock. Sudden profuse cold sweating with chest discomfort substantially raises the probability of a serious cardiac cause.

### 24. Angor animi (sense of impending death) and severe anxiety [E]

- Pathogenesis: not psychological in origin — massive catecholamine release and afferent visceral input during transmural ischaemia, dissection, or massive embolism. Reported spontaneously, it is a genuine red flag rather than a soft symptom.

### Group 4 — Arrhythmia and conduction symptoms

### 25. Palpitations [E]

- Definition: unpleasant awareness of the heartbeat. The history must extract: onset (abrupt versus gradual), offset, rate, regularity, duration, triggers, associated syncope, and family history of sudden death.
- Pathogenesis by pattern:
  - Isolated "thump" or "missed beat" — atrial or ventricular ectopy; the perceived thump is the post-extrasystolic beat with augmented stroke volume from increased diastolic filling, and the "missed beat" is the compensatory pause.
  - Rapid regular abrupt-onset and abrupt-offset — paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (re-entrant).
  - Rapid irregular — atrial fibrillation.
  - Gradual onset and offset, rate rising with activity or anxiety — sinus tachycardia; usually secondary, so search for the cause.
  - Rapid with syncope or family history of sudden death — ventricular tachycardia or inherited channelopathy; this combination is the highest-risk pattern.
- Current frameworks diverge: the ESC/EACTS 2024 atrial fibrillation guideline organises care around the AF-CARE structure, while the US operates from the 2023 ACC/AHA atrial fibrillation guideline [1,2]. Confirm which your unit follows. [G]

### 26. Neck pounding (frog sign as the patient's symptom) [E]

- Definition: sensation of rapid pulsation in the neck during tachycardia.
- Pathogenesis: atrial contraction against a closed tricuspid valve during AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia generates cannon A waves transmitted to the jugular veins. Its presence favours AVNRT over other supraventricular tachycardias.

### 27. Post-tachycardia polyuria [E]

- Pathogenesis: atrial stretch during sustained tachycardia releases atrial natriuretic peptide → natriuresis and diuresis after the episode terminates. A reported diuresis following a palpitation episode supports a genuine sustained tachyarrhythmia rather than anxiety.

### 28. Presyncope and dizziness [E]

- Definition: lightheadedness, greying of vision, and impending loss of consciousness without actual loss.
- Pathogenesis: transient reduction in cerebral perfusion insufficient in degree or duration to abolish consciousness. Must be distinguished from vertigo (illusion of movement, vestibular) and from disequilibrium.

### 29. Syncope [E]

- Definition: transient loss of consciousness due to global cerebral hypoperfusion, characterised by rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery.
- Pathogenesis by class:
  - Reflex (neurally mediated) — vasovagal, situational (micturition, cough, defecation, swallow), carotid sinus. Mechanism: inappropriate vasodilatation, bradycardia, or both, triggered via central autonomic pathways; typically has a prodrome of nausea, warmth, sweating, and visual dimming, and identifiable triggers.
  - Orthostatic hypotension — autonomic failure, volume depletion, or drugs; occurs on standing, within seconds to minutes.
  - Cardiac arrhythmic — bradyarrhythmia (sinus node disease, AV block) or tachyarrhythmia (VT, torsades, rapidly conducted AF in accessory pathway). Mechanism: abrupt loss of effective cardiac output. Characteristically no or minimal prodrome, occurs supine or during exertion, and may cause injury.
  - Cardiac structural or obstructive — severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with outflow obstruction, mitral stenosis, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, cardiac tamponade, myxoma or thrombus obstructing an orifice. Mechanism for exertional syncope in aortic stenosis: output is fixed while exercise-induced peripheral vasodilatation reduces systemic vascular resistance, so mean pressure falls; ventricular baroreceptor activation and exercise-induced arrhythmia contribute.
- Highest-risk historical features: syncope during exertion, syncope while supine, absent prodrome, syncope causing injury, palpitations immediately preceding, and family history of sudden death under 40. These distinguish cardiac from reflex syncope and change disposition. [E]

### Group 5 — Congestion symptoms

### 30. Peripheral (dependent) oedema [E]

- Definition: pitting swelling, gravity-dependent — ankles and pretibial region when ambulant, sacrum when bed-bound.
- Pathogenesis: elevated right atrial pressure raises systemic venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure → transudation exceeding lymphatic clearance capacity; compounded by neurohormonal sodium and water retention through RAAS, sympathetic activation, and vasopressin. Cardiac oedema is therefore bilateral and symmetrical; unilateral swelling indicates local venous or lymphatic disease, not heart failure.

### 31. Weight gain [E]

- Pathogenesis: fluid retention precedes clinically detectable oedema, because several litres can accumulate in the interstitium before pitting appears. This is why daily weights, not oedema examination, are the monitoring tool in heart failure.

### 32. Abdominal distension and ascites [E]

- Pathogenesis: elevated hepatic sinusoidal and peritoneal capillary pressure from systemic venous hypertension. Prominent in constrictive pericarditis, tricuspid disease, and right-dominant failure, where ascites can precede peripheral oedema.

### 33. Right upper quadrant pain or heaviness [E]

- Pathogenesis: acute hepatic venous congestion distends the liver and stretches Glisson's capsule, whose nociceptors produce dull constant pain. In acute right heart failure this can mimic biliary disease.

### 34. Anorexia, early satiety, nausea, and cardiac cachexia [E]

- Pathogenesis: bowel wall oedema and splanchnic congestion impair absorption and motility; ascites and hepatomegaly mechanically reduce gastric capacity; systemic inflammation with elevated tumour necrosis factor alpha and interleukin-6 drives catabolism; and anabolic-catabolic imbalance produces loss of both fat and lean mass. Cachexia is an independent adverse prognostic marker in heart failure. [E]

### 35. Nocturia [E]

- Pathogenesis: during the day, reduced renal perfusion and high sympathetic and RAAS tone retain sodium; on recumbency, interstitial fluid returns to the circulation, renal perfusion rises, and sympathetic tone falls, so the retained load is excreted at night. Nocturia is an early and frequently overlooked congestion symptom.

### 36. Cardiac cough [E]

- Definition: dry or minimally productive cough, worse on recumbency and exertion, often nocturnal.
- Pathogenesis: interstitial and peribronchial oedema stimulates J receptors and bronchial mucosal irritant receptors; peribronchial cuffing narrows small airways, producing "cardiac wheeze," which is misdiagnosed as asthma.
- Mandatory differential in a treated cardiac patient: ACE inhibitor cough, mediated by bradykinin and substance P accumulation, which is dry, non-postural, and resolves on switching to an angiotensin receptor blocker.

### 37. Haemoptysis [E]

- Pathogenesis differs by lesion and the pattern is diagnostically useful:
  - Mitral stenosis — rupture of dilated bronchial submucosal veins at bronchopulmonary venous anastomoses under high left atrial pressure, causing frank, sometimes brisk bleeding.
  - Acute pulmonary oedema — pink frothy sputum from alveolar transudate containing erythrocytes.
  - Pulmonary infarction — blood-streaked sputum from alveolar haemorrhage.
  - Chronic left heart failure — rusty sputum containing haemosiderin-laden macrophages from repeated microhaemorrhage.

### Group 6 — Peripheral vascular, venous, and lymphatic symptoms

### 38. Digital colour change (Raynaud phenomenon) [E]

- Definition: episodic, demarcated pallor then cyanosis then reactive hyperaemia of digits on cold or emotional stress.
- Pathogenesis: exaggerated digital arteriolar vasospasm; in secondary Raynaud, superimposed structural microvascular disease from connective tissue disease. Digital ulceration or asymmetry indicates secondary disease and warrants investigation.

### 39. Digital ulceration, non-healing wounds, and gangrene [E]

- Pathogenesis: perfusion inadequate for the metabolic cost of tissue repair; minor trauma initiates a wound that cannot heal. Arterial ulcers are distal, punched-out, painful, with a pale dry base; venous ulcers are at the medial malleolus, shallow, exudative, and less painful — the distinction is made on site and character.

### 40. Venous symptoms — aching, heaviness, itching, restless legs [E]

- Pathogenesis: ambulatory venous hypertension from valvular incompetence or obstruction → capillary hypertension, interstitial oedema, red cell extravasation with haemosiderin deposition, and chronic inflammation producing lipodermatosclerosis. Symptoms worsen through the day with standing and improve on elevation, the reverse of arterial rest pain.

### 41. Unilateral limb swelling and pain (deep vein thrombosis) [E]

- Pathogenesis: venous outflow obstruction raises distal capillary pressure and produces oedema; inflammation of the vein wall causes pain and tenderness. Unilaterality is the discriminator from cardiac oedema.

### 42. Limb heaviness with non-pitting swelling (lymphoedema) [E]

- Pathogenesis: lymphatic transport failure with accumulation of protein-rich interstitial fluid → fibrosis and adipose deposition, hence non-pitting and involving the dorsum of the foot and toes.

### 43. Erectile dysfunction

- Pathogenesis: endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis of the internal pudendal and cavernosal arteries; the small calibre of these vessels means flow limitation appears before symptomatic coronary disease. [E]
- It functions as a sentinel symptom of generalised atherosclerosis and should prompt cardiovascular risk assessment. The commonly quoted lead-time before coronary events derives from limited observational data — no reliable pooled figure located; do not quote a number. [L]

### Group 7 — Embolic and cerebrovascular symptoms

### 44. Focal neurological deficit or transient ischaemic attack [E]

- Pathogenesis: cardioembolism from atrial fibrillation, left ventricular thrombus, endocarditic vegetation, prosthetic valve, atrial myxoma, or paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale; or artery-to-artery embolism from carotid plaque.
- A first stroke may be the presenting symptom of previously silent atrial fibrillation, which is the rationale for rhythm monitoring after cryptogenic stroke.

### 45. Amaurosis fugax [E]

- Definition: painless transient monocular visual loss, often described as a descending curtain, lasting minutes with full recovery.
- Pathogenesis: transient retinal arterial occlusion, usually by embolic material from an ipsilateral carotid plaque or the heart.

### Group 8 — Compressive and mechanical symptoms

### 46. Hoarseness (cardiovocal or Ortner syndrome) [E]

- Pathogenesis: compression of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve as it loops under the aortic arch, by a massively dilated left atrium, dilated pulmonary artery in pulmonary hypertension, or thoracic aortic aneurysm → left vocal cord palsy.

### 47. Dysphagia (dysphagia megalatriensis) [E]

- Pathogenesis: an enlarged left atrium or aortic aneurysm compresses the oesophagus posteriorly. Rare, but when present in a patient with a diastolic murmur it points strongly to severe mitral stenosis.

### 48. Sensation of neck or head pulsation [E]

- Pathogenesis: in severe aortic regurgitation, a wide pulse pressure produces perceived head pounding and visible carotid pulsation; in severe tricuspid regurgitation, giant systolic v waves produce pulsatile neck fullness and sometimes pulsatile hepatic discomfort.

### 49. Hiccup, cough, or dyspnoea from pericardial or mediastinal mass effect [L]

- Pathogenesis: large pericardial effusion or cardiac mass compressing adjacent structures — bronchus, oesophagus, phrenic nerve. Reported in case series only; do not treat as a reliable diagnostic pointer.

### Group 9 — Systemic and constitutional symptoms of cardiovascular disease

### 50. Fever, night sweats, weight loss, and malaise [E]

- Pathogenesis: infective endocarditis (persistent bacteraemia and immune activation), acute rheumatic fever, myocarditis, and pericarditis. Any fever with a new murmur, or fever in a patient with a prosthetic valve or indwelling cardiac device, must be treated as possible endocarditis until excluded.

### 51. Arthralgia, rash, and other immune features [E]

- Pathogenesis: post-streptococcal immune cross-reactivity in acute rheumatic fever; immune complex phenomena in endocarditis. These extracardiac symptoms are often what brings the patient in, with carditis found on examination.

### 52. Cyanosis [E]

- Central cyanosis — bluish discoloration of tongue, lips, and mucosae; mechanism: arterial desaturation from intracardiac or intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting, or severe pulmonary oedema impairing gas exchange.
- Peripheral cyanosis — extremities only, with warm tongue; mechanism: increased peripheral oxygen extraction due to sluggish flow in low-output states or vasoconstriction, with normal arterial saturation.
- Differential cyanosis (lower limbs cyanotic, upper limbs pink) indicates a right-to-left shunt distal to the left subclavian artery, as in patent ductus arteriosus with pulmonary hypertension.

### Group 10 — Paediatric and congenital presentations

### 53. Poor feeding, sweating with feeds, and failure to thrive [E]

- Pathogenesis: feeding is the infant's principal exertion; a limited cardiac output reserve means feeding provokes tachypnoea, sympathetic activation with sweating, and early fatigue, so intake falls while metabolic demand is elevated. This is the infant equivalent of exertional dyspnoea.

### 54. Squatting and hypoxic (cyanotic) spells [E]

- Pathogenesis in tetralogy of Fallot: squatting kinks the femoral arteries and raises systemic vascular resistance, which reduces the right-to-left shunt across the ventricular septal defect and increases pulmonary blood flow, improving saturation. Hypoxic spells arise from dynamic infundibular spasm with abrupt increase in shunting.

### 55. Exertional syncope or chest pain in a young person [E]

- Pathogenesis: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with outflow obstruction or arrhythmia, anomalous coronary artery origin, congenital aortic stenosis, or inherited channelopathy. In an adolescent or young adult these symptoms are never to be attributed to deconditioning or anxiety before cardiac exclusion, because the first alternative presentation may be sudden death.

**Escalation note (Rule B11):** several symptoms in this inventory are decision-forcing rather than descriptive — exertional or supine syncope, syncope with a family history of sudden death, rest pain, the six features of acute limb ischaemia, migratory tearing chest pain, and fever with a new murmur. ## Second: Signs of the Cardiovascular System
### 1. Cardiac cachexia [E]

- Definition: loss of both fat and lean mass, with temporal wasting, prominent clavicles, and loss of interosseous muscle bulk, in a patient with chronic heart disease.
- Mechanism: neurohormonal and inflammatory catabolism (TNF-α, IL-6), anorexia from gut oedema and hepatomegaly, malabsorption from splanchnic congestion, and increased respiratory work. An independent adverse prognostic marker in heart failure.

### 2. Central cyanosis [E]

- Definition: bluish discoloration of the **tongue, lips, and oral mucosa**, implying arterial desaturation.
- Mechanism: sufficient absolute deoxyhaemoglobin in the arterial blood to alter its colour — arising from intracardiac or intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting, or from severe impairment of alveolar gas exchange as in pulmonary oedema. Because it depends on the *absolute* amount of deoxyhaemoglobin, it appears earlier in polycythaemia and may be absent in severe anaemia despite profound desaturation.

### 3. Peripheral cyanosis [E]

- Definition: bluish discoloration of the extremities with a **pink tongue**.
- Mechanism: increased peripheral oxygen extraction due to sluggish capillary transit — low cardiac output, cold-induced vasoconstriction, or local arterial disease — with normal arterial saturation.

### 4. Differential cyanosis [E]

- Definition: lower limbs cyanosed and clubbed, upper limbs pink.
- Mechanism: right-to-left shunting distal to the left subclavian origin — classically a patent ductus arteriosus with pulmonary hypertension, so that desaturated pulmonary arterial blood enters the descending aorta only.
- **Reverse differential cyanosis** (upper limbs blue, lower pink) indicates transposition of the great arteries with a duct, and is nearly specific.

### 5. Pallor [E]

- Mechanism: anaemia (of chronic disease, iron deficiency, haemolysis on a prosthetic valve, or gastrointestinal loss on antithrombotics), or intense sympathetic cutaneous vasoconstriction in shock. The distinction matters: anaemic pallor is warm, shock pallor is cold and clammy.

### 6. Jaundice [E]

- Mechanism in cardiac disease: chronic hepatic congestion ("cardiac cirrhosis") with hepatocellular dysfunction; or haemolysis from mechanical erythrocyte destruction by a prosthetic valve or a severely stenotic native valve.

### 7. Tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, and inability to complete sentences [E]

- Mechanism: increased respiratory drive from J-receptor stimulation by interstitial oedema, reduced lung compliance, and metabolic acidosis in shock.

### 8. Orthopnoeic posture [E]

- Definition: the patient sits bolt upright or leans forward; refuses to lie flat.
- Mechanism: as for orthopnoea (Part 1, item 13) — upright posture reduces thoracic blood volume and increases functional residual capacity.

### 9. Diaphoresis with cool skin [E]

- Mechanism: sympathetic cholinergic activation of eccrine glands with simultaneous adrenergic cutaneous vasoconstriction. The combination — sweating **and** cold — is the signature of acute ischaemia, pulmonary oedema, or shock, and distinguishes it from the warm sweating of fever or thyrotoxicosis.

### 10. Restlessness, agitation, confusion, or drowsiness [E]

- Mechanism: cerebral hypoperfusion below the autoregulatory threshold. In chronic hypertension the autoregulatory curve is right-shifted, so confusion may appear at a "normal" blood pressure.

### 11. Malar flush (mitral facies) [E]

- Definition: dusky pink-purple discoloration over the cheekbones.
- Mechanism: chronic low cardiac output with pulmonary hypertension causing peripheral vasoconstriction and cutaneous venous stasis with dilated malar venules. Classically described in severe mitral stenosis.

### 12. Peripheral, sacral, and generalised oedema [E]

- Definition: pitting swelling, gravity-dependent — pretibial and ankle in the ambulant patient, **sacral in the bed-bound patient** (which is why you must roll the patient), scrotal or vulval when severe, and anasarca in extreme cases.
- Mechanism: elevated systemic venous and therefore capillary hydrostatic pressure from raised right atrial pressure, compounded by RAAS-driven sodium and water retention and by hypoalbuminaemia in cardiac cachexia. Cardiac oedema is **bilateral and symmetrical**; asymmetry redirects you to local venous or lymphatic disease.

### 13. Body habitus and syndromic clues [E]

- **Marfanoid habitus** — tall, long limbs, arm span exceeding height, arachnodactyly, high-arched palate, pectus deformity, scoliosis, blue sclerae, lens dislocation. Mechanism: fibrillin-1 deficiency degrading elastic fibre integrity, producing aortic root dilatation, aortic regurgitation, dissection, and mitral valve prolapse.
- **Turner syndrome** — short stature, webbed neck, widely spaced nipples, cubitus valgus. Association: bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, aortic dilatation.
- **Down syndrome** — association: atrioventricular septal defect, VSD, ASD.
- **Williams syndrome** — elfin facies. Association: supravalvular aortic stenosis (elastin gene deletion).
- **Noonan syndrome** — association: pulmonary stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- **Ankylosing spondylitis posture** — question-mark spine, loss of lumbar lordosis. Association: aortic root dilatation with aortic regurgitation, and conduction block.
- **Kyphoscoliosis or severe pectus excavatum** — can cause cor pulmonale through restrictive lung disease, and displaces the apex, mimicking cardiomegaly.

### 14. Lipid deposition signs [E]

- **Xanthelasma palpebrarum** — soft yellow plaques on the medial eyelids. Mechanism: dermal macrophage lipid accumulation. Present in hyperlipidaemia but also in normolipidaemic individuals, so it is suggestive, not diagnostic.
- **Corneal arcus** — grey-white ring at the corneal periphery. Mechanism: lipid deposition in the peripheral corneal stroma. **Age-dependent significance**: physiological over 50, but under 40 it points to familial hypercholesterolaemia.
- **Tendon xanthomata** — firm nodules on the Achilles tendon and extensor tendons of the hands. Mechanism: lipid-laden macrophages within tendon. **Close to specific for familial hypercholesterolaemia** and therefore high-yield.
- **Eruptive xanthomata** — crops of yellow papules on buttocks and extensor surfaces. Mechanism: severe hypertriglyceridaemia.
- **Palmar crease xanthomata** — orange-yellow discoloration of palmar creases. Mechanism: remnant particle accumulation; associated with type III dysbetalipoproteinaemia.

### 15. Iatrogenic and treatment-related signs [E]

- **Cushingoid facies, striae, thin skin, bruising** — glucocorticoid exposure.
- **Gingival hyperplasia** — dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (classically nifedipine) and ciclosporin.
- **Gynaecomastia** — spironolactone (androgen receptor antagonism), digoxin (oestrogen-like effect).
- **Bruising and purpura** — antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy.
- **Ankle oedema without raised JVP** — dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker; mechanism is precapillary arteriolar dilatation raising capillary pressure without any rise in venous pressure. **Distinguishing this from heart failure oedema prevents inappropriate diuresis.**
- **Gouty tophi** — diuretic-induced hyperuricaemia.

### 16. Scars and devices — inspect for these before touching the patient [E]

- **Median sternotomy scar** — CABG, valve surgery, aortic surgery, congenital repair.
- **Saphenous vein harvest scar (leg) or radial harvest scar (forearm)** — confirms CABG rather than valve surgery, when the sternotomy is ambiguous.
- **Left lateral thoracotomy scar** — coarctation repair, patent ductus ligation, older mitral valvotomy.
- **Infraclavicular device box** — pacemaker, ICD, or cardiac resynchronisation device. Note laterality and whether the patient has a device identity card.
- **Arteriovenous fistula** — dialysis access, implying CKD and its cardiovascular consequences.
- **Groin puncture scars or bruising** — recent catheterisation.

### Group 2 — Hands and arms

### 17. Clubbing [E]

- Definition: loss of the normal nail-fold angle (Lovibond angle exceeding 180°), increased nail-bed fluctuance, increased longitudinal and transverse nail curvature, and drumstick terminal phalanges. Schamroth's window (the diamond gap between opposed nails) is obliterated.
- Mechanism: incompletely established. The prevailing account is that megakaryocytes and platelet clumps bypass the pulmonary capillary filter through a right-to-left shunt or diseased lung, reach the digital vasculature, and release **platelet-derived growth factor and VEGF**, driving vascular proliferation and connective tissue hypertrophy [L].
- Cardiovascular causes: **cyanotic congenital heart disease**, **infective endocarditis** (subacute, over weeks to months), and atrial myxoma.
- **Differential clubbing** (toes only) — patent ductus arteriosus with pulmonary hypertension, as in item 4.

### 18. Splinter haemorrhages [E]

- Definition: linear, longitudinal, reddish-brown streaks under the nail, aligned with the nail bed capillaries.
- Mechanism: microembolic or immune-complex-mediated occlusion of nail-bed capillaries with extravasation. Longitudinal orientation reflects the longitudinal course of those capillaries.
- Caveat: trauma is by far the commonest cause, especially in manual workers, and traumatic splinters are distal. Endocarditic splinters tend to be proximal.

### 19. Osler nodes [E]

- Definition: **tender**, erythematous or violaceous nodules on the finger and toe pads.
- Mechanism: immune-complex deposition with a sterile leucocytoclastic vasculitis of dermal arterioles; the tenderness reflects the inflammatory component and the rich innervation of the digital pads.
- **Mnemonic anchor: Osler = Ouch (tender) = immune.**

### 20. Janeway lesions [E]

- Definition: **non-tender**, flat, erythematous or haemorrhagic macules on the palms and soles.
- Mechanism: septic microembolic occlusion with microabscess formation and haemorrhage — an embolic rather than immune phenomenon, hence painless.
- **Mnemonic anchor: Janeway = Jump (embolic) = painless.**

### 21. Tar (nicotine) staining [E]

- Mechanism: direct pigment deposition on keratin. Not a cardiac sign in itself, but it establishes the dominant modifiable atherosclerotic risk factor at a glance.

### 22. Peripheral temperature gradient and delayed capillary refill [E]

- Definition: a palpable transition from warm to cool along the limb; capillary refill prolonged beyond roughly two seconds after nail-bed compression.
- Mechanism: sympathetic cutaneous vasoconstriction diverting flow centrally in low-output states, or fixed arterial obstruction. Together with mentation and urine output, this is the bedside triad for the "cold" haemodynamic profile.

### 23. Quincke sign [E]

- Definition: visible pulsatile blanching and reddening of the nail bed on gentle transillumination or light pressure.
- Mechanism: a widened pulse pressure transmitted to the capillary bed. A sign of significant aortic regurgitation, and one of a family of AR signs all arising from the same single mechanism.

### 24. Arachnodactyly with wrist and thumb signs [E]

- **Walker–Murdoch (wrist) sign**: the thumb and fifth finger overlap when encircling the contralateral wrist.
- **Steinberg (thumb) sign**: the adducted thumb projects beyond the ulnar border of the hand.
- Mechanism: fibrillin-1 deficiency with disproportionate long-bone growth and ligamentous laxity. Points to Marfan syndrome and therefore to aortic root pathology.

### 25. Fine tremor [E]

- Mechanism: β₂-adrenergic stimulation of skeletal muscle — inhaled or systemic β-agonist therapy, or thyrotoxicosis, which is itself a cause of atrial fibrillation and high-output heart failure.

### Pulse signs

### 26. Rate [E]

- Tachycardia above 100/min, bradycardia below 60/min at rest.
- Mechanism: net balance of sympathetic and parasympathetic input on sinoatrial node automaticity, or replacement of sinus rhythm by an ectopic or re-entrant focus, or block of conduction.

### 27. Rhythm — regular, regularly irregular, irregularly irregular [E]

- **Irregularly irregular with varying volume** — atrial fibrillation. Mechanism: chaotic atrial activity with variable AV nodal conduction, so both the R-R interval and the preceding diastolic filling time vary, producing variable stroke volume.
- **Regularly irregular** — second-degree AV block with fixed ratio, or frequent ectopy in a fixed coupling pattern (bigeminy, trigeminy).
- **Irregular becoming regular on exertion** — favours ectopy over atrial fibrillation, since increased sinus rate suppresses ectopic foci.

### 28. Volume [E]

- Low volume (thready) — reduced stroke volume, as in shock, severe aortic stenosis, tamponade.
- High volume (bounding) — increased stroke volume, as in anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, sepsis, aortic regurgitation, or arteriovenous fistula.

### 29. Collapsing (water-hammer, Corrigan) pulse [E]

- Definition: a rapidly rising, rapidly collapsing pulse, best appreciated by grasping the forearm and elevating it above the level of the heart.
- Mechanism: **severe aortic regurgitation** — a large forward stroke volume produces a steep upstroke, and rapid regurgitant runoff into the ventricle during diastole produces the abrupt collapse. Elevating the arm removes the hydrostatic column and exaggerates it.
- Also produced by any state of wide pulse pressure with rapid diastolic runoff: large arteriovenous fistula, patent ductus arteriosus, thyrotoxicosis, severe anaemia.

### 30. Pulsus parvus et tardus (anacrotic pulse) [E]

- Definition: a small-volume, slow-rising pulse with a delayed and sustained peak, sometimes with a palpable shudder on the upstroke.
- Mechanism: **severe aortic stenosis** — the fixed orifice both limits stroke volume and prolongs the ejection period, flattening the upstroke.
- Caveat: markedly blunted in the elderly with stiff, non-compliant arteries, in whom severe aortic stenosis may present with a normal-feeling pulse. Do not use its absence to exclude severe stenosis.

### 31. Pulsus bisferiens [E]

- Definition: two systolic peaks within a single cardiac cycle, palpable in the carotid.
- Mechanism: percussion wave followed by a reflected or tidal wave, seen in **mixed aortic stenosis and regurgitation**, in isolated severe aortic regurgitation, and in **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with dynamic outflow obstruction** (where the mid-systolic dip reflects the Venturi-driven obstruction developing during ejection — the "spike and dome").

### 32. Dicrotic pulse [E]

- Definition: two peaks, one in systole and one in **diastole**, the second being an exaggerated dicrotic notch.
- Mechanism: very low stroke volume with reduced peripheral resistance and a compliant aorta — severe systolic heart failure, dilated cardiomyopathy, or cardiac tamponade. Distinguish from bisferiens by the fact that one peak is diastolic.

### 33. Pulsus alternans [E]

- Definition: regular alternation of strong and weak beats with a regular rhythm.
- Mechanism: severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction. On the beat following a strong ejection, less residual volume and incomplete recovery of calcium cycling and contractile protein availability produce a weaker beat; the resulting larger residual volume then augments the next. A sign of advanced failure.

### 34. Pulsus paradoxus [E]

- Definition: an exaggerated inspiratory fall in systolic pressure, conventionally taken as more than 10 mmHg — a figure that is definitional and long-standing consensus, and quoted as such rather than as a derived statistic.
- Mechanism, in the sequence to memorise: inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure → increased systemic venous return fills the right ventricle → within a **fixed pericardial volume** (tamponade) or a fixed thoracic constraint, right ventricular filling can only occur at the expense of the left → the interventricular septum shifts leftward and the pericardial constraint limits total volume → left ventricular preload and therefore stroke volume fall → systolic pressure falls. This is **ventricular interdependence**, and it is the single mechanism you need for tamponade physiology.
- Also occurs in severe asthma or COPD (large negative inspiratory swings), massive pulmonary embolism, and tension pneumothorax. Notably **reduced or absent** in tamponade coexisting with aortic regurgitation, severe LV hypertrophy, or an atrial septal defect.
- **Reverse pulsus paradoxus** — systolic pressure *rises* on inspiration; seen with positive-pressure ventilation and in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [L].

### 35. Radio-radial delay or asymmetry [E]

- Mechanism: stenosis, occlusion, or dissection involving one subclavian or brachial artery proximal to the point of palpation — atherosclerotic, dissection-related, or from Takayasu arteritis.

### 36. Radio-femoral delay [E]

- Definition: the femoral pulse arriving palpably later than the radial.
- Mechanism: **coarctation of the aorta** — flow to the lower body traverses a narrowed segment and collateral pathways, delaying and damping the pulse. Accompanied by upper limb hypertension and a lower limb blood pressure that is lower than the arm.

### 37. Apex-radial (pulse) deficit [E]

- Definition: the rate counted at the apex exceeds the rate counted at the radial artery.
- Mechanism: some ventricular contractions follow so short a diastole that stroke volume is insufficient to generate a palpable peripheral pulse. Characteristic of atrial fibrillation and of frequent ectopy.

### 38. Absent, diminished, or asymmetric pulses; bruits over accessible arteries [E]

- Mechanism: flow-limiting stenosis or occlusion damping the pulse wave distally; turbulence across the stenosis generating an audible bruit.
- Palpate and auscultate systematically: radial, brachial, carotid, subclavian, abdominal aorta and renal areas, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis.

### 39. Buerger's test and Buerger's angle [E]

- Definition: with the patient supine, the leg is elevated until the sole blanches; the angle at which pallor appears is Buerger's angle. On subsequent dependency, the foot becomes reactively red (sunset foot).
- Mechanism: an angle below 20° indicates that gravity alone can overcome the residual perfusion pressure, implying severe arterial insufficiency. Reactive hyperaemia on dependency reflects accumulated vasodilator metabolites in an ischaemic, maximally dilated capillary bed.

### 40. Allen test [E]

- Definition: with both radial and ulnar arteries occluded, the hand blanches; release of the ulnar artery should restore colour within seconds.
- Mechanism: tests the patency of the palmar arch collateral supply. Performed before radial arterial cannulation or radial artery harvest, to ensure the hand will survive on ulnar supply alone.

### Blood pressure signs

### 41. Hypertension and hypotension [E]

- Mechanism: mean arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance; every cause acts through one or both. Framework of causes and thresholds is deferred to Part 3 and Part 4, since thresholds and staging are guideline-dependent and the ESC 2024 and AHA/ACC 2025 documents diverge [7,8,12] [G].

### 42. Wide pulse pressure [E]

- Mechanism: increased stroke volume, rapid diastolic runoff, or reduced arterial compliance. Causes: aortic regurgitation, arteriovenous fistula, patent ductus arteriosus, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, pregnancy, and isolated systolic hypertension of arterial stiffening in the elderly.

### 43. Narrow pulse pressure [E]

- Mechanism: reduced stroke volume with maintained or elevated systemic vascular resistance. Causes: cardiogenic shock, severe aortic stenosis, tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, hypovolaemia. A narrow pulse pressure in a shocked patient favours a cardiac or obstructive cause over a distributive one.

### 44. Inter-arm blood pressure difference [E]

- Mechanism: unilateral subclavian, axillary, or brachial stenosis, or aortic dissection involving a branch origin. A new difference with acute severe chest pain is a dissection red flag.

### 45. Upper-to-lower limb blood pressure discrepancy [E]

- Mechanism: coarctation or aortic obstruction (arm pressure exceeding leg), or dissection with iliac involvement. Normally leg pressure slightly **exceeds** arm pressure because of pulse wave amplification, so equality or reversal is abnormal.

### 46. Orthostatic (postural) hypotension [E]

- Definition: a fall in systolic pressure of at least 20 mmHg, or diastolic of at least 10 mmHg, within three minutes of standing — the conventional definitional thresholds. [E]
- Mechanism: failure of the baroreflex-mediated compensatory vasoconstriction and tachycardia on standing — from volume depletion, autonomic neuropathy, or drugs. **Note the heart rate**: an absent compensatory tachycardia indicates autonomic failure or β-blockade, whereas a marked tachycardia favours volume depletion.

### 47. Osler sign (pseudohypertension) [L]

- Definition: the radial or brachial artery remains palpable as a rigid cord when the cuff is inflated above systolic pressure.
- Mechanism: heavily calcified, non-compressible arteries prevent the cuff from occluding the vessel, producing falsely high cuff readings. Reported in the elderly and in CKD; the evidence for its reproducibility is limited, so it should prompt confirmation rather than a conclusion.

### 48. Hill sign [L]

- Definition: popliteal systolic pressure exceeding brachial systolic pressure by a large margin in aortic regurgitation.
- Mechanism: exaggerated pulse wave amplification down a compliant arterial tree carrying a large stroke volume. Correlates with AR severity but the quantitative thresholds rest on old, small studies; know the sign, do not quote a cut-off.

### Group 3 — Face, eyes, and mouth

### 50. Conjunctival and palatal petechiae [E]

- Mechanism: microembolic or immune-complex capillary rupture in infective endocarditis. Look under the eyelids and at the soft palate; these sites are commonly omitted.

### 51. Roth spots [E]

- Definition: retinal haemorrhages with a pale centre.
- Mechanism: a retinal capillary rupture whose central clot is subsequently infiltrated by fibrin and leucocytes, producing the white core. Seen in infective endocarditis, but also in anaemia, leukaemia, diabetes, and hypertension — so suggestive, not specific.

### 52. Hypertensive retinopathy [E]

- Findings in progression: arteriolar narrowing and tortuosity, arteriovenous nipping, then flame haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots, then hard exudates, then papilloedema.
- Mechanism: chronic pressure-induced arteriolar hyalinisation and medial hypertrophy, then fibrinoid necrosis with breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier (haemorrhage and exudate) and nerve fibre layer infarction (cotton-wool spots). Papilloedema indicates a **hypertensive emergency**.

### 54. Poor dentition, dental abscess, gingivitis [E]

- Mechanism: oral flora portal of entry for viridans streptococci in native valve endocarditis. Its presence in a febrile patient with a murmur is a directive finding.

### 56. Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) [E]

- Mechanism in a cardiovascular context: interruption of the sympathetic chain by an expanding thoracic aortic aneurysm, or by carotid dissection compressing the pericarotid sympathetic plexus. In a young patient with neck pain, this is a carotid dissection sign.

### 57. Argyll Robertson pupil [E]

- Definition: a small, irregular pupil that accommodates but does not react to light.
- Mechanism: tertiary neurosyphilis. Relevant here because the same disease causes **syphilitic aortitis** with ascending aortic aneurysm and aortic regurgitation.

**58. Aortic regurgitation eponyms of the head and face** [E] — all produced by the **single** mechanism of a wide pulse pressure transmitted to a vascular bed. Learn them as one family, not five facts.

- **De Musset sign** — head nodding synchronous with the pulse.
- **Müller sign** — pulsation of the uvula.
- **Landolfi sign** — alternating pupillary constriction and dilatation with the pulse.
- **Becker sign** — visible pulsation of the retinal arteries.

### Group 4 — Neck

### 59. Jugular venous pressure — elevation [E]

- Definition: visible venous pulsation in the internal jugular vein more than 3 cm vertically above the sternal angle with the patient at 45°, corresponding to a right atrial pressure above roughly 8 cmH₂O. These are the conventional definitional reference values. [E]
- Mechanism: the internal jugular vein communicates with the right atrium without intervening valves, so it functions as a manometer of right atrial pressure. Elevation therefore indicates raised right atrial pressure — from right ventricular failure, volume overload, tricuspid disease, pericardial constraint, or pulmonary hypertension.
- **Distinguishing venous from arterial pulsation:** venous pulsation is non-palpable, has a double waveform, is obliterated by light pressure at the base of the neck, varies with respiration (falling on inspiration), and rises with the hepatojugular manoeuvre. The carotid does none of these.

### 60. JVP waveform components [E] — the highest-yield mechanistic content in cardiovascular examination.

- **a wave** — atrial contraction against a partially open tricuspid valve. Precedes the carotid upstroke.
- **c wave** — bulging of the closed tricuspid valve into the right atrium during early ventricular systole, plus transmitted carotid pulsation.
- **x descent** — atrial relaxation and downward displacement of the tricuspid annulus during ventricular systole (the "atrial diastolic descent").
- **v wave** — passive atrial filling against a closed tricuspid valve during ventricular systole.
- **y descent** — tricuspid valve opening with rapid ventricular filling.

### 61. Abnormal JVP waveforms and their mechanisms [E]

- **Absent a waves** — no organised atrial contraction: **atrial fibrillation**.
- **Large (giant) a waves** — forceful atrial contraction against increased resistance: tricuspid stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy from pulmonary stenosis or pulmonary hypertension.
- **Cannon a waves** — intermittent, very large a waves occurring when atrial contraction coincides with a closed tricuspid valve during ventricular systole. Mechanism: atrioventricular dissociation — complete heart block, ventricular tachycardia, or AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia. This is the objective correlate of the patient's neck-pounding symptom (Part 1, item 26).
- **Giant v waves with an obliterated x descent (systolic venous pulsation)** — **severe tricuspid regurgitation**; ventricular systole drives blood directly back into the atrium and great veins. Often accompanied by a pulsatile liver and pulsatile earlobes.
- **Rapid, deep y descent (Friedreich sign)** — a stiff but rapidly filling ventricle: **constrictive pericarditis** and restrictive cardiomyopathy. Mechanism: early diastolic filling is unimpeded and abrupt, then halts against the constraint.
- **Slow or absent y descent** — impaired early diastolic filling: **cardiac tamponade** (where filling is limited throughout diastole) and tricuspid stenosis.
- **The tamponade-versus-constriction discriminator to memorise:** tamponade has a blunted y descent and pulsus paradoxus; constriction has a prominent y descent and Kussmaul sign. This distinction rests on whether the constraint operates throughout diastole (tamponade) or only after rapid early filling (constriction).

### 62. Kussmaul sign [E]

- Definition: a **rise** in JVP on inspiration, instead of the normal fall.
- Mechanism: inspiration augments systemic venous return, but a non-compliant right heart or pericardium cannot accommodate the extra volume, so pressure rises rather than the column falling. Seen in constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, right ventricular infarction, and severe right heart failure. **Characteristically absent in classical tamponade.**

### 63. Hepatojugular (abdominojugular) reflux [E]

- Definition: firm sustained pressure over the right upper quadrant for 10–15 seconds produces a rise in JVP that is **sustained** for the duration of compression, rather than a transient rise.
- Mechanism: abdominal compression translocates splanchnic venous blood into the thorax; a normal right ventricle accommodates this and the JVP returns promptly, whereas a failing or constrained right ventricle cannot, and the venous pressure remains elevated. Ensure the patient does not perform a Valsalva manoeuvre, which produces a false positive.

### 64. Fixed, elevated, non-pulsatile jugular distension [E]

- Mechanism: **superior vena cava obstruction** — the venous column is no longer in communication with the right atrium, so it neither pulsates nor varies with respiration. Accompanied by facial and upper limb oedema, plethora, and dilated chest wall collateral veins.

### 65. Carotid pulse character and Corrigan sign [E]

- Corrigan sign: visible, forceful, dancing carotid pulsation in severe aortic regurgitation, from a wide pulse pressure.
- The carotid, being closest to the aorta, is the correct site to assess pulse *character* (parvus et tardus, bisferiens, shudder); the radial is the site to assess rate, rhythm, and volume. Assessing aortic stenosis from the radial pulse is a common and misleading error.

### 66. Carotid shudder [L]

- Definition: a palpable thrill or vibration on the carotid upstroke.
- Mechanism: transmitted turbulence from a stenotic aortic valve. Reported to be relatively specific for aortic stenosis; reliable operating characteristics not sourced here.

### 67. Carotid bruit [E]

- Mechanism: turbulent flow across a carotid stenosis. Important caveat with a mechanistic basis: a **very** severe, near-occlusive stenosis may be silent because flow is too low to generate turbulence, so absence of a bruit does not exclude severe disease.

### 68. Goitre and thyroid bruit [E]

- Mechanism: a hypervascular thyroid in Graves disease. Relevant because thyrotoxicosis causes atrial fibrillation, high-output failure, and a wide pulse pressure.

### Group 5 — Praecordium: inspection and palpation

### 69. Visible pulsation and chest wall deformity [E]

- Visible apical or parasternal pulsation implies a hyperdynamic or dilated ventricle, or a thin chest wall. Harrison's sulcus or a prominent praecordium in a child suggests long-standing volume overload.

### 70. Apex beat position [E]

- Definition: the most inferolateral point at which a cardiac impulse is palpable; normally the fifth intercostal space at or medial to the midclavicular line.
- Mechanism of displacement: left ventricular dilatation (volume overload), or extracardiac displacement by pleural effusion, pneumothorax, lobar collapse, or scoliosis. **Confirm the trachea and percussion note before attributing displacement to the heart.**
- **Impalpable apex** — obesity, emphysema with hyperinflation, large pericardial effusion, dextrocardia (palpate the right side), or thick chest wall.

### 71. Apex beat character [E]

- **Tapping** — a palpable first heart sound with no true left ventricular impulse. Mechanism: **mitral stenosis** — a loud S1 from a thickened valve closing from a wide-open position, without ventricular dilatation.
- **Heaving (sustained, pressure-loaded)** — a forceful, prolonged outward thrust with normal position. Mechanism: **concentric left ventricular hypertrophy** against a pressure load — aortic stenosis or hypertension.
- **Thrusting (hyperdynamic, volume-loaded)** — a vigorous, diffuse, briefly displaced impulse. Mechanism: **volume overload** — aortic or mitral regurgitation.
- **Double impulse** — mechanism: **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** (palpable S4 plus systolic impulse).
- **Dyskinetic or diffuse "rocking"** — mechanism: a large left ventricular aneurysm or severe global dysfunction, with paradoxical systolic bulging of non-contractile wall.

### 72. Left parasternal heave [E]

- Definition: a sustained outward thrust felt with the heel of the hand at the left parasternal area.
- Mechanism: right ventricular hypertrophy or dilatation — pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary stenosis, or a large left-to-right shunt.

### 73. Thrills [E]

- Definition: a palpable murmur, corresponding to at least grade 4 on the Levine scale.
- Mechanism: high-velocity turbulent flow transmitting vibration to the chest wall. Localise and time it (systolic or diastolic) — a **diastolic thrill** is uncommon and points to severe mitral stenosis or aortic regurgitation.

### 74. Palpable heart sounds [E]

- **Palpable P2** in the second left intercostal space — pulmonary hypertension.
- **Palpable S1** — the tapping apex of mitral stenosis.
- **Palpable S3 or S4** — contributing to the double or triple apical impulse.

### 75. Epigastric pulsation [E]

- Three distinct mechanisms, distinguished by direction and character:
  - **Expansile and pulsatile in two planes** — abdominal aortic aneurysm.
  - **Transmitted (single plane, pushing the fingers upward)** — normal aorta in a thin patient, or a hyperdynamic circulation.
  - **Downward-thrusting** — right ventricular enlargement.

### 76. Pulsatile liver [E]

- Mechanism: **severe tricuspid regurgitation** transmitting systolic ventricular pressure retrograde into the hepatic veins. Place a hand over the liver and confirm the pulsation is systolic and expansile.

### Group 6 — Auscultatory signs

*Technique, sequence, and the performance of dynamic manoeuvres are covered in Part 3. Here: the sign and its mechanism.*

### 77. First heart sound (S1) [E]

- Mechanism: closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves with abrupt tensing of the valve apparatus and surrounding blood.
- **Loud S1** — mitral stenosis (a thickened valve closing from a widely open position, with a high left atrial pressure holding it open until late), short PR interval, tachycardia, high-output states.
- **Soft S1** — mitral regurgitation (incomplete apposition), long PR interval, poor left ventricular contractility, calcified immobile valve, or anything increasing the distance to the stethoscope.
- **Variable S1** — atrial fibrillation and complete heart block, where the PR interval and therefore the valve position at the onset of systole vary beat to beat.

### 78. Second heart sound (S2) and its splitting [E]

- Mechanism: closure of the aortic (A2) then pulmonary (P2) valves.
- **Normal physiological splitting** — widens on inspiration. Mechanism: inspiration increases right ventricular filling and prolongs right ventricular ejection, delaying P2, while simultaneously reducing pulmonary venous return to the left ventricle and slightly advancing A2.
- **Wide but still variable splitting** — delayed right ventricular emptying: right bundle branch block, pulmonary stenosis, or a large ASD/VSD.
- **Fixed wide splitting (no respiratory variation)** — **atrial septal defect**. Mechanism: the interatrial communication equalises the effect of respiration on the two atria, abolishing the normal respiratory variation in right ventricular filling.
- **Reversed (paradoxical) splitting — narrowing on inspiration** — delayed **left** ventricular emptying so that A2 follows P2: left bundle branch block, severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, right ventricular pacing.
- **Loud P2** — pulmonary hypertension.
- **Soft or absent A2** — severe calcific aortic stenosis with an immobile valve.
- **Single S2** — a single functioning semilunar valve, or fusion of the components.

### 79. Third heart sound (S3) [E]

- Definition: a low-frequency early diastolic sound producing a triple "Ken-tuc-ky" cadence, best heard at the apex with the bell.
- Mechanism: abrupt deceleration of rapid early diastolic ventricular filling against a **dilated, poorly compliant** ventricle, with sudden tensing of the ventricular wall and chordal apparatus.
- **Left-sided S3** — heart failure with dilated left ventricle, mitral regurgitation, aortic regurgitation. In an adult, a left-sided S3 is a strong indicator of elevated filling pressures.
- **Right-sided S3** — heard at the lower left sternal edge, louder on inspiration; right ventricular failure.
- **Physiological S3** — normal in children, young adults, athletes, and pregnancy, where high-volume rapid filling occurs in a healthy ventricle.

### 80. Fourth heart sound (S4) [E]

- Definition: a low-frequency **presystolic** sound producing a "Ten-nes-see" cadence.
- Mechanism: forceful atrial contraction propelling blood into a **stiff, non-compliant but not necessarily dilated** ventricle. Because it requires organised atrial contraction, **S4 cannot exist in atrial fibrillation** — a clean logical test point.
- Causes: left ventricular hypertrophy from hypertension or aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, acute myocardial ischaemia, and restrictive physiology.

### 81. Summation gallop [E]

- Mechanism: at rapid heart rates, diastole shortens until S3 and S4 merge into a single loud mid-diastolic sound. Implies both impaired compliance and elevated filling pressures.

### 82. Opening snap [E]

- Definition: a sharp, high-frequency early diastolic sound after S2, heard at the apex.
- Mechanism: abrupt tensing of a **stenotic but still pliable** mitral valve as it reaches its maximal excursion — hence it disappears when the valve becomes heavily calcified and rigid.
- **The A2–opening snap interval is inversely related to severity**: a higher left atrial pressure opens the valve earlier, so a *shorter* interval means *more* severe stenosis. Counterintuitive and therefore examinable.

### 83. Ejection click [E]

- Definition: an early systolic high-frequency sound.
- Mechanism: abrupt doming of a congenitally abnormal but mobile semilunar valve — bicuspid aortic valve, or congenital pulmonary stenosis. Disappears with calcification.

### 84. Mid-systolic click [E]

- Mechanism: sudden tensing of redundant mitral leaflets and elongated chordae as the prolapsing leaflet reaches its maximum excursion — **mitral valve prolapse**. Its timing moves earlier with reduced ventricular volume (standing, Valsalva) and later with increased volume (squatting), because a smaller cavity allows prolapse to occur sooner.

### 85. Pericardial knock [E]

- Definition: an early diastolic sound, higher-pitched and earlier than S3.
- Mechanism: **constrictive pericarditis** — abrupt cessation of rapid early diastolic filling against the rigid pericardial shell. The auscultatory counterpart of the rapid deep y descent.

### 86. Tumour plop [L]

- Definition: a low-pitched early diastolic sound, variable between examinations and with posture.
- Mechanism: a pedunculated **left atrial myxoma** prolapsing into the mitral orifice and abruptly halting. Rare; its positional variability is the clue.

### 87. Prosthetic valve sounds [E]

- Mechanism: mechanical valves produce crisp metallic opening and closing clicks. A mechanical mitral prosthesis clicks in systole (closure); a mechanical aortic prosthesis clicks in diastole (closure). A **muffled or absent** click, or a new murmur, suggests thrombosis or dehiscence and is an urgent finding.

### 88. Murmur grading (Levine scale) [E]

- Grade 1 barely audible with concentration; 2 soft but immediately audible; 3 moderately loud, no thrill; 4 loud **with a thrill**; 5 audible with the stethoscope edge partly off the chest; 6 audible with the stethoscope off the chest. The presence of a thrill at grade 4 is the objective anchor.
- Important mechanistic caveat: **loudness does not track severity**. A critically stenotic valve with a failing ventricle generates little flow and therefore a quiet murmur; a trivial lesion in a hyperdynamic circulation can be loud.

### 89. Systolic murmurs [E]

- **Aortic stenosis** — harsh mid-systolic ejection (crescendo–decrescendo) murmur, right upper sternal edge, radiating to the carotids. Mechanism: turbulent high-velocity flow across a narrowed orifice, peaking as flow peaks. Severity correlates with **late peaking**, a soft or absent A2, and a slow-rising pulse — not with loudness.
- **Mitral regurgitation** — high-pitched blowing pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. Mechanism: a systolic pressure gradient from left ventricle to left atrium existing throughout systole.
- **Tricuspid regurgitation** — pansystolic at the lower left sternal edge, **louder on inspiration (Carvallo sign)**. Mechanism: inspiration augments right ventricular filling and therefore regurgitant volume — the single reliable manoeuvre for lateralising a murmur to the right heart.
- **Ventricular septal defect** — harsh pansystolic at the lower left sternal edge with a thrill. Mechanism: a large systolic pressure gradient across the septum. **A small defect gives a loud murmur; a very large defect or one with equalised pressures gives a soft or absent murmur** — again, loudness is not severity.
- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with outflow obstruction** — mid-to-late systolic murmur at the left sternal edge that **increases** with Valsalva and standing and **decreases** with squatting and handgrip. Mechanism: the obstruction is dynamic and worsens as ventricular volume falls, which is the exact opposite of the response of fixed aortic stenosis. This differential response is the highest-yield dynamic manoeuvre in cardiology.
- **Mitral valve prolapse** — mid-systolic click with a late systolic murmur.
- **Pulmonary stenosis** — ejection systolic murmur, left upper sternal edge, radiating to the left shoulder, louder on inspiration.
- **Innocent (flow) murmur** — soft, mid-systolic, grade 1–2, no thrill, no radiation, normal S2, no other abnormality, often varying with posture. Mechanism: increased flow velocity across normal valves in a hyperdynamic state — fever, anaemia, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis, childhood.

### 90. Diastolic murmurs [E]

- **Aortic regurgitation** — soft, high-pitched **early diastolic decrescendo** murmur at the left sternal edge, best heard sitting forward in held expiration. Mechanism: retrograde flow down the aortic-to-ventricular pressure gradient, which is maximal at the start of diastole and decays. Severity tracks the **duration** of the murmur, not its loudness.
- **Mitral stenosis** — low-pitched **mid-diastolic rumble** at the apex with **presystolic accentuation** if in sinus rhythm, best heard in the left lateral position with the bell. Mechanism: turbulent flow across the narrowed orifice during passive filling, with a further increase during atrial contraction — which is why the presystolic accentuation **disappears in atrial fibrillation**.
- **Tricuspid stenosis** — mid-diastolic rumble at the lower left sternal edge, louder on inspiration. Rare; usually rheumatic and accompanied by mitral disease.
- **Pulmonary regurgitation** — early diastolic decrescendo at the left upper sternal edge.
- **Austin Flint murmur** — a mid-diastolic apical rumble in **severe aortic regurgitation without mitral stenosis**. Mechanism: the regurgitant aortic jet strikes the anterior mitral leaflet, causing it to vibrate and functionally narrow the mitral inflow. Distinguished from true mitral stenosis by the absence of a loud S1 and of an opening snap.
- **Graham Steell murmur** — early diastolic murmur of **pulmonary regurgitation secondary to severe pulmonary hypertension**, classically in advanced mitral stenosis. Mechanism: pulmonary annular dilatation from high pulmonary pressure.
- **Carey Coombs murmur** — a short mid-diastolic apical murmur in **acute rheumatic mitral valvulitis**. Mechanism: turbulent flow across an acutely inflamed, oedematous mitral valve; transient, resolving with the carditis.

### 91. Continuous murmurs [E]

- **Patent ductus arteriosus (Gibson murmur)** — continuous "machinery" murmur, left infraclavicular. Mechanism: a pressure gradient from aorta to pulmonary artery persisting through both systole and diastole.
- **Arteriovenous fistula** — continuous bruit over the fistula, with a palpable thrill and a hyperdynamic circulation.
- **Ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm, coronary arteriovenous fistula, coarctation collaterals** — same principle, a persistent gradient between two chambers or vessels.
- **Venous hum** — a benign continuous murmur in children, abolished by light jugular compression or by turning the head. Mechanism: turbulent flow in the jugular veins.

### 92. Pericardial friction rub [E]

- Definition: a superficial, scratching or creaking sound, classically with up to three components (atrial systolic, ventricular systolic, ventricular diastolic), best heard sitting forward, and **not abolished by breath-holding**.
- Mechanism: friction between inflamed visceral and parietal pericardial surfaces.
- Key properties: **evanescent** — present on one examination, absent hours later — so a single negative examination does not exclude pericarditis; and it **disappears as effusion accumulates** and separates the surfaces, so loss of a rub with clinical deterioration should raise concern for tamponade rather than reassure.
- The current European framework for pericardial disease is the 2025 ESC guideline on myocarditis and pericarditis, which replaced the 2015 pericardial diseases guideline [11] [G].

### 93. Pleuropericardial rub [E]

- Distinguished from a pericardial rub because it **is** abolished by breath-holding, indicating a pleural rather than purely pericardial origin.

### 94. Means–Lerman scratch [L]

- A systolic scratching sound at the left second intercostal space in thyrotoxicosis, attributed to friction between a hyperdynamic pericardium and pleura. Rare; know the name.

**95. Peripheral auscultatory signs of aortic regurgitation** [E] — again, one mechanism (wide pulse pressure with rapid runoff), several eponyms.

- **Duroziez sign** — a to-and-fro double bruit over the femoral artery with light distal compression by the stethoscope.
- **Traube sign (pistol-shot femorals)** — loud systolic and diastolic sounds over the femoral arteries.

### Group 7 — Respiratory signs of cardiac origin

### 96. Bibasal fine inspiratory crackles [E]

- Mechanism: sudden equalisation of pressure as small airways and alveoli, collapsed or fluid-filled by pulmonary oedema, snap open during inspiration. Gravity-dependent, hence basal, and rising to the mid or upper zones as oedema worsens.
- Critical caveat: crackles are neither sensitive nor specific for heart failure, since chronic heart failure with adapted lymphatic clearance may produce none, while fibrosis and pneumonia mimic them. Interpret alongside JVP, S3, and oedema.

### 97. Cardiac wheeze [E]

- Mechanism: peribronchial oedema and cuffing narrowing small airways, producing expiratory wheeze in acute pulmonary oedema. Frequently misdiagnosed and treated as asthma; the discriminators are the JVP, the S3, and the orthopnoea.

### 98. Pleural effusion signs [E]

- Stony dull percussion note, reduced breath sounds, reduced vocal resonance, reduced expansion.
- Mechanism: elevated pulmonary capillary and systemic venous pressure exceeding pleural lymphatic drainage capacity. In heart failure, effusions are typically bilateral, or right-greater-than-left when unilateral.

### 99. Cheyne–Stokes respiration [E]

- Definition: cyclical crescendo–decrescendo tidal volume with intervening apnoea.
- Mechanism: prolonged circulatory transit time in low-output heart failure delays the arrival of arterial blood at the central chemoreceptors, so the feedback loop overshoots and undershoots — an instability of the respiratory control system. A marker of advanced heart failure.

### Group 8 — Abdominal signs

### 100. Tender hepatomegaly [E]

- Mechanism: acute hepatic venous congestion distending Glisson's capsule. Tenderness indicates recent or rapid distension; a chronically congested liver may be enlarged, firm, and painless.

### 102. Ascites [E]

- Signs: distension, shifting dullness, fluid thrill.
- Mechanism: elevated hepatic sinusoidal and peritoneal capillary pressure from systemic venous hypertension. **Ascites disproportionate to peripheral oedema** points specifically to constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, or tricuspid disease rather than to biventricular failure.

### 103. Splenomegaly [E]

- Mechanism in a cardiovascular context: reticuloendothelial hyperplasia from chronic bacteraemia in **subacute infective endocarditis**, or splenic infarction from septic embolism.

### 104. Expansile pulsatile abdominal mass [E]

- Mechanism: abdominal aortic aneurysm. **Expansile** (fingers pushed apart) rather than merely transmitted (fingers pushed upward) is the discriminating feature.

### 105. Abdominal, renal, and femoral bruits [E]

- Mechanism: turbulence across aortic, renal, mesenteric, or iliac stenosis. A renal bruit in a young hypertensive patient points to fibromuscular dysplasia or a large-vessel vasculitis; in an older patient, to atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis.

### Group 9 — Peripheral arterial, venous, and lymphatic signs

### 106. Trophic changes of chronic arterial insufficiency [E]

- Signs: hair loss over the dorsum of the foot and lower leg, shiny atrophic skin, thickened dystrophic nails, muscle wasting, delayed capillary refill.
- Mechanism: chronically inadequate nutritive perfusion cannot support the metabolic demands of appendages and repair. These develop over months to years and therefore indicate chronic rather than acute disease.

### 107. Arterial ulcer [E]

- Morphology: punched-out, deep, with a pale dry base and sharply demarcated edge; painful; on the toes, dorsum of the foot, lateral malleolus, or pressure points.
- Mechanism: perfusion inadequate for tissue repair, so minor trauma initiates a wound that cannot heal.

### 108. Venous ulcer [E]

- Morphology: shallow, irregular, exudative, with granulating base; at the **medial malleolus** (the gaiter area); relatively less painful; surrounded by haemosiderin staining and lipodermatosclerosis.
- Mechanism: ambulatory venous hypertension → capillary hypertension → fibrin cuffing, red cell extravasation, and chronic inflammation → skin fragility.

### 109. Neuropathic ulcer [E] — included because it is the third member of the triad you must distinguish.

- Morphology: painless, on pressure-bearing surfaces (plantar metatarsal heads, heel), with surrounding callus and warm well-perfused surrounding skin.
- Mechanism: loss of protective sensation with repetitive unnoticed pressure.

### 110. Gangrene [E]

- Dry gangrene: mummified, black, demarcated, no infection. Mechanism: progressive ischaemic necrosis with desiccation.
- Wet gangrene: swollen, malodorous, discharging. Mechanism: ischaemic necrosis with superimposed infection — a surgical emergency.

### 111. Chronic venous insufficiency signs [E]

- **Varicosities** — dilated tortuous superficial veins. Mechanism: valvular incompetence with reversed flow and progressive dilatation.
- **Haemosiderin (brown) staining** — extravasated red cells degraded to haemosiderin within dermal macrophages.
- **Lipodermatosclerosis** — indurated, woody, inverted-champagne-bottle leg. Mechanism: chronic inflammation with dermal and subcutaneous fibrosis.
- **Atrophie blanche** — smooth ivory-white atrophic plaques with surrounding telangiectasia. Mechanism: healed microinfarction of dermal capillaries.
- **Corona phlebectatica** — fan of small dilated veins around the medial ankle. Mechanism: an early marker of advanced venous disease.

### 112. Deep vein thrombosis signs [E]

- Unilateral limb swelling, warmth, erythema, calf tenderness, dilated superficial collateral veins, and a measurable calf circumference difference.
- Mechanism: venous outflow obstruction raising distal capillary pressure, with vein wall inflammation causing tenderness.
- **Homans sign** (calf pain on passive dorsiflexion) is **obsolete** — neither sensitive nor specific, and there is a theoretical concern about dislodging thrombus. Do not perform it; know that it exists so that you recognise it as outdated. This will be flagged again in the obsolete-signs list of the examination section (Part 3, per template C5 principles).

### 113. Lymphoedema signs [E]

- **Non-pitting** swelling, involving the **dorsum of the foot and the toes** (giving a squared "buffalo hump" forefoot), with skin thickening, peau d'orange, and later papillomatosis.
- **Stemmer sign** — inability to pinch and lift a fold of skin at the base of the second toe. Mechanism: dermal fibrosis from protein-rich interstitial fluid.
- The involvement of the toes is what distinguishes lymphoedema from venous or cardiac oedema, which characteristically spare them.

### 114. Livedo reticularis and blue toe syndrome [E]

- Definition: a net-like violaceous mottling of the skin; discrete blue or purple discoloration of one or more toes with preserved pedal pulses.
- Mechanism: **atheroembolism** — showers of cholesterol crystals from a proximal plaque or aneurysm occluding cutaneous arterioles. Often follows catheterisation or aortic surgery. Preserved pulses with distal cyanosis is the paradoxical combination that identifies embolic rather than obstructive disease.

### 115. Raynaud phenomenon (objective triphasic colour change) [E]

- Sequence: sharply demarcated pallor → cyanosis → reactive hyperaemia on rewarming.
- Mechanism: exaggerated digital arteriolar vasospasm, with superimposed structural microvascular disease in secondary Raynaud. **Digital ulceration, asymmetry, or abnormal nailfold capillaries indicate secondary disease** and mandate investigation for connective tissue disease.

### 116. Ankle–brachial index [E]

- Definition: the ratio of the highest ankle systolic pressure to the highest brachial systolic pressure.
- Mechanism and its failure mode: a reduced ratio reflects a pressure drop across proximal stenoses. **In medial arterial calcification — diabetes, CKD — the vessels are incompressible and the index is falsely normal or high**, which is precisely the population in whom peripheral arterial disease is most prevalent. Toe–brachial index or waveform analysis is then required. Interpretive thresholds are deferred to Part 3.

### Group 10 — Signs of shock and haemodynamic profiling

### 117. The four bedside signs that define the haemodynamic profile [E]

- **Congestion axis ("wet" versus "dry")**: raised JVP, positive hepatojugular reflux, S3, oedema, crackles, ascites.
- **Perfusion axis ("cold" versus "warm")**: cool extremities, narrow pulse pressure, pulsus alternans, altered mentation, oliguria, and mottling.
- Mechanism and utility: these two axes are orthogonal and generate the four profiles (warm-wet, cold-wet, cold-dry, warm-dry) that direct whether the patient needs decongestion, inotropy, or volume. This is examination generating a treatment decision rather than a label, which is why it survives despite modern imaging.

### 118. Signs of cardiac tamponade (Beck triad and its companions) [E]

- **Beck triad**: hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds. Mechanism: pericardial pressure exceeding right atrial pressure impairs filling; fluid attenuates sound transmission.
- Companions: **pulsus paradoxus**, tachycardia, blunted y descent, absent Kussmaul sign, and **loss of a previously audible pericardial rub**.
- Honest caveat: the full triad is present in only a minority of cases, and each element is individually unreliable. Absence of the triad must not be used to exclude tamponade [L].

### 119. Signs of constrictive pericarditis [E]

- Raised JVP with a **rapid deep y descent (Friedreich sign)**, **Kussmaul sign**, **pericardial knock**, ascites disproportionate to peripheral oedema, hepatomegaly, and a quiet praecordium with an impalpable apex.
- Mechanism: a rigid pericardium permits rapid unimpeded early diastolic filling then halts it abruptly, and prevents any inspiratory accommodation of increased venous return.

### 120. Signs of aortic dissection [E]

- Inter-arm or arm-to-leg blood pressure differential, a pulse deficit of abrupt onset, a **new** early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation, focal neurological deficit, Horner syndrome, hypotension or shock if tamponade or rupture supervenes, and hypertension in the majority at presentation.
- Mechanism: propagation of the dissection flap across branch origins (pulse deficits, stroke, limb or visceral ischaemia), distortion of the aortic annulus (regurgitation), and rupture into the pericardium (tamponade).

**Escalation note (Rule B11):** several signs in this inventory are, by themselves, decision-forcing. A new inter-arm pressure differential with a new diastolic murmur; pulsus paradoxus with a raised JVP and hypotension; a muffled prosthetic valve click; an expansile pulsatile abdominal mass with pain; and papilloedema in a hypertensive patient are each sufficient to change disposition immediately. Recognising them is your job; deciding the intervention is not one to make alone.

## Third: Full clinical examination of the cardiovascular system

### 3.0 Preparation, sequence, and what has been abandoned

**Consent, position, exposure, environment** [E]

- Introduce yourself, state what you intend to do, obtain consent, wash hands.
- **Position: 45°.** Not flat, not upright. The JVP is only interpretable at a known angle, and 45° is the convention around which the 3 cm reference is defined.
- **Exposure: from the waist up.** For a woman, expose the chest only when you reach the praecordium, and offer a chaperone. A cardiovascular examination performed through clothing is not a cardiovascular examination.
- **Environment:** warm room (cold produces vasoconstriction and mimics low-output signs), good lighting with **tangential** illumination for the JVP, and quiet — you cannot hear a soft early diastolic murmur on a ward round.
- **Equipment:** stethoscope with both bell and diaphragm, correctly sized sphygmomanometer cuff, ophthalmoscope, tape measure, pen torch, watch with a second hand, and where available a handheld ultrasound probe.

**Sequence: modified IPPA, run head-to-toe rather than region-by-region** [E]

- End-of-bed general inspection
- Hands and arms
- Pulse — rate, rhythm, volume, character (radial, then **carotid** for character)
- Blood pressure — both arms, and lower limbs when indicated
- Face, eyes, mouth
- Neck — JVP, hepatojugular reflux, carotids (palpate then auscultate)
- Praecordium — inspect, palpate, then auscultate
- Dynamic manoeuvres and repositioning
- Posterior chest — lung bases, sacral oedema
- Abdomen — liver, ascites, aorta, renal and femoral bruits
- Lower limbs — oedema, peripheral pulses, trophic changes, ulcers
- Complete the examination — fundoscopy, urinalysis, temperature chart, observation chart, ECG

**Percussion: obsolete for the cardiac component, and this must be stated plainly** [E]

**Percussion of the cardiac borders: historical/obsolete.**

Percussion to define the cardiac outline was abandoned because it is imprecise, poorly reproducible between examiners, and has been **replaced by the chest radiograph and by echocardiography**, which measure chamber size directly. Do not perform it, and do not be impressed by someone who does.

Percussion retains genuine utility in this examination for three **non-cardiac** purposes, which is why it stays in the sequence:

- Detecting a **pleural effusion** (stony dullness at the bases) as a manifestation of cardiac failure.
- Assessing **liver span** in congestive hepatomegaly.
- **Shifting dullness** for ascites.

**Ewart sign** — dullness with bronchial breathing below the left scapula in a large pericardial effusion — is a percussion sign that survives only as an examination curiosity. Tier 3.

### 3.1 Inspection

**End of the bed, before touching the patient** [E]

Look deliberately for: general state and distress; body habitus and syndromic features; respiratory rate, pattern (including Cheyne–Stokes), accessory muscle use, ability to speak in sentences; posture (orthopnoeic, sitting forward); colour (central cyanosis, pallor, jaundice, malar flush); visible pulsations in the neck or praecordium; oedema; cachexia; scars and devices; and the surroundings — oxygen, infusions, monitoring, glyceryl trinitrate spray, inhalers, a fluid balance chart, a walking aid.

**Scars and devices, because they rewrite the differential before you begin** [E]

Median sternotomy; leg or forearm vein/artery harvest scars (which distinguish CABG from isolated valve surgery); left lateral thoracotomy; infraclavicular device box; arteriovenous fistula; groin puncture marks.

*Mechanisms of every inspection finding are given in Part 2 and are not repeated.*

**Documentation of inspection:** record what you looked for and did not find, not only what you found. "No cyanosis, clubbing, oedema, or scars" is information; silence is not.

### 3.2 Palpation

**Hands and arms** [E]

Temperature and sweating; capillary refill; clubbing (Schamroth's window); splinter haemorrhages; Osler nodes (**tender** — press them); Janeway lesions; tar staining; Quincke sign on transillumination; arachnodactyly with Walker–Murdoch and Steinberg signs.

**Pulse — a structured five-part assessment** [E]

- **Rate** — count for a full 15 seconds minimum, 60 seconds if irregular.
- **Rhythm** — regular, regularly irregular, irregularly irregular.
- **Volume**.
- **Character** — assess at the **carotid**, not the radial. The radial pulse has been modified by transmission down the arterial tree; the carotid is closest to the aorta and is where pulsus parvus et tardus, bisferiens, and the anacrotic shudder are actually detectable. Assessing aortic stenosis from the radial pulse is a common and misleading error.
- **Comparison** — radio-radial delay, radio-femoral delay, apex-radial deficit.

**Full pulse survey, in a fixed order so you never omit one** [E]

Radial → brachial → carotid (one at a time, never both simultaneously) → abdominal aorta → femoral → popliteal → posterior tibial → dorsalis pedis. Grade each as absent, reduced, normal, or bounding, and record laterality.

**Blood pressure** [E]

- Correct cuff size; arm supported at heart level; patient seated and rested; feet on the floor; no talking.
- **Both arms, always, on first assessment.** An inter-arm difference is otherwise never found.
- Lower limb pressure when coarctation, dissection, or peripheral arterial disease is suspected.
- Lying and standing pressures when syncope, dizziness, or autonomic dysfunction is in question — **and record the heart rate response**, since an absent compensatory tachycardia distinguishes autonomic failure or β-blockade from volume depletion.
- Measurement technique matters more than most trainees assume; the 2025 AHA/ACC high blood pressure guideline addresses measurement and out-of-office confirmation explicitly [18,32] [G].

**Neck** [E]

- **JVP:** patient at 45°, head turned slightly to the left with the neck relaxed, tangential lighting. Identify the venous meniscus — the highest point of pulsation in the right internal jugular vein — and measure the **vertical** distance to the sternal angle. The best definition of elevated JVP is when the top of the internal jugular vein is ≥3 cm above the sternal angle; the angle of the bed is irrelevant as long as the top of the venous column can be identified and the vertical distance to the sternal angle determined; central venous pressure is the JVP plus approximately 5 cm, the distance of the sternal angle above the right atrium [and see 83] [E]. Brown Med-Peds
- **Confirm it is venous:** non-palpable, double waveform, obliterated by light basal pressure, falls on inspiration, rises with hepatojugular reflux.
- **Read the waveform** — a, c, x, v, y — and specifically look for absent a waves, giant a waves, cannon a waves, giant v waves with obliterated x descent, and the character of the y descent. Mechanisms in Part 2.
- **Hepatojugular reflux:** firm sustained right upper quadrant pressure for 10–15 seconds; a **sustained** rise is positive. Ensure the patient breathes normally and does not Valsalva.
- **Kussmaul sign:** watch the JVP through a full inspiration.
- **Carotids:** palpate one at a time for character and shudder, then auscultate.

**Praecordium** [E]

- **Apex beat** — locate the most inferolateral point of impulse; record the intercostal space and its relation to the midclavicular line. Characterise as tapping, heaving, thrusting, double, or dyskinetic.
- **Parasternal heave** — heel of the hand at the left parasternal area.
- **Thrills** — flat of the hand over each auscultatory area; time any thrill as systolic or diastolic.
- **Palpable heart sounds** — palpable P2 in the second left intercostal space; palpable S1 at the apex.
- If the apex is impalpable, palpate the **right** side before concluding it is absent.

**Abdomen** [E]

Liver edge and span, and whether the liver is tender or pulsatile; splenomegaly; ascites; **expansile** versus transmitted aortic pulsation; then auscultate for aortic, renal, and femoral bruits.

**Lower limbs** [E]

Pitting oedema — press for at least 10–15 seconds over the tibia and check the **sacrum** in a bed-bound patient; calf circumference difference; temperature gradient; trophic changes; ulcers with their site and character; Stemmer sign at the second toe if swelling is non-pitting.

**Utility tiering of palpation manoeuvres** — an addition to the C3 template, adapted from the C5 framework, because it tells you where to spend limited bedside time:

- **Tier 1 (bedside standard, materially changes probability, must master):** JVP height, JVP waveform, hepatojugular reflux, bilateral blood pressure, carotid upstroke character, apex position and character, full pulse survey, peripheral oedema including sacral.
- **Tier 2 (useful in specific contexts):** Kussmaul sign, pulsus paradoxus measurement, Allen test, Buerger's test and angle, thrill detection, palpable heart sounds, Stemmer sign, expansile aortic pulsation.
- **Tier 3 (academic or historical):** Osler sign of pseudohypertension (prompts confirmation, never a conclusion); Hill sign (thresholds rest on small old studies); the aortic regurgitation eponym constellation — de Musset, Müller, Landolfi, Becker, Quincke, Traube, Duroziez — all of which restate one finding, a wide pulse pressure, and none of which adds to it.

### 3.3 Auscultation

**Technique** [E]

- **Diaphragm** for high-frequency sounds: S1, S2, ejection and mid-systolic clicks, most systolic murmurs, the early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation, and the opening snap.
- **Bell**, applied lightly, for low-frequency sounds: S3, S4, the mid-diastolic rumble of mitral stenosis, and the pericardial knock. Pressing the bell firmly converts it into a diaphragm and abolishes exactly the sounds you are listening for — a common and self-defeating error.
- Auscultate with the **stethoscope on skin**, in a quiet room, with your eyes on the JVP or the carotid so that you can **time** each sound against the cardiac cycle rather than guessing.

**Sequence and positioning** [E]

- Apex (mitral) — diaphragm, then bell.
- Lower left sternal edge, fourth space (tricuspid).
- Left upper sternal edge, second space (pulmonary).
- Right upper sternal edge, second space (aortic).
- **Left lateral decubitus, bell at the apex** — for the mid-diastolic murmur of mitral stenosis and for S3. Ask the patient to breathe out and hold.
- **Sitting forward, diaphragm at the left sternal edge, in held expiration** — for the early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation and for a pericardial rub.
- **Axilla** — radiation of mitral regurgitation.
- **Carotids** — radiation of aortic stenosis.
- **Back, interscapular** — coarctation and collateral flow.
- **Lung bases** — crackles, wheeze, effusion.

**For every murmur, extract seven attributes** [E]

Timing (systolic, diastolic, continuous); duration and shape (ejection, pansystolic, decrescendo, mid-diastolic); site of maximal intensity; radiation; pitch and quality; grade on the Levine scale; and response to dynamic manoeuvres.

**Dynamic manoeuvres, in tiers** [E]

### Tier 1 — these change management:

- **Respiration (Carvallo sign).** Right-sided murmurs increase on inspiration; left-sided murmurs do not. Mechanism: inspiration augments right ventricular filling. This is the only reliable single manoeuvre for lateralising a murmur, and it separates tricuspid from mitral regurgitation.
- **Valsalva and standing versus squatting.** Reduced ventricular volume (Valsalva strain, standing) **increases** the murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with dynamic outflow obstruction and moves the mitral prolapse click earlier; increased volume (squatting, passive leg raise) **decreases** the HCM murmur and delays the click. Fixed aortic stenosis behaves in the **opposite** direction: the murmur of valvular aortic stenosis is augmented on squatting or following a premature beat, and its intensity is reduced during Valsalva strain [and see 93]. This differential response is the single highest-yield dynamic manoeuvre in cardiovascular examination, because it distinguishes two conditions with opposite management. Medscape
- **Positioning** — left lateral and sitting-forward, as above. Murmurs missed at 45° are routinely audible in the correct position; this is not a refinement but a requirement.

### Tier 2:

- **Isometric handgrip** — raises afterload, increasing regurgitant murmurs (mitral and aortic regurgitation) and decreasing the HCM murmur.
- **Post-extrasystolic or post-pause beat** — augments the aortic stenosis murmur.
- **Passive leg raise** — increases preload; a low-effort alternative to squatting in the frail or immobile.

### Tier 3 (specific indication, monitored setting only, not a routine bedside test):

- **Carotid sinus massage** — for suspected carotid sinus hypersensitivity in unexplained syncope. Requires ECG and blood pressure monitoring, resuscitation facilities, and exclusion of carotid bruit or recent stroke. This is a procedure, not part of routine examination.

### 3.4 Additional tests to complete the examination

[E] Fundoscopy (hypertensive retinopathy, Roth spots, Takayasu-type retinopathy); urinalysis and albumin:creatinine ratio (hypertensive and cardiorenal target-organ damage); temperature and observation charts; weight and daily weight trend; 12-lead ECG; ankle–brachial index; and, where trained and available, focused cardiac and lung ultrasound (see 3.6).

### 3.5 The evidence base — what the examination is actually worth

This is where trainees are usually misled in both directions, so the numbers matter.

**Aortic stenosis — the best-evidenced part of the cardiac examination** [E] for the figures; [L] for the underlying evidence quality, which the authors state explicitly

A systematic review and meta-analysis found that auscultating a diminished second heart sound (likelihood ratio 10.87, 95% CI 3.94–30.12) and palpating a delayed carotid upstroke (LR 9.04, 95% CI 3.12–25.44) are useful for detecting aortic stenosis of at least moderate severity, and the absence of a systolic murmur radiating to the neck (LR 0.11, 95% CI 0.06–0.23) rules against aortic stenosis of at least moderate severity [19]. The authors are candid that this is low-quality evidence from observational studies, supporting a diminished second heart sound and a delayed carotid upstroke as having moderate accuracy for the presence of at least moderate aortic stenosis, with absence of a murmur radiating to the neck equally accurate for exclusion [19]. ScienceDirectScienceDirect

### Three conclusions to carry to the bedside:

- The **most useful positive findings** are a diminished or absent A2 and a delayed carotid upstroke — not murmur loudness.
- The **most useful negative finding** is the absence of a murmur radiating to the neck, which is a genuine rule-out.
- In a single-centre study, the absence of the second heart sound had the highest area under the curve (0.85), followed by late-systolic murmur duration (0.79) and pulsus tardus grade ≥2 (0.77) for severe aortic stenosis [25] — and the same study found that murmur intensity ≥4 or a delayed carotid upstroke had great specificity but low sensitivity, while murmur intensity ≥3 or radiation to the apex or neck had high sensitivity [25]. High specificity with low sensitivity means: **when present, act; when absent, do not relax.** ScienceDirectScienceDirect

The overall calibration of cardiac auscultation: compared with echocardiography, auscultation has low sensitivity for detecting aortic valve lesions (56.6–73%) but high inter-examiner agreement and high specificity (92–98%) [26] [E]. This is the correct mental model for the whole cardiac examination — **specific, not sensitive**. It rules in; it does not rule out. European Society of Cardiology

**The third heart sound** [E] for the figures

A meta-analysis of 19 studies involving 5,614 participants found the pooled sensitivity of S3 for heart failure to be 0.23 (95% CI 0.15–0.33) and specificity 0.94 (95% CI 0.82–0.98), with a diagnostic odds ratio of 4.55, compared with sensitivity 0.70 and specificity 0.79 for left ventricular ejection fraction [20]. The authors conclude that S3 alone has lower sensitivity for diagnosing heart failure than LVEF, though it is useful in early pathological assessment [20]. nihnih

**Read that correctly, because it is the model for the whole examination:** an S3 has a sensitivity of roughly one in four and a specificity of roughly 94%. **Hearing one is powerful; not hearing one tells you almost nothing.** Three quarters of patients with heart failure will not have an audible S3. Any trainee who excludes heart failure because there was no S3 has misunderstood the test.

**Jugular venous assessment — where the traditional method loses to the probe** [E] for the figures

Jugular venous distension assessed **by ultrasound** in emergency department patients with dyspnoea had sensitivity 99% (95% CI 92.2–100), specificity 59% (95% CI 40.9–74.4), LR+ 2.4 (1.6–3.6), and LR− 0.01 (0.0007–0.20) for identifying heart failure against cardiology echocardiography as the criterion standard [21]. An LR− of 0.01 is a near-definitive rule-out — an operating characteristic that traditional visual inspection of the JVP does not achieve. This single comparison is the strongest argument in this part for learning focused ultrasound. PubMed

### 3.6 The current role of POCUS — augmenting or replacing traditional manoeuvres

[G]/[E] This is no longer optional knowledge for an internal medicine resident.

- **Where POCUS is now positioned:** point-of-care ultrasound has evolved from a diagnostic adjunct into an extension of bedside clinical reasoning in acute cardiovascular care, with lung ultrasound enabling reliable detection and monitoring of pulmonary congestion and outperforming both chest radiography and physical examination; the Venous Excess Ultrasound Score offers structured assessment of systemic venous congestion through abdominal venous Doppler; the left ventricular outflow tract velocity–time integral serves as a reproducible surrogate for forward flow; and focused cardiac ultrasound provides rapid structural and functional evaluation [24]. PubMed Central
- **Guideline anchoring:** POCUS use in acute myocardial infarction was emphasised in the 2025 Guideline on Cardiac Ultrasound in Cardiovascular Emergency and Critical Care, endorsed by the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging, for confirming diagnosis, establishing differential diagnoses, assessing global and regional ventricular function, and detecting early complications including mechanical complications and intraventricular thrombus [24] [G]. PubMed Central
- **Diagnostic accuracy in acute heart failure, from meta-analysis** [E]: lung POCUS using B-line patterns and pleural effusion gives sensitivity 80% and specificity 82%; cardiac POCUS parameters (E/A, E/e′, LVEF <40–<50%) plus a plethoric IVC give 79% and 79%; IVC ultrasound alone gives sensitivity 57% and specificity 89%, making it more confirmatory than screening [22]. ScienceDirect
- **Combination is where the power is** [E]: studies evaluating heart failure identification with POCUS found that diagnostic sensitivity and specificity may reach 90–100% when IVC measurement is coupled with lung ultrasound assessing B-lines or pleural effusion [23]. nih
- **Risk stratification** [E]: in STEMI, lung ultrasound detects B-lines more sensitively than physical examination; added to the Killip classification it improved risk prediction with a net reclassification improvement of 0.18, and the absence of B-lines — "dry lungs" — showed a 98.1% negative predictive value [24]. PubMed Central
- **What POCUS replaces, and what it does not** [E]:
  - **Effectively replaced:** percussion of the cardiac borders (already obsolete); visual estimation of the JVP where a probe is available, given the LR− of 0.01 for ultrasound JVD [21]; chest radiography as the first-line test for pulmonary congestion [24].
  - **Augmented, not replaced:** volume status assessment, which still requires the clinical picture, since IVC alone has a sensitivity of 57% [22].
  - **Not replaced:** murmur characterisation and dynamic manoeuvres, which remain the way a valve lesion is *detected* and triaged toward formal echocardiography; and the two-axis wet/dry, warm/cold haemodynamic profiling that directs treatment.
- **Honest limitation:** operator dependence. POCUS accuracy figures come from trained operators; an untrained resident with a probe is less reliable than a trained resident with a stethoscope. Seek supervised training before acting on your own images.

### 3.8 Documentation

**Normal examination** [E]

Comfortable at rest at 45°, no distress. No cyanosis, clubbing, splinter haemorrhages, or peripheral stigmata. Hands warm, capillary refill <2 s. Pulse 72/min, regular, normal volume and character; no radio-radial or radio-femoral delay. BP 118/74 right arm, 116/72 left arm. No xanthelasma or corneal arcus; conjunctivae not pale. JVP not elevated (<3 cm above sternal angle at 45°), normal waveform, hepatojugular reflux negative. Carotids normal upstroke, no bruits. Praecordium: no scars or visible pulsation. Apex 5th intercostal space, medial to midclavicular line, normal character. No parasternal heave, no thrills, no palpable heart sounds. S1 and S2 normal with physiological splitting; no S3, S4, click, snap, murmur, or rub in any position or with any manoeuvre. Chest clear, no crackles or effusion. Abdomen soft, liver not enlarged or pulsatile, no ascites, no aortic, renal, or femoral bruits, aortic pulsation not expansile. No peripheral oedema. All peripheral pulses present and symmetrical; no trophic changes or ulcers. Fundi normal. Urinalysis negative.

**Abnormal examination** [E]

Sitting forward, dyspnoeic at rest, speaking in short phrases, cool and clammy peripheries. Central cyanosis present. Pulse 108/min, irregularly irregular, low volume; apex-radial deficit of 14/min. BP 96/74 both arms; pulse pressure narrow. JVP elevated at 8 cm above the sternal angle at 45°, absent a waves, giant systolic v waves with obliterated x descent, sustained hepatojugular reflux. Apex displaced to the 6th intercostal space in the anterior axillary line, thrusting in character. Left parasternal heave present. S1 variable, S2 with loud P2. Grade 3/6 pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge, louder on inspiration, and a left-sided S3 at the apex on the bell in the left lateral position. No rub. Bibasal fine inspiratory crackles to mid-zones; stony dullness right base. Tender pulsatile hepatomegaly 5 cm below the costal margin; shifting dullness present. Pitting oedema to both thighs and over the sacrum. Impression: decompensated biventricular failure with atrial fibrillation and severe functional tricuspid regurgitation; cold-and-wet haemodynamic profile.

### 3.9 Errors, pitfalls, and difficult populations

[E]

- **Measuring the JVP at the wrong angle**, or measuring the oblique rather than the vertical distance. Both produce systematic error.
- **Mistaking carotid for venous pulsation** — apply the five discriminators.
- **A false-positive hepatojugular reflux from a Valsalva manoeuvre** — instruct the patient to keep breathing.
- **Assessing pulse character at the radial artery.**
- **Equating murmur loudness with severity.** A critically stenotic valve with a failing ventricle is quiet; a trivial lesion in a hyperdynamic circulation is loud.
- **Pressing the bell firmly**, abolishing the low-frequency sounds you are seeking.
- **Omitting the left lateral and sitting-forward positions**, then recording "no murmur."
- **Excluding heart failure on the absence of an S3 or of crackles.** Sensitivity of S3 is approximately 0.23 [20].
- **Attributing ankle oedema to heart failure in a patient on a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker** — check the JVP before starting a diuretic.
- **Failing to roll a bed-bound patient**, thereby missing sacral oedema and a sacral pressure ulcer.
- **Anatomical variants that mimic pathology:** dextrocardia (impalpable left apex); pectus excavatum and scoliosis (displaced apex, innocent murmur from right ventricular outflow proximity); a prominent aorta in a thin patient mimicking aneurysm; venous hum in a child mimicking a continuous pathological murmur.
- **Difficult populations:** obesity (impalpable apex, muffled sounds, inaccurate cuff readings with an undersized cuff, and — relevant to Section Fourth — lower natriuretic peptide concentrations); emphysema (hyperinflation muffling all sounds and displacing the JVP interpretation); the elderly (blunted pulsus parvus et tardus from arterial stiffening, so severe aortic stenosis may present with a normal pulse); the ventilated patient (reversed intrathoracic pressure relationships invalidate normal JVP and pulsus paradoxus interpretation); pregnancy (physiological S3, flow murmur, wide pulse pressure — do not over-investigate); children (physiological S3 and innocent murmurs are the norm).
- **Practical shortcuts that are legitimate:** the two-axis wet/dry, warm/cold assessment can be completed in under two minutes and directs treatment; a JVP plus apex character plus a single dynamic manoeuvre answers most ward questions; and if you can only do one thing before ordering an echocardiogram, palpate the carotid upstroke and listen for A2.

## Fourth: Investigations specific to the cardiovascular system

### 4.1 Laboratory

**High-sensitivity cardiac troponin** [E]

- Principle: immunoassay detection of cardiac-specific troponin I or T released from injured cardiomyocytes.
- Answers: has there been myocardial injury, and is it acute?
- Interpretation: requires a **rise and/or fall** on serial sampling plus clinical context. An elevated troponin means myocardial injury, **not necessarily infarction**; the distinction is definitional and clinical.
- Non-ACS causes to hold in mind: heart failure, myocarditis, tachyarrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, chronic kidney disease, hypertensive emergency, Takotsubo, cardiac contusion, strenuous exercise.
- **Assay-specific thresholds and rule-out/rule-in pathways are not reproduced here.** They differ between assays and between guidelines, and I did not retrieve them. The current US framework is the 2025 ACC/AHA ACS guideline [15] [G]. **Use your laboratory's assay-specific pathway, not a remembered number.**

**Natriuretic peptides — BNP and NT-proBNP** [E]

- Principle: released from ventricular myocardium in response to wall stress; NT-proBNP is the inactive cleavage fragment with a longer half-life.
- **Rule-out thresholds** [E]: in acute heart failure, NT-proBNP <300 pg/mL and BNP <100 pg/mL exclude the diagnosis; in chronic heart failure in patients without a prior diagnosis and not on treatment, NT-proBNP <125 pg/mL and BNP <35 pg/mL are the exclusion values [36]. The universal definition of heart failure specifies an ambulatory NT-proBNP of 125 pg/mL as corroborating evidence of heart failure when accompanied by symptoms or signs, and ESC guidelines specify the same rule-out threshold in the non-acute setting [35]. PubMed CentralPubMed Central
- **A genuine and clinically important divergence** [G], per Rule B7: NICE guidelines specify a rule-out threshold for chronic heart failure at NT-proBNP of 400 pg/mL, with referral for echocardiography above that level; the higher NICE threshold was chosen on the basis of a cost-effectiveness analysis [35]. So ESC/universal-definition and NICE **differ by more than threefold** on the same decision. This is a cost-effectiveness-driven divergence exactly of the kind Rule B7 flags. **Confirm which threshold your laboratory and unit report against** — a patient with an NT-proBNP of 200 pg/mL is referred under one framework and discharged under the other. PubMed Central
- **Performance at these thresholds** [E]: in a large linked-data diagnostic accuracy study of 155,347 patients with NT-proBNP tests of whom 14,585 (9.4%) were diagnosed with heart failure, the single ESC threshold of ≥125 pg/mL had sensitivity 94.6% (94.2–95.0) and specificity 50.0% (49.7–50.3); the ESC Heart Failure Association age-adjusted rule-in thresholds of ≥125, ≥250, and ≥500 pg/mL for ages <50, 50–74, and ≥75 respectively had reduced sensitivity (83.5%, 88.5%, 84.4%) but increased specificity (77.6%, 67.8%, 63.5%); and a high-risk threshold of ≥2000 pg/mL had sensitivity 38.9% (38.1–39.7) and specificity 96.1% (96.0–96.2) [34]. A high BMI was associated with lower sensitivity at each age-adjusted threshold [34]. nihnih
- **Confounders you must know** [E]:
  - **Raise the level** independently of heart failure: increasing age, female sex, renal impairment, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, anaemia.
  - **Lower the level**, causing false reassurance: **obesity** [34], and — this one is regularly missed — **flash pulmonary oedema** with too short a time for peptide release.
  - **Drug-specific trap** [E]: BNP, but not NT-proBNP, is a substrate for neprilysin, so an angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor can increase BNP levels but not NT-proBNP levels [and see 142]. In a patient on sacubitril/valsartan, **BNP is uninterpretable and NT-proBNP must be used.** Onlinejcf
  - NT-proBNP ≥125 pg/mL is frequently observed in many demographic groups, including around 10% of young females, many of whom are unlikely to have preclinical heart failure [35] — so this threshold is a referral trigger in the symptomatic, not a screening test in the well. AHA Journals

**Lipids and atherogenic lipoproteins** [G]

Per the 2026 ACC/AHA/Multisociety dyslipidemia guideline, which retires and replaces the 2018 AHA/ACC blood cholesterol guideline and is retitled to reflect the evolving understanding of ASCVD risk associated with atherogenic lipoproteins beyond LDL-C, including triglyceride-rich remnant particles and lipoprotein(a) [29]: American Heart Association

- Standard lipid profile — total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides; fasting not required for risk assessment.
- **Lipoprotein(a)** — at least one measurement is advised to help identify patients at elevated cardiovascular risk [30]. Genetically determined and largely unmodifiable by lifestyle, which is why once is usually enough. Fshp
- **Apolipoprotein B** — expanded role in the 2026 guideline [29].
- Screening ages and intervals are in Section Fifth.

**Other laboratory investigations of the cardiovascular system** [E]

- **Renal:** creatinine, eGFR, electrolytes (potassium especially, given RAAS inhibitors, diuretics, and digoxin), urea. Urine albumin:creatinine ratio for target-organ damage.
- **Glycaemic:** fasting glucose, HbA1c.
- **Full blood count:** anaemia as a cause and a consequence; platelet count before antithrombotics.
- **Thyroid function:** reversible cause of atrial fibrillation and of high-output failure.
- **Iron studies (ferritin and transferrin saturation):** iron deficiency is common in heart failure and independently limits exercise capacity.
- **Liver function and albumin:** congestive hepatopathy; baseline before statins and amiodarone.
- **D-dimer:** used within a validated pre-test probability pathway for venous thromboembolism, never in isolation. The current US framework for pulmonary embolism is the 2026 AHA/ACC multisociety guideline [14] [G].
- **Blood cultures — at least three sets from separate sites before antibiotics** when endocarditis is suspected.
- **Inflammatory markers** — ESR, CRP for pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis, large-vessel vasculitis.
- **Genetic testing** — for inherited cardiomyopathies, channelopathies, familial hypercholesterolaemia, and heritable thoracic aortopathy. Requires counselling and specialist involvement, not a blood tube sent speculatively.

### 4.2 Electrophysiological investigations

**12-lead ECG** [E]

- Principle: surface recording of the summed cardiac electrical vector across 12 spatial leads.
- Answers: rhythm, rate, conduction, axis, chamber hypertrophy, ischaemia and infarction with territory, pericarditis, electrolyte effects, drug effects, channelopathy patterns, device function.
- Systematic interpretation sequence: patient identifiers and date → calibration and paper speed → rate → rhythm → P waves → PR interval → QRS axis → QRS duration and morphology → Q waves → ST segments → T waves → QT interval with rate correction → chamber hypertrophy criteria → comparison with any previous ECG.
- **Comparison with a previous ECG is the single highest-yield step** and is the one most often skipped.
- Artefacts and pitfalls: limb lead reversal (producing apparent right axis deviation with an inverted P in lead I); poor electrode contact mimicking ST change; tremor and shivering mimicking arrhythmia; misplaced V1–V2 too high, mimicking anterior Q waves or an incomplete right bundle branch block pattern.
- **Interpretive thresholds — voltage criteria for hypertrophy, QT correction cut-offs, ST elevation criteria by lead and sex — are guideline- and criterion-dependent and were not retrieved. They are therefore omitted** [?]. Use your local ECG criteria reference.

**Ambulatory rhythm monitoring** [E]

- **24-hour to 7-day Holter** — for frequent symptoms.
- **Patch and extended external monitors** — for less frequent symptoms.
- **Event and transtelephonic monitors** — patient-triggered.
- **Implantable loop recorder** — for infrequent, high-risk events such as unexplained syncope; monitoring over years.
- **Consumer wearables and smartwatch single-lead ECG** — increasingly present in the consultation. Useful for capturing paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but generate false positives and patient anxiety; a wearable trace is a prompt for a formal ECG, not a diagnosis [L].
- **The selection principle:** match the monitoring duration to the symptom frequency. A 24-hour Holter for monthly palpitations is a wasted investigation, and its normality is meaningless.

**Provocative and functional electrophysiological testing** [E]

- **Exercise ECG** — functional capacity, exercise-induced arrhythmia, chronotropic response, blood pressure response, and exercise-induced ischaemia. Its role in ischaemia diagnosis has narrowed substantially in favour of imaging; it retains value for functional capacity and rhythm.
- **Tilt-table testing** — for suspected reflex syncope where the history is not diagnostic.
- **Carotid sinus massage** — under monitoring, for carotid sinus hypersensitivity.
- **Invasive electrophysiology study** — for mechanism definition and mapping before ablation, and for selected risk stratification.
- **Signal-averaged ECG** — largely obsolete [L].

### 4.3 Imaging

**Chest radiograph** [E]

- Answers: cardiac size and contour, pulmonary venous congestion and oedema, pleural effusion, mediastinal widening, valve calcification, prosthetic hardware and lead position, rib notching in coarctation, alternative causes of breathlessness.
- Limitation: lung ultrasound outperforms chest radiography for detecting and monitoring pulmonary congestion [24]. PubMed Central

**Echocardiography — the pivotal test of the system** [E]

- **Transthoracic (TTE):** chamber dimensions and volumes, systolic and diastolic function, ejection fraction and global longitudinal strain, valve structure and function with gradients and regurgitant quantification, pericardium and effusion, tamponade physiology, pulmonary pressure estimation, intracardiac masses and thrombus, shunt detection.
- **Transoesophageal (TOE):** higher resolution for endocarditis vegetations and abscess, prosthetic valve assessment, left atrial appendage thrombus before cardioversion or occlusion, aortic dissection, and small shunts.
- **Stress echocardiography** — exercise or dobutamine, for inducible ischaemia, contractile reserve in low-flow low-gradient aortic stenosis, and dynamic mitral regurgitation.
- **Contrast (agitated saline)** — for shunt detection, including the platypnoea–orthodeoxia patient of Part 1.

## Fifth: Clinical assessment of cardiovascular health, and screening programmes

### 5.1 What "a healthy cardiovascular system" actually means

**The central conceptual point, and it is not semantic:** absence of symptoms is not cardiovascular health. The 2026 Second Universal Definition of Heart Failure makes this structural: the document reaffirms the four-stage continuum A to D and centres Stage B (pre-heart failure) as the critical window for early detection, close monitoring, and proactive individualised intervention — a strategic pivot away from a guideline focus dominated by symptomatic heart failure, given that the majority of the population resides in stages 0, A, or B [16] [G]. American Heart Association

That reframing tells you what to assess in a person who feels well: not "are there symptoms" but **which stage of the continuum are they on, and what is their risk trajectory.**

**Positive criteria for cardiovascular health, as far as they can be assessed clinically** [E]

- **Functional capacity** — can they climb two flights of stairs, or walk briskly on the flat, without symptoms? Functional capacity is the single most informative question in the history and the strongest bedside prognostic marker.
- **Blood pressure** in the non-elevated range on properly measured, confirmed readings (thresholds diverge — see 5.3).
- **Rhythm** — regular pulse; no history of palpitations, syncope, or presyncope.
- **No structural disease** — no murmur, no displaced or abnormal apex, no S3 or S4, no elevated JVP, no oedema.
- **No target-organ damage** — normal fundi, normal renal function and albumin:creatinine ratio, no left ventricular hypertrophy on ECG.
- **Vascular integrity** — symmetrical pulses, no bruits, no claudication, normal ABI where indicated.
- **Risk factor profile** — lipids, glucose, weight, smoking status, physical activity, sleep, alcohol.
- **Family history** — premature ASCVD, sudden death under 40, inherited cardiomyopathy or channelopathy, familial hypercholesterolaemia, heritable aortopathy. A family history taken properly is a screening test in itself and costs nothing.

**The three ways an apparently healthy cardiovascular system deceives you** [E]

- **Silent ischaemia** — objectively documented ischaemia with no perceived symptom, especially in diabetes, the elderly, women, CKD, and after transplantation (Part 1, item 3).
- **Asymptomatic structural disease** — severe aortic stenosis, severe mitral regurgitation, and moderate left ventricular dysfunction can all be asymptomatic because patients unconsciously reduce activity. Ask what they *used to* be able to do.
- **Stage B (pre-heart failure)** — structural or biomarker abnormality without symptoms, which is precisely where the 2026 Universal Definition directs attention [16].

## Sixth: Diseases of the cardiovascular system

### A. Ischaemic heart disease

**Chronic coronary syndrome (stable angina)** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* fixed atherosclerotic stenosis limiting coronary flow reserve; supply–demand mismatch reproducible at a fixed workload.
- *Typical presentation:* exertional retrosternal discomfort, reproducible at a consistent threshold, relieved within minutes by rest or nitrates.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **reproducibility at a fixed exertional threshold** — the pattern that separates it from the crescendo, unpredictable pattern of ACS.

**Acute coronary syndrome spectrum (unstable angina / NSTEMI / STEMI)** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombus → abrupt supply reduction. STEMI reflects complete occlusion with transmural injury (ST elevation); NSTEMI reflects subtotal occlusion or distal embolisation.
- *Typical presentation:* new, prolonged, rest, or crescendo ischaemic chest discomfort with autonomic features.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **the 12-lead ECG at presentation** — ST elevation mandates immediate reperfusion; its absence routes the patient through the troponin-based NSTEMI/UA pathway. Current US framework: 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline, which consolidated the previously separate STEMI and NSTEMI documents <a class="src">[19]</a>.

**MINOCA (myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries)** [L]

- *Pathogenesis:* heterogeneous — coronary vasospasm, microvascular dysfunction, plaque disruption without flow-limiting stenosis, or an unrecognised non-atherosclerotic cause (myocarditis, Takotsubo) misclassified as MI.
- *Typical presentation:* troponin-positive ACS with a normal or near-normal angiogram.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **diagnosis of exclusion** — requires exclusion of myocarditis and Takotsubo, typically by cardiac MRI, before the label is accepted.

**Vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* focal epicardial coronary vasospasm, often at rest, endothelium-dependent.
- *Typical presentation:* rest pain, classically nocturnal or early morning, with transient ST elevation that resolves with the pain.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **transient ST elevation during pain that normalises between episodes**, in a patient without a flow-limiting stenosis on angiography.

**Coronary microvascular dysfunction** [L]

- *Pathogenesis:* impaired vasodilator reserve at the level of the microcirculation, angiographically invisible.
- *Typical presentation:* exertional angina with objective ischaemia but non-obstructive epicardial coronaries.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **abnormal coronary flow reserve or index of microcirculatory resistance on invasive physiological testing**, in the absence of epicardial disease.

### B. Heart failure

**HFrEF (ejection fraction ≤40%)** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* loss of contractile mass (ischaemic, toxic, genetic) or chronic volume/pressure overload → adverse remodelling, neurohormonal activation (RAAS, sympathetic, natriuretic peptide systems).
- *Typical presentation:* the full congestive and low-output symptom set from Part 1.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **measured LVEF ≤40% on imaging**, which is what licenses the guideline-directed medical therapy quadruple pillars.

**HFpEF (ejection fraction ≥50%)** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* impaired ventricular relaxation and compliance (diastolic dysfunction), frequently in the context of hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and ageing; increasingly recognised as a systemic, often inflammatory, multi-organ syndrome.
- *Typical presentation:* exertional dyspnoea and congestion with a preserved ejection fraction; frequently under-recognised because the heart "looks normal."
- *Distinguishing feature:* **elevated left ventricular filling pressures (E/e′, natriuretic peptide) with a normal EF** — the diagnosis is made by demonstrating diastolic abnormality, not by ejection fraction alone.

**HFmrEF and HFimpEF** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* mildly reduced EF (41–49%) shares features of both phenotypes; HFimpEF is HFrEF that has improved to >40% with treatment but retains the underlying substrate and relapse risk.
- *Typical presentation:* variable; HFimpEF patients are often asymptomatic on therapy.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **HFimpEF requires a documented prior LVEF ≤40%** — stopping therapy in these patients causes relapse, which is the key practical trap.
- The 2026 Second Universal Definition of Heart Failure reframes the whole system around the A–D staging continuum, with **Stage B (pre-heart failure)** as the pivotal window for early intervention <a class="src">[21]</a>. [G]

### C. Valvular heart disease

**Aortic stenosis** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* degenerative calcific disease (most common in the elderly) or bicuspid valve disease (younger patients) → progressive leaflet fibrosis and calcification → fixed outflow obstruction.
- *Typical presentation:* the classic triad — exertional dyspnoea, angina, syncope — appearing late, after a long asymptomatic latent period.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **pulsus parvus et tardus with a diminished or absent A2** — the most specific combination on examination; confirmed by echocardiographic gradient and valve area.
- The 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular heart disease guideline lowered the **TAVI age threshold from 75 to 70 years** in tricuspid aortic valve stenosis and extended intervention criteria to some asymptomatic patients based on flow and LVEF reserve <a class="src">[40,41]</a>. [G]

**Aortic regurgitation** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* leaflet disease (bicuspid, endocarditis, rheumatic) or aortic root disease (dilatation, dissection, aortitis, Marfan) causing diastolic backflow.
- *Typical presentation:* often silent for years due to compensatory LV dilatation; eventually exertional dyspnoea as LV function declines.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **wide pulse pressure with a collapsing pulse and an early diastolic decrescendo murmur** — the entire eponym family in Part 2 restates this one mechanism.
- 2025 ESC/EACTS: TAVI may now be considered in symptomatic inoperable patients with suitable anatomy; surgical thresholds in asymptomatic patients clarified by LVESDi, LVESVi, or LVEF cut-offs <a class="src">[40]</a>. [G]

**Mitral stenosis** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* rheumatic commissural fusion (overwhelmingly the cause worldwide) → fixed diastolic inflow obstruction.
- *Typical presentation:* exertional dyspnoea, atrial fibrillation, haemoptysis, in a patient often from a region with a high rheumatic fever burden.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **mid-diastolic rumble with presystolic accentuation and an opening snap**, with a shorter A2–OS interval indicating more severe stenosis.
- 2025 ESC/EACTS: transcatheter mitral valve implantation now emerges as an option in severe mitral annular calcification with high surgical risk <a class="src">[40]</a>. [G]

**Mitral regurgitation — primary versus secondary, a distinction the 2025 guideline sharpens** [G]

- *Pathogenesis (primary):* structural leaflet or chordal disease (prolapse, flail, rheumatic, endocarditis).
- *Pathogenesis (secondary):* normal leaflets, annular or ventricular dilatation distorting geometry — now split into **atrial** (from AF-driven annular dilatation) and **ventricular** (from LV dysfunction) phenotypes with different prognosis and management <a class="src">[40]</a>.
- *Typical presentation:* exertional dyspnoea; primary MR often has a longer asymptomatic phase than secondary MR, which tracks the underlying ventricle.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **the atrial-versus-ventricular secondary MR split is now guideline-mandated** because it determines whether AF ablation/LAA occlusion, TEER, or GDMT optimisation is the primary lever <a class="src">[40]</a>.
- Surgical repair criteria for asymptomatic primary MR without LV dysfunction were advanced when **three or more risk factors** (AF, pulmonary hypertension, significant LA dilatation, concomitant TR) are present <a class="src">[40]</a>. [G]

**Tricuspid regurgitation** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* usually secondary to right ventricular or annular dilatation (pulmonary hypertension, left heart disease, AF), or lead-related in patients with cardiac devices.
- *Typical presentation:* right heart failure signs — raised JVP with giant v waves, pulsatile liver, ascites, peripheral oedema.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **giant systolic v waves with an obliterated x descent** on the JVP, and a pulsatile liver.

### D. Cardiomyopathies

**Dilated cardiomyopathy** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* heterogeneous — genetic (titin, lamin A/C and others), viral/post-myocarditic, toxic (alcohol, anthracycline), peripartum, idiopathic — converging on chamber dilatation with systolic impairment.
- *Typical presentation:* progressive heart failure symptoms; may present with sudden arrhythmic death, especially in lamin A/C disease.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **LV dilatation with systolic dysfunction disproportionate to any identified loading condition**, after ischaemic and valvular causes are excluded. Current European framework: 2023 ESC cardiomyopathies guideline <a class="src">[49]</a>. [G]

**Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* sarcomeric protein gene mutations (most commonly *MYH7*, *MYBPC3*) causing myocyte hypertrophy, disarray, and fibrosis, with or without dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.
- *Typical presentation:* often asymptomatic and detected incidentally or through family screening; exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, palpitations, or syncope when symptomatic; **sudden cardiac death may be the first presentation**, particularly in young athletes.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **a mid-to-late systolic murmur that increases with Valsalva/standing and decreases with squatting/handgrip** — the opposite dynamic response to fixed aortic stenosis. The 2024 AHA/ACC/AMSSM/HRS/PACES/SCMR guideline (reaffirmed current as of May 2025) introduced **mavacamten**, a cardiac myosin inhibitor, as an option before invasive septal reduction therapy for symptomatic obstructive HCM <a class="src">[45,47]</a>, and directs exclusion of **phenocopies** (Danon disease, Fabry disease, RASopathies) before the label is applied <a class="src">[48]</a>.

**Restrictive and infiltrative cardiomyopathy (amyloid, sarcoid, haemochromatosis)** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* abnormal material infiltrates the myocardial interstitium (amyloid fibrils, non-caseating granulomas, iron) → impaired relaxation with preserved or near-preserved systolic function until late.
- *Typical presentation:* heart failure with a "stiff heart" physiology — often HFpEF-labelled initially — plus extracardiac clues (carpal tunnel syndrome and macroglossia in amyloid; heart block or uveitis in sarcoid; skin bronzing and diabetes in haemochromatosis).
- *Distinguishing feature:* **low-voltage ECG disproportionate to wall thickness on echocardiography** is the classic amyloid clue; **bone-tracer scintigraphy** now allows non-biopsy diagnosis of transthyretin amyloidosis specifically.

**Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* desmosomal gene mutations → fibrofatty replacement of right ventricular (and often left ventricular) myocardium.
- *Typical presentation:* exercise-related palpitations, syncope, or sudden death in a young athlete; may mimic RV outflow tract VT.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **epsilon waves and localised right precordial T-wave inversion** on ECG, with regional RV wall motion abnormality on imaging.

**Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* catecholamine surge–mediated transient myocardial stunning, typically apical, following severe emotional or physical stress.
- *Typical presentation:* acute chest pain and dyspnoea mimicking STEMI, often in a postmenopausal woman after an acute stressor.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **apical ballooning with basal hyperkinesis on ventriculography/echocardiography, with non-obstructive coronaries** — a MINOCA subtype resolved by imaging, not angiography alone.

**Peripartum cardiomyopathy** [L]

- *Pathogenesis:* incompletely understood; implicated mechanisms include anti-angiogenic cleaved prolactin fragments and vascular dysfunction in the peripartum period.
- *Typical presentation:* new heart failure in the last month of pregnancy or within months postpartum, without another identifiable cause.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **temporal relationship to pregnancy** in the absence of pre-existing cardiomyopathy — a diagnosis of exclusion and timing.

### E. Arrhythmias and conduction disease

**Atrial fibrillation and flutter** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* chaotic atrial electrical activity (AF) or a re-entrant macro-circuit (flutter, classically around the tricuspid annulus), usually on a substrate of atrial stretch, fibrosis, or inflammation.
- *Typical presentation:* palpitations, fatigue, or entirely asymptomatic, detected incidentally or after a thromboembolic event.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **irregularly irregular pulse with absent JVP a waves and an apex-radial deficit** for AF; flutter is often regular at a stereotyped rate (commonly around 150/min with 2:1 block) and shows sawtooth flutter waves on ECG. Frameworks diverge: ESC/EACTS 2024 AF-CARE structure versus the 2023 ACC/AHA AF guideline.

**Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (AVNRT/AVRT)** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* re-entry within or involving the AV node (AVNRT) or via an accessory pathway (AVRT, e.g. Wolff–Parkinson–White).
- *Typical presentation:* abrupt-onset, abrupt-offset rapid regular palpitations, often terminated by vagal manoeuvres.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **abrupt onset and offset** distinguishes it from the gradual ramp of sinus tachycardia; neck pounding (cannon waves) favours AVNRT specifically.

**Ventricular tachycardia and channelopathies** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* re-entry around scar (post-infarct VT), triggered activity, or an inherited ion-channel abnormality (long QT, Brugada, catecholaminergic polymorphic VT) predisposing to malignant arrhythmia in a structurally normal heart.
- *Typical presentation:* syncope without prodrome, palpitations with near-syncope, or cardiac arrest; channelopathies often present in the young with exertional or emotional triggers.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **wide-complex tachycardia with AV dissociation, capture or fusion beats** confirms VT over SVT with aberrancy; a resting ECG showing a Brugada pattern or QT prolongation identifies the channelopathy between events.

**Sinus node disease and AV block** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* degenerative fibrosis of the sinus node or conduction system, or reversible causes (drugs, ischaemia, high vagal tone, electrolyte disturbance).
- *Typical presentation:* fatigue, presyncope, or syncope; may be entirely asymptomatic on ECG (first-degree, Mobitz I).
- *Distinguishing feature:* **Mobitz II and third-degree (complete) block carry a high risk of progression to asystole** and are the ones that mandate pacing consideration, unlike the generally benign first-degree and Mobitz I patterns.

### F. Pericardial and myocardial disease

**Acute pericarditis** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* viral (most common), autoimmune, uraemic, neoplastic, or post-cardiac injury (Dressler-type) inflammation of the pericardium.
- *Typical presentation:* sharp pleuritic chest pain, positional, relieved sitting forward.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression across multiple territories** on ECG, and a friction rub that may be evanescent. Governing document: the 2025 ESC guideline on myocarditis and pericarditis, the first to cover both conditions together.

**Pericardial effusion and tamponade** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* fluid accumulation exceeding pericardial compliance → impaired diastolic filling once intrapericardial pressure approaches right atrial pressure.
- *Typical presentation:* dyspnoea, hypotension, and elevated JVP — Beck's triad in its full but uncommon form.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **pulsus paradoxus with a blunted y descent**, echocardiographic RV diastolic collapse, and IVC plethora — the single mechanism is ventricular interdependence in a fixed pericardial volume.

**Constrictive pericarditis** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* fibrotic, often calcified, rigid pericardium restricting diastolic filling after unimpeded early filling.
- *Typical presentation:* right heart failure with ascites disproportionate to peripheral oedema; frequently a delayed sequela of tuberculous or post-surgical pericarditis.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **Kussmaul sign with a rapid deep y descent (Friedreich sign) and a pericardial knock** — the opposite JVP signature to tamponade.

**Myocarditis** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* viral infection or immune-mediated myocardial inflammation, occasionally fulminant.
- *Typical presentation:* chest pain, heart failure, or arrhythmia following a viral prodrome; can mimic ACS with troponin rise and non-obstructive coronaries.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **late gadolinium enhancement in a non-coronary distribution on cardiac MRI**, distinguishing it from infarction. Governing document: 2025 ESC myocarditis/pericarditis guideline.

### G. Infective disease

**Infective endocarditis** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* bacteraemia seeding a damaged, prosthetic, or device-bearing endocardial surface → vegetation formation with local destruction and systemic embolic/immune phenomena.
- *Typical presentation:* fever with a new or changing murmur, often with embolic or immunological peripheral stigmata (Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, splinter haemorrhages, Roth spots).
- *Distinguishing feature:* **vegetation on echocardiography plus persistently positive blood cultures for a typical organism** — the core of the Duke-family criteria. Current European framework: 2023 ESC endocarditis guideline, refining prophylaxis, diagnostics, outpatient therapy, and device-related cases <a class="src">[49]</a>. The 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria improved sensitivity over the 2000 Modified Duke criteria but at a substantial cost to specificity in external validation <a class="src">[50,51]</a>. [L] for the trade-off's clinical implication.

**Rheumatic heart disease** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* post-streptococcal (group A) molecular mimicry driving immune-mediated valvular damage, cumulative over repeated episodes of acute rheumatic fever.
- *Typical presentation:* mitral stenosis (classically) or mixed mitral/aortic disease in a young adult from a high-burden region, often with a history of childhood polyarthritis or chorea.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **mitral stenosis in a young patient without degenerative risk factors**, from a region with a high rheumatic burden — the epidemiological pattern is itself diagnostic-directing.

**Cardiac implantable device infection** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* biofilm-forming organisms (commonly staphylococci) colonising the generator pocket or leads, with potential extension to lead-associated endocarditis.
- *Typical presentation:* pocket erythema and discharge, or fever with bacteraemia and no other source in a device-bearing patient.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **lead vegetation on transoesophageal echocardiography** in a bacteraemic device patient — usually mandates device extraction, not antibiotics alone.

### H. Vascular disease

**Hypertension and hypertensive heart disease** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* chronically elevated afterload → concentric LV hypertrophy → diastolic dysfunction → eventually systolic failure; vascular remodelling drives target-organ damage elsewhere (retina, kidney, brain).
- *Typical presentation:* often silent until target-organ damage or a hypertensive emergency; category and thresholds diverge between ESC 2024 and AHA/ACC 2025.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **left ventricular hypertrophy pattern on ECG or echocardiography in a patient with sustained elevated readings**, once secondary causes are excluded.

**Aortic dissection and aneurysm** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* medial degeneration (hypertension, ageing, connective tissue disease) weakens the aortic wall; dissection follows an intimal tear, aneurysm follows progressive dilatation without a tear.
- *Typical presentation:* dissection — abrupt maximal-onset tearing pain, often migrating; aneurysm — usually silent until rupture or incidental detection.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **instantaneous maximal pain intensity with migration** for dissection versus the typically silent, incidentally discovered course of unruptured aneurysm. Current European framework: the 2024 ESC guideline on peripheral arterial and aortic diseases, the first to merge aortic and peripheral arterial disease into one document <a class="src">[14–17]</a>. [G]

**Peripheral arterial disease** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* atherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion of limb arteries, sharing risk factors with coronary and cerebrovascular disease.
- *Typical presentation:* intermittent claudication, or, in advanced disease, rest pain and tissue loss.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **an ankle–brachial index below the normal range**, which the 2024 ESC guideline confirms as the initial diagnostic and screening test, doubling as a surrogate marker for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality <a class="src">[56]</a>. Falsely normal in medial arterial calcification (diabetes, CKD) — see Part 3.

**Venous thromboembolism (DVT/PE)** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* Virchow's triad — stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability — precipitating venous thrombosis, with embolisation to the pulmonary circulation.
- *Typical presentation:* unilateral limb swelling and pain (DVT); pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, or syncope (PE), severity ranging from asymptomatic to obstructive shock.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **unilaterality** for DVT versus cardiac oedema; for PE, **hypoxaemia with a clear chest X-ray** is the classic dissociation prompting the diagnosis. Current US framework: 2026 AHA/ACC multisociety acute pulmonary embolism guideline.

**Chronic venous insufficiency and lymphoedema** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* venous — valvular incompetence causing ambulatory venous hypertension; lymphatic — impaired lymphatic transport with protein-rich fluid accumulation.
- *Typical presentation:* venous — aching, heaviness, medial malleolar ulceration; lymphatic — non-pitting swelling involving the toes.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **toe involvement with a positive Stemmer sign** identifies lymphoedema and separates it from venous or cardiac oedema, which spare the toes.

**Large-vessel vasculitis (Takayasu arteritis, giant cell arteritis)** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* granulomatous panarteritis, entering via the vasa vasorum, targeting the aorta and its primary branches.
- *Typical presentation:* constitutional illness with vascular ischaemic features in a young woman (Takayasu) or cranial ischaemic symptoms in a patient over 50 (GCA).
- *Distinguishing feature:* **age at onset (≤40 for Takayasu classification, ≥50 for GCA)** is the primary axis separating the two, per the full account already delivered in this project.

### I. Pulmonary circulation

**Pulmonary arterial hypertension and pulmonary hypertension groups 2–5** [G]

- *Pathogenesis:* Group 1 (PAH) — pulmonary vascular remodelling, idiopathic, heritable, or associated with connective tissue disease, HIV, or portal hypertension; Group 2 — left heart disease raising pulmonary venous pressure; Group 3 — chronic lung disease and hypoxia; Group 4 — chronic thromboembolic disease; Group 5 — miscellaneous.
- *Typical presentation:* progressive exertional dyspnoea, often with a considerable diagnostic delay because early symptoms are non-specific.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **right heart catheterisation remains the gold standard for diagnosis and group classification** <a class="src">[52]</a>, and the group assignment — not the pressure number alone — determines the entire treatment pathway. Current framework: 2022 ESC/ERS pulmonary hypertension guideline.

**Cor pulmonale** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* right ventricular hypertrophy and failure secondary to chronic pulmonary disease (Group 3 pulmonary hypertension), independent of left heart disease.
- *Typical presentation:* progressive dyspnoea with right heart failure signs in a patient with known chronic lung disease.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **right heart failure signs with a primary respiratory disease and no significant left heart disease** on echocardiography.

### J. Congenital heart disease in the adult

**Atrial septal defect** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* persistent interatrial communication → left-to-right shunt (usually), volume-loading the right heart.
- *Typical presentation:* often asymptomatic into adulthood; exertional dyspnoea or arrhythmia (AF/flutter) with age as the right heart dilates.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **fixed, wide splitting of S2 that does not vary with respiration** — the interatrial communication equalises respiratory effects on right ventricular filling.

**Ventricular septal defect** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* persistent interventricular communication; small defects are haemodynamically trivial, large defects risk Eisenmenger physiology.
- *Typical presentation:* small — asymptomatic with an incidental loud murmur; large, uncorrected — progressive pulmonary vascular disease and eventual shunt reversal.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **murmur loudness is inversely related to defect size** — a very large defect with equalised pressures can be nearly silent.

**Patent ductus arteriosus** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* failure of ductal closure → continuous aortopulmonary shunt.
- *Typical presentation:* often asymptomatic if small; a continuous "machinery" murmur is frequently the presenting finding.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **the continuous, machinery-like murmur** persisting through both systole and diastole (Gibson murmur) — the shunt gradient never falls to zero.

**Coarctation of the aorta** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* discrete narrowing, typically just distal to the left subclavian origin, producing proximal hypertension and distal hypoperfusion.
- *Typical presentation:* hypertension detected in a young person, sometimes with leg fatigue or claudication.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **radio-femoral delay with an arm-to-leg blood pressure gradient** (arm exceeding leg, the reverse of normal pulse-wave amplification).

**Bicuspid aortic valve** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* congenital two-leaflet aortic valve, predisposing to accelerated calcific stenosis and to aortopathy (root and ascending aortic dilatation) independent of the valve lesion.
- *Typical presentation:* incidental murmur or premature aortic stenosis/regurgitation; may present first with aortic dissection.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **an ejection click** (mobile abnormal valve, disappearing with calcification) plus **disproportionate ascending aortic dilatation** relative to the degree of valve disease — the aortopathy must be actively looked for, not inferred from the valve alone.

**Tetralogy of Fallot** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* right ventricular outflow obstruction, VSD, aortic override, and RV hypertrophy — the classic four-lesion complex.
- *Typical presentation:* cyanosis, squatting behaviour, and hypoxic spells in the unrepaired child; in the repaired adult, late pulmonary regurgitation and arrhythmia risk.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **relief of cyanosis with squatting** in the unrepaired patient — squatting raises systemic vascular resistance and reduces right-to-left shunting.

**Eisenmenger syndrome** [E]

- *Pathogenesis:* long-standing uncorrected left-to-right shunt → irreversible pulmonary vascular remodelling → shunt reversal to right-to-left.
- *Typical presentation:* cyanosis and clubbing developing late in a patient with a previously "asymptomatic" congenital shunt.
- *Distinguishing feature:* **shunt reversal — a formerly left-to-right shunt becoming right-to-left** as pulmonary vascular resistance exceeds systemic resistance; this is a physiological turning point, not merely a worsening of the same lesion.

## Seventh: Emergency Conditions of the Cardiovascular System

### 1. Acute ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI)

**Pathogenesis:** An occlusive thrombus superimposed on a ruptured or eroded coronary atherosclerotic plaque produces complete cessation of antegrade blood flow in the affected coronary artery, resulting in transmural myocardial ischemia. Without prompt reperfusion, myocardial necrosis progresses in a wavefront from the subendocardium to the epicardium over 3-6 hours. Irreversible myocyte injury begins after 20-30 minutes of total occlusion. The infarct size, location, and subsequent complications depend on the culprit artery and the duration of occlusion. Complications include: ventricular arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation is the most common cause of pre-hospital death), cardiogenic shock (loss of >40% of left ventricular myocardium), acute mitral regurgitation (papillary muscle rupture or dysfunction), ventricular septal rupture, free wall rupture (leading to cardiac tamponade and electromechanical dissociation), and left ventricular aneurysm. [1,5]

### Red Flags:

- Severe, persistent (>20 minutes) substernal chest pressure with diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, vomiting, and a sense of impending doom.
- ECG: ST-segment elevation ≥1 mm in two contiguous limb leads or ≥2 mm in two contiguous precordial leads, or new left bundle branch block. Posterior MI: ST depression in V1-V3 (upright T waves, tall R waves in V1-V2 indicate posterior wall MI; obtain V7-V9 to look for ST elevation). Right ventricular MI: ST elevation in V4R (right-sided precordial lead), with hypotension, clear lung fields, and elevated JVP.
- Hypotension, bradycardia, and clear lung fields suggest right ventricular MI (inferior MI extending to the right ventricle); these patients are preload-dependent and must NOT receive nitrates or diuretics as these will further reduce preload and precipitate cardiovascular collapse.
- A new, loud holosystolic murmur with acute pulmonary edema and shock after an acute MI suggests either papillary muscle rupture (acute severe mitral regurgitation) or ventricular septal rupture (left-to-right shunt). Immediate echocardiography is mandatory to differentiate these two mechanical complications, both of which require emergency surgical repair.
- Sudden electromechanical dissociation (pulseless electrical activity) after acute MI suggests free wall rupture with cardiac tamponade.

**Detection:** Immediate 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of presentation for all patients with chest pain or suspected ACS. Do not delay reperfusion therapy for troponin results if the ECG is diagnostic of STEMI. Emergency coronary angiography with primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the reperfusion strategy of choice if it can be performed within 120 minutes of first medical contact (door-to-balloon time ≤90 minutes in PCI-capable hospitals). If PCI is not available within 120 minutes, fibrinolytic therapy should be administered within 30 minutes of arrival (door-to-needle time ≤30 minutes), unless contraindicated. [5]

### 2. Cardiac Tamponade

**Pathogenesis:** Rapid or large accumulation of fluid in the pericardial space increases intrapericardial pressure to the point where it equals or exceeds cardiac chamber pressures, compressing the heart and preventing diastolic filling. Cardiac output and blood pressure fall. The patient is in a state of obstructive shock with low cardiac output, elevated central venous pressure, and clear lungs (no pulmonary edema, because the left ventricle cannot fill, so left atrial and pulmonary capillary pressures are not elevated—a key distinguishing feature from cardiogenic shock). Pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg is the hallmark physical finding. [1,11]

### Red Flags:

- Hypotension, tachycardia, tachypnea, markedly elevated JVP with a blunted or absent y descent, muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad). Pulsus paradoxus >12-15 mmHg.
- Clear lung fields on auscultation in a patient with shock and elevated JVP strongly suggests tamponade (or right ventricular infarction).
- Electrical alternans on ECG (alternating QRS amplitude due to the heart swinging in the pericardial fluid) is highly specific but not always present.
- Echocardiogram shows pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle, respiratory variation in mitral and tricuspid inflow velocities, and a dilated IVC with reduced inspiratory collapse. These are the diagnostic criteria, and echocardiography is the definitive bedside test.

**Detection:** Echocardiography is the key diagnostic test and can be performed emergently at the bedside. Cardiac tamponade is a clinical diagnosis, and in an unstable patient, immediate pericardiocentesis is lifesaving—do not wait for imaging confirmation if the clinical picture is classic and the patient is deteriorating. [1,11]

### 3. Acute Aortic Dissection

**Pathogenesis:** A tear in the aortic intima allows blood to enter the aortic media, creating a false lumen that propagates along the length of the aorta. The dissection can extend proximally (into the aortic root, coronary arteries, pericardium, aortic valve) or distally (into the arch, descending thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, and branch vessels). The Stanford classification: Type A (involves the ascending aorta, regardless of origin—surgical emergency) and Type B (does not involve the ascending aorta—usually managed medically unless complicated). Predisposing factors: hypertension (the most important risk factor), Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, aortitis (giant cell arteritis, Takayasu, syphilis), pregnancy, and trauma. The untreated mortality of Type A dissection is 1-2% per hour for the first 48 hours, reaching 50% by 48 hours. Causes of death: cardiac tamponade (retrograde dissection into the pericardial space), acute severe aortic regurgitation, myocardial infarction (dissection into coronary ostia), stroke (extension into carotid arteries), and aortic rupture. [1,5]

### Red Flags:

- Sudden, severe, "tearing" or "ripping" chest pain, often radiating to the back between the scapulae. The pain is maximal at onset, unlike MI pain, which builds gradually. The pain may migrate as the dissection propagates.
- Pulse deficit or asymmetry (difference in systolic BP >20 mmHg between arms suggests subclavian or brachiocephalic artery involvement).
- Aortic regurgitation murmur (new diastolic murmur in a patient with severe chest pain).
- Neurological deficits (carotid artery involvement causing stroke).
- Widened mediastinum on chest X-ray (present in 60-90% of cases, but its absence does not exclude dissection).
- ST-elevation myocardial infarction pattern on ECG can occur if the dissection involves the coronary ostium (typically the right coronary artery), leading to inferior STEMI. Administering thrombolytics or antiplatelet agents to a dissection patient mistakenly diagnosed as STEMI is catastrophic.
- Fibrinolysis is absolutely contraindicated if aortic dissection is in the differential diagnosis.

**Detection:** CT angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with IV contrast is the diagnostic test of choice (sensitivity 98-100%, specificity 98-100%). Transesophageal echocardiography is an alternative when CT is not immediately available or the patient is too unstable for transport (sensitivity 97-99%, performed at the bedside, can also assess aortic regurgitation and pericardial effusion). Immediate blood pressure control (beta-blocker first, usually esmolol, to reduce dP/dt, then vasodilators) and emergency cardiothoracic surgical consultation for Type A dissection are critical. [1,5]

### 4. Ventricular Fibrillation and Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (Cardiac Arrest)

**Pathogenesis:** Ventricular fibrillation (VF) is chaotic, uncoordinated ventricular electrical activity with no effective mechanical contraction. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a rapid, organized ventricular rhythm that fails to produce a perfusing cardiac output. Both result in sudden cardiac arrest: immediate loss of consciousness, absent pulses, absent breathing or agonal gasping, and death within minutes if not promptly treated. The most common underlying cause is acute myocardial ischemia or infarction, but other causes include: electrolyte disturbances (severe hyperkalemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia), structural heart disease (dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy), hereditary channelopathies (long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, catecholaminergic polymorphic VT), drug toxicity (proarrhythmic drugs, QT-prolonging agents, cocaine, digoxin toxicity), and commotio cordis (blunt chest trauma during a vulnerable period of the cardiac cycle). The Hs and Ts of cardiac arrest: Hypoxia, Hypovolemia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo-/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia, Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade (cardiac), Toxins, Thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary). [5,13]

### Red Flags:

- Sudden unresponsiveness, absent normal breathing, absent carotid pulse. The patient may have agonal gasps (not normal breathing).
- Immediate initiation of high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR): chest compressions at a rate of 100-120 per minute, depth of 5-6 cm (2-2.4 inches), allowing full chest recoil between compressions, minimizing interruptions.
- Early defibrillation is the single most important determinant of survival in VF/pulseless VT. For every minute without defibrillation, survival decreases by 7-10%. Immediate defibrillation (biphasic: 120-200 J; monophasic: 360 J) followed by immediate resumption of CPR for 2 minutes before rhythm check.
- Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes. Amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO bolus for refractory VF/pulseless VT, followed by 150 mg if needed.

**Detection:** Cardiac arrest is a clinical diagnosis—begin CPR immediately, do not delay for ECG or other tests. The ECG rhythm is identified when the defibrillator pads are applied: VF is a chaotic, irregular, disorganized rhythm with no identifiable QRS complexes; pulseless VT is a wide-complex, regular tachycardia with no palpable pulse. [5,13]

### 5. Acute Decompensated Heart Failure / Acute Pulmonary Edema

**Pathogenesis:** A rapid increase in left ventricular filling pressures (pulmonary capillary wedge pressure >25 mmHg) overwhelms the capacity of the pulmonary lymphatics to drain interstitial fluid, resulting in acute pulmonary edema. Fluid fills the pulmonary interstitium and alveoli, causing severe dyspnea, hypoxemia, and respiratory failure. Triggers include: acute myocardial ischemia, acute valvular regurgitation (papillary muscle rupture, chordal rupture, endocarditis), hypertensive emergency, arrhythmia (rapid atrial fibrillation), medication non-adherence, dietary indiscretion (high sodium intake), and acute kidney injury. The pathophysiology is a vicious cycle: hypoxemia and hypercapnia cause sympathetic activation and systemic vasoconstriction, further increasing left ventricular afterload and worsening the heart failure. [1,7]

### Red Flags:

- Severe dyspnea at rest, orthopnea, tachypnea, accessory muscle use, inability to speak in full sentences.
- Pink, frothy sputum (a sign of fulminant alveolar edema—protein-rich edema fluid mixed with air).
- Diffuse bilateral crackles and wheezes (cardiac asthma) on auscultation.
- Hypoxemia on pulse oximetry, progressing to hypercapnic respiratory failure (requires immediate non-invasive positive pressure ventilation [BiPAP/CPAP] or intubation).
- Cool, diaphoretic, ashen skin, hypotension (cardiogenic shock).
- S3 gallop, elevated JVP, hepatojugular reflux.

**Detection:** Clinical diagnosis based on history, physical exam, and chest X-ray. BNP/NT-proBNP is markedly elevated. Chest X-ray shows cardiomegaly, cephalization of pulmonary vessels, interstitial edema (Kerley B lines, peribronchial cuffing), and alveolar edema (perihilar "bat-wing" pattern, pleural effusions). Immediate treatment: upright positioning (dangle the legs to reduce venous return), supplemental oxygen, non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV reduces preload and afterload, improves oxygenation, and decreases the work of breathing), IV loop diuretics (furosemide: initial 40-80 mg IV bolus, or double the patient's usual oral dose if on chronic furosemide), IV nitrates (nitroglycerin infusion, primarily venodilator, reduces preload and relieves dyspnea within minutes), and morphine (reduces dyspnea and anxiety, but its routine use is controversial and may increase mortality). NIPPV reduces the need for intubation and improves survival in acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema. [1,7]

## Eighth: Pharmacological Drug Categories for the Cardiovascular System

### 1. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) Inhibitors

### ACE Inhibitors (Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors):

  - *Drug names and MOA:* "-pril" suffix. Captopril, Enalapril, Lisinopril, Ramipril. Mechanism: Competitively inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), which converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II and degrades bradykinin. This reduces angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor and stimulator of aldosterone secretion) and increases bradykinin (a vasodilator that also stimulates prostaglandin and nitric oxide synthesis). Net effect: arteriolar and venous vasodilation, reduced aldosterone secretion (decreased sodium and water retention, increased potassium), reduced sympathetic activity, and inhibition of cardiac and vascular remodeling. [3,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Dry cough (bradykinin and substance P accumulation; 5-20% of patients, more common in women and East Asians, class effect—switching to an ARB resolves the cough), angioedema (bradykinin-mediated; 0.1-0.7%; life-threatening airway swelling, can occur at any time during therapy, absolute contraindication to all ACE inhibitors), hyperkalemia (reduced aldosterone; monitor potassium and renal function), acute kidney injury (afferent arteriolar dilation is prostaglandin-dependent; in renal artery stenosis, hypovolemia, or NSAID use, ACE inhibition can precipitate acute renal failure), hypotension (first-dose hypotension is common, especially in volume-depleted patients), and teratogenicity (absolute contraindication in pregnancy—causes fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, and neonatal death). [3,14]

### ARBs (Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers):

  - *Drug names and MOA:* "-sartan" suffix. Losartan, Valsartan, Candesartan, Irbesartan. Mechanism: Selectively block the angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor, preventing angiotensin II-mediated vasoconstriction, aldosterone secretion, and sympathetic activation. Unlike ACE inhibitors, ARBs do not inhibit bradykinin degradation, so cough and angioedema are much less common. The clinical effects and indications are identical to ACE inhibitors. [3,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, hypotension, teratogenicity. Cough is not a side effect (AT1 receptor blockade does not affect bradykinin). Angioedema is very rare but can occur. [3,14]

### ARNI (Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor):

  - *Drug names and MOA:* Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto). Mechanism: Sacubitril is a neprilysin inhibitor. Neprilysin degrades natriuretic peptides (BNP, ANP), bradykinin, adrenomedullin, and other vasoactive peptides. Inhibiting neprilysin increases levels of these beneficial peptides (natriuresis, diuresis, vasodilation, anti-fibrotic effects). Valsartan blocks the AT1 receptor. The combination provides simultaneous RAAS blockade and augmentation of the natriuretic peptide system. [3,14]
  - *Indication:* Class I recommendation for HFrEF (LVEF ≤40%) to replace an ACE inhibitor or ARB, as it further reduces cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalizations compared to enalapril (PARADIGM-HF trial). [5]
  - *Key side effects:* Hypotension, hyperkalemia, angioedema (contraindicated with concomitant ACE inhibitor—must have a 36-hour washout period between stopping ACE inhibitor and starting sacubitril/valsartan to avoid angioedema from dual bradykinin accumulation). [3,14]

### Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs):

  - *Drug names and MOA:* Spironolactone (competitive aldosterone antagonist; also antiandrogen effects—gynecomastia, impotence) and Eplerenone (selective aldosterone antagonist without antiandrogen effects). Mechanism: Block the mineralocorticoid receptor in the distal tubule and collecting duct, inhibiting aldosterone-mediated sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion (mild diuretic). In heart failure, they also block the adverse effects of aldosterone on cardiac fibrosis, remodeling, and endothelial dysfunction—the mortality benefit is independent of their diuretic effect. [3,14]
  - *Indications:* Class I for HFrEF (LVEF ≤35%), post-MI with LVEF ≤40% and heart failure or diabetes, and resistant hypertension. [5]
  - *Key side effects:* Hyperkalemia (must monitor potassium and renal function; risk increases with concomitant ACE inhibitor/ARB, renal impairment, and in the elderly), gynecomastia and impotence (spironolactone only—due to androgen receptor antagonism and increased peripheral conversion of testosterone to estradiol). [3,14]

### 2. Beta-Adrenergic Receptor Blockers (Beta-Blockers)

- **Drug names and MOA:** "-olol" suffix. Metoprolol (beta-1 selective), Bisoprolol (beta-1 selective), Carvedilol (non-selective beta-blocker + alpha-1 blocker), Atenolol, Nebivolol (beta-1 selective + nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation). Mechanism: Competitive antagonists at beta-adrenergic receptors. Beta-1 blockade in the heart reduces heart rate (negative chronotropy), contractility (negative inotropy), and conduction velocity through the AV node (negative dromotropy). These effects reduce myocardial oxygen demand (the basis for anti-anginal efficacy) and improve survival in heart failure and post-MI (by reducing the toxic effects of chronic sympathetic overactivation, preventing arrhythmias, reducing myocardial apoptosis, and reversing adverse remodeling). Beta-2 blockade (non-selective agents) causes bronchoconstriction and peripheral vasoconstriction. Alpha-1 blockade (carvedilol) causes additional vasodilation. [3,14]
- **Key indications:** Coronary artery disease (angina, post-MI), heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), hypertension, supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation (rate control), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic dissection (reduce dP/dt), hyperthyroidism (symptomatic control). [5,14]
- **Key side effects:** Bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, fatigue, cold extremities, nightmares, erectile dysfunction, bronchospasm (non-selective agents), masking of hypoglycemia symptoms (tachycardia and tremor, but not sweating—caution in diabetes). Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound tachycardia, hypertension, and acute coronary ischemia (upregulation of beta-receptors during chronic blockade)—must be tapered. [3,14]

### 3. Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

- **Dihydropyridines:** "-dipine" suffix. Amlodipine, Nifedipine, Felodipine, Nicardipine. Mechanism: Block L-type calcium channels primarily in vascular smooth muscle, causing arteriolar vasodilation (potent antihypertensives). Minimal direct cardiac effects. Reflex tachycardia can occur (particularly with short-acting nifedipine, which is avoided). [3,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Peripheral edema (capillary leak from arteriolar dilation without venodilation—high capillary hydrostatic pressure; not due to heart failure and does not respond to diuretics; more common in women), headache, flushing, dizziness, gingival hyperplasia. [3,14]
- **Non-dihydropyridines:** Verapamil (phenylalkylamine) and Diltiazem (benzothiazepine). Mechanism: Block L-type calcium channels in both cardiac myocytes and vascular smooth muscle. Cardiac effects: negative chronotropy (slow sinus rate), negative dromotropy (slow AV conduction), and negative inotropy. Vascular effects: arteriolar vasodilation. Verapamil is more cardioselective and a more potent AV nodal blocker than diltiazem; diltiazem has intermediate effects. [3,14]
  - *Key indications:* Angina, hypertension, supraventricular tachycardia (rate control in atrial fibrillation/flutter, termination of AV nodal reentrant tachycardia). Verapamil is contraindicated in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction due to its negative inotropic effect, and in Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome with atrial fibrillation (can paradoxically increase ventricular rate by blocking the AV node and promoting conduction down the accessory pathway). [3,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Bradycardia, AV block, constipation (verapamil is the most constipating drug in common use), hypotension, worsening of heart failure (verapamil). [3,14]

### 4. Diuretics

- **Loop Diuretics:** Furosemide, Bumetanide, Torsemide. Mechanism: Inhibit the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) on the luminal membrane of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, blocking sodium, potassium, and chloride reabsorption. This abolishes the countercurrent multiplier mechanism, preventing the generation of a hypertonic medullary interstitium and impairing both urine concentration and dilution. Loop diuretics are the most potent diuretic class, producing a large volume of isotonic urine (fractional sodium excretion up to 20-25%). [3,14]
  - *Key indications:* Acute pulmonary edema, volume overload in heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, hypercalcemia, and hypertension (when other agents are inadequate or renal function is impaired, as they remain effective at low GFR unlike thiazides). [5,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, ototoxicity (dose-related, reversible; risk increases with rapid IV bolus, renal impairment, and concomitant aminoglycosides), hypocalcemia, and volume depletion (pre-renal azotemia). [3,14]
- **Thiazide Diuretics:** Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone, Indapamide. Mechanism: Inhibit the Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) on the luminal membrane of the distal convoluted tubule, reducing sodium and chloride reabsorption. Thiazides are less potent than loop diuretics (fractional sodium excretion 5-10%) but are more effective antihypertensives due to their additional effect of reducing peripheral vascular resistance (mechanism unclear, possibly through calcium-activated potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle). [3,14]
  - *Key side effects:* Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hyponatremia (more common than with loop diuretics, especially in elderly women), hypercalcemia (thiazides increase calcium reabsorption in the distal tubule—the opposite of loop diuretics), hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia. Chlorthalidone is more potent and has a longer half-life than hydrochlorothiazide and is preferred for hypertension based on outcome data (ALLHAT trial). [3,14]
- **Potassium-Sparing Diuretics:** See MRAs above. Amiloride and triamterene are ENaC blockers in the collecting duct (not mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists). They are weak diuretics used primarily for their potassium-sparing effect in combination with thiazides or loop diuretics. [3,14]

### 5. Vasodilators

- **Nitrates:** Nitroglycerin (sublingual, IV), Isosorbide mononitrate and dinitrate (oral). Mechanism: Nitrates are converted to nitric oxide (NO) in vascular smooth muscle, activating guanylyl cyclase and increasing cGMP, which relaxes smooth muscle. Venodilation predominates at low doses (reduced preload), and arteriolar dilation occurs at higher doses (reduced afterload). Coronary vasodilation increases myocardial oxygen supply. [3,14]
  - *Key indications:* Acute angina (sublingual nitroglycerin 0.4 mg every 5 minutes, up to 3 doses), acute heart failure/pulmonary edema (IV nitroglycerin), chronic stable angina (long-acting oral nitrates). [5]
  - *Key side effects:* Headache (severe, throbbing, from meningeal artery dilation—diminishes with continued use), hypotension, reflex tachycardia, flushing. Tolerance develops rapidly with continuous use (requires a nitrate-free interval of 10-12 hours daily). Absolute contraindication with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) due to profound, potentially fatal hypotension. [3,14]
- **Hydralazine:** Direct arteriolar vasodilator (mechanism unclear: inhibits IP3-induced calcium release from sarcoplasmic reticulum in vascular smooth muscle). Used in combination with isosorbide dinitrate in heart failure (particularly in African Americans, in whom the fixed-dose combination hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate [BiDil] showed a mortality benefit in the A-HeFT trial). Also used in hypertension in pregnancy. Side effects: reflex tachycardia (mitigated by co-administration with beta-blocker), headache, flushing, and drug-induced lupus syndrome (dose-dependent, more common in slow acetylators; ANA positive in >90%, clinical lupus in 5-10%; resolves upon discontinuation). [3,14]

### 6. Antiarrhythmic Drugs (Vaughan-Williams Classification)

- **Class I (Sodium Channel Blockers):** Flecainide, Propafenone (Class IC; no effect on repolarization; used for atrial fibrillation in patients without structural heart disease), Lidocaine (Class IB; accelerates repolarization; used for ventricular arrhythmias), Procainamide (Class IA; prolongs repolarization; used for atrial and ventricular arrhythmias). [3,14]
- **Class II (Beta-Blockers):** See above. Reduce sympathetic tone, slow AV node conduction, control ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation, suppress ventricular ectopy. [3,14]
- **Class III (Potassium Channel Blockers):** Amiodarone, Dronedarone, Sotalol, Dofetilide. Mechanism: Prolong the action potential duration and effective refractory period by blocking potassium channels (IKr, IKs), prolonging repolarization (QT prolongation). Amiodarone also has Class I, II, and IV effects. Amiodarone is the most potent antiarrhythmic but has significant extracardiac toxicity: pulmonary fibrosis (1-5%, potentially fatal, dose-dependent—cumulative dose >400 g), thyroid dysfunction (both hypo- and hyperthyroidism due to its high iodine content—37% iodine by weight; each 200 mg tablet releases 6 mg of free iodine, 40 times the daily requirement), corneal deposits (nearly universal, rarely affects vision), photosensitivity, slate-gray skin discoloration, hepatotoxicity, and peripheral neuropathy. It has a very long half-life (25-110 days), so these toxicities persist long after discontinuation. Amiodarone does not increase mortality in heart failure or post-MI (unlike many Class I agents), making it the preferred antiarrhythmic in patients with structural heart disease. Dronedarone is a structural analog of amiodarone without the iodine moiety, designed to reduce toxicity, but is less effective and is contraindicated in HFrEF and permanent atrial fibrillation due to increased mortality. [3,14]
- **Class IV (Calcium Channel Blockers – Non-Dihydropyridines):** Verapamil, Diltiazem. See above. Slow AV node conduction. [3,14]

### 7. Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Agents

- **Warfarin:** Vitamin K antagonist. Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), depleting reduced vitamin K, which is a necessary cofactor for the gamma-carboxylation of clotting factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX, and X, and the anticoagulant proteins C and S. The carboxylated factors cannot bind calcium and phospholipid surfaces, and are inactive. Monitored by INR (target 2.0-3.0 for most indications, 2.5-3.5 for mechanical mitral valve). [3,14]
- **Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs):** Dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor), Rivaroxaban, Apixaban, Edoxaban (direct Factor Xa inhibitors). Advantages over warfarin: fixed dosing, no routine monitoring, fewer food and drug interactions, rapid onset and offset. At least as effective as warfarin for stroke prevention in non-valvular atrial fibrillation, with lower rates of intracranial hemorrhage. Not approved for mechanical heart valves or moderate-severe mitral stenosis. [3,14]

### Antiplatelet agents:

  - *Aspirin:* Irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) in platelets, inhibiting thromboxane A2 synthesis, a potent platelet aggregator and vasoconstrictor. Platelets cannot synthesize new COX-1, so the effect lasts the life of the platelet (7-10 days). [3,14]
  - *P2Y12 receptor inhibitors:* Clopidogrel (prodrug, requires CYP2C19 for activation; genetic polymorphisms cause reduced efficacy in 2-14% of patients), Prasugrel (prodrug, more rapid and consistent activation, more potent, contraindicated in patients with history of stroke/TIA due to increased bleeding risk), Ticagrelor (direct, reversible P2Y12 antagonist, not a prodrug, twice-daily dosing, associated with dyspnea from adenosine reuptake inhibition). Mechanism: Block the P2Y12 ADP receptor on platelets, inhibiting ADP-mediated platelet activation and aggregation, and preventing the conformational change of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor. [3,14]
  - *Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors:* Abciximab, Eptifibatide, Tirofiban. Block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation (fibrinogen cross-linking of platelets). Used in high-risk PCI. [3,14]

### 8. Digoxin (Cardiac Glycoside)

- **MOA:** Inhibits the Na-K-ATPase pump on the cardiomyocyte membrane. Increased intracellular sodium reduces the activity of the Na-Ca exchanger (NCX), which normally extrudes one calcium ion for three sodium ions. The resulting increase in intracellular calcium increases cardiac contractility (positive inotrope—unique mechanism that does not increase heart rate or myocardial oxygen consumption). At therapeutic concentrations, digoxin also increases vagal (parasympathetic) tone, slowing the sinus rate and AV nodal conduction (negative chronotrope and dromotrope), which is useful for rate control in atrial fibrillation. [3,14]
- **Indications:** Rate control in atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (second-line, particularly useful in patients with concomitant heart failure and hypotension who cannot tolerate beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers), and symptomatic chronic HFrEF despite optimal therapy with ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, and MRA (reduces heart failure hospitalizations, but not overall mortality, in the DIG trial). [5,14]
- **Key side effects:** Narrow therapeutic index (therapeutic serum concentration 0.5-0.9 ng/mL; toxicity >1.2-2.0 ng/mL). Toxicity: cardiac arrhythmias (the hallmark is paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with 2:1 AV block, but also premature ventricular contractions, bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, bradycardia, and AV block of any degree), neurological symptoms (yellow-green visual halos [xanthopsia], scotomas, blurred vision, confusion, delirium), and gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, anorexia, diarrhea). Factors predisposing to toxicity: hypokalemia (digoxin competes with potassium for the Na-K-ATPase; low potassium increases digoxin binding), hypercalcemia, hypomagnesemia, renal impairment (digoxin is renally excreted), and interacting drugs (amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine increase digoxin levels). Treatment of digoxin toxicity: discontinue the drug, correct electrolytes, and administer digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Digibind or DigiFab) for life-threatening arrhythmias, hemodynamic instability, or hyperkalemia >5.0 mmol/L. [3,14]
